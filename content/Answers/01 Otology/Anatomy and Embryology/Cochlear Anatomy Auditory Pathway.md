> - ## Part A: Microstructure of Cochlea (Q23, Q62)
> - that makes **2.5 turns** — designated as basal, middle, and apical — around a central bony pillar called the **modiolus**. The modiolus serves as the axial skeleton of the cochlea and contains both the spiral ganglion (housing the cell bodies of cochlear nerve afferent neurons) and the blood vessels that supply the cochlear structures. The total length of the cochlear canal is approximately **35 mm** in humans. Architecturally, the cochlea tapers progressively from its widest point at the base to its narrowest at the apex.
> - occupies the upper position and contains perilymph; at its basal end it opens into the vestibule at the oval window, where the stapes footplate transmits vibrations into the cochlear fluid system. The **scala media**, also known as the cochlear duct, occupies the middle position and is the only compartment filled with endolymph; it is a closed compartment, essentially a blind sac of the membranous labyrinth. The **scala tympani** lies in the lower position, also contains perilymph, and terminates at its basal end at the round window membrane, which acts as a pressure relief valve allowing fluid displacement within the rigid bony cochlea.
> - (perilymphatic duct). The scala media communicates indirectly with the endolymphatic sac through the **ductus reuniens** (which connects to the saccule) and thence via the **endolymphatic duct** that traverses the vestibular aqueduct.
> - defined by three distinct boundaries, each with specialized histological and functional properties.
> - is formed by **Reissner's membrane** (vestibular membrane), a delicate structure only two cell layers thick. This membrane separates the endolymph of the scala media from the perilymph of the scala vestibuli and permits selective ion exchange between the two compartments.
> - is composed of the **stria vascularis** overlying the **spiral ligament**. The stria vascularis holds the distinction of being the only vascular epithelium in the body, containing intraepithelial capillaries within its substance. It is the primary generator of the endocochlear potential (+80 mV) and actively secretes potassium ions into the endolymph, maintaining the unique ionic composition essential for mechanotransduction.
> - is the **basilar membrane**, upon which the organ of Corti sits. The basilar membrane is responsible for frequency tuning through its graded mechanical properties — it is narrow, thick, and stiff at the base (responding to high frequencies) and wide, thin, and flexible at the apex (responding to low frequencies). This gradient forms the structural basis of tonotopic organization.
> - is the sensory neuroepithelium of hearing, situated on the basilar membrane within the scala media. It is the biological transducer that converts mechanical vibrations of the cochlear partition into electrical signals transmitted to the brain via the cochlear nerve. The organ of Corti is an exquisitely organized structure containing sensory hair cells, an array of specialized supporting cells, and a covering extracellular membrane, all arranged in a precise architecture that extends the full length of the cochlear duct.
> - are arranged in a **single row** along the inner aspect of the organ of Corti, numbering approximately **3,500 cells** in the human cochlea. They are flask-shaped (pyriform) cells and serve as the **primary sensory transducers** responsible for hearing. Each inner hair cell receives dense afferent innervation, with approximately **95% of all afferent (Type I) cochlear nerve fibres** terminating on inner hair cells. Their stereocilia are arranged in a shallow U-shaped or roughly linear pattern on the apical surface. Functionally, the inner hair cells are the true sensory receptors: it is the depolarization of inner hair cells and the consequent release of glutamate at their ribbon synapses that generates the neural code of hearing transmitted to the brain.
> - are arranged in **three rows** (occasionally four in the apical turn), numbering approximately **12,000 cells**. They are cylindrical in shape and are distinguished by the presence of the motor protein **prestin** in their lateral cell membrane. Prestin endows outer hair cells with the remarkable property of **electromotility** — the ability to change length in response to changes in membrane potential. Through this mechanism, the outer hair cells function as the **cochlear amplifier**, actively enhancing the sensitivity and frequency selectivity of the basilar membrane response by **40–60 dB**. Without functional outer hair cells, hearing thresholds would be elevated by this magnitude and frequency discrimination would be markedly impaired. The stereocilia of outer hair cells are arranged in a characteristic W or V pattern.
> - is a gelatinous extracellular matrix that overlies the organ of Corti like a canopy. It is composed of collagen fibres and glycoproteins and is attached medially to the spiral limbus. The **tallest stereocilia of the outer hair cells are embedded** directly into the undersurface of the tectorial membrane, creating a direct mechanical coupling. In contrast, the stereocilia of the inner hair cells are **not directly attached** to the tectorial membrane but are instead deflected by fluid movement in the narrow subtectorial space during basilar membrane vibration. This difference in coupling is fundamental to the distinct roles of the two hair cell populations.
> - are rigid, elongated cells that contain dense tonofilament bundles, giving them structural stiffness. Together they form the walls of the **tunnel of Corti**, a triangular extracellular space that runs the length of the organ of Corti. The tunnel of Corti is filled with **cortilymph**, a fluid whose composition resembles perilymph (high Na+, low K+) rather than endolymph, despite the tunnel being situated within the organ of Corti. The inner pillar cells form the inner wall and the outer pillar cells form the outer wall of this tunnel.
> - (outer phalangeal cells) are the primary supporting cells for the outer hair cells. Each Deiters' cell has a cup-shaped base that cradles the base of an outer hair cell, while its phalangeal process extends upward to the surface of the organ of Corti. These phalangeal processes interdigitate with the apical surfaces of the outer hair cells to form the **reticular lamina**, a rigid mosaic of cell apices that creates a tight seal separating the endolymphatic space above from the cortilymph space below. This seal is critical: it maintains the electrochemical gradient necessary for mechanotransduction.
> - are tall columnar cells located lateral to the outermost row of outer hair cells, providing both structural support and a barrier function at the lateral margin of the organ of Corti. **Claudius' cells** lie further lateral on the basilar membrane as cuboidal epithelial cells continuous with the outer sulcus. **Boettcher's cells** are present exclusively in the **basal turn** of the cochlea, located between Claudius' cells and the basilar membrane. On the medial side, **inner sulcus cells** are cuboidal epithelial cells lining the inner sulcus medial to the inner hair cells, while **border cells** (inner phalangeal cells) lie immediately adjacent to the inner hair cells and provide them with structural support.
> - on their apical surfaces — these are modified microvilli containing a core of cross-linked actin filaments, arranged in graded rows of increasing height. Adjacent stereocilia are connected by fine extracellular filaments called **tip links**, composed of **cadherin 23** (upper attachment) and **protocadherin 15** (lower attachment). Deflection of the stereocilia bundle toward the tallest row places tension on the tip links, which directly opens **mechanically gated ion channels** (MET channels) located at or near the lower insertion of each tip link. Potassium ions from the endolymph then flow into the hair cell down the enormous electrochemical gradient created by the endocochlear potential (+80 mV) and the negative resting potential of the hair cell (-45 to -70 mV), yielding a total driving force of approximately **150 mV**. This K+ influx depolarizes the hair cell, which in turn opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels at the basolateral membrane. Calcium entry triggers the release of the neurotransmitter **glutamate** at specialized **ribbon synapses** at the base of the hair cell, generating excitatory postsynaptic potentials in the afferent nerve fibres.
> - (of Corti) is located within the modiolus and contains the cell bodies of the cochlear nerve afferent neurons. These are **bipolar neurons** whose peripheral processes extend to the hair cells and whose central processes coalesce to form the cochlear nerve.
> - | 95% | Myelinated, large diameter | Inner hair cells | Convergent: each IHC innervated by ~10 Type I neurons; each neuron innervates only 1 IHC |
> - | 5% | Unmyelinated, small diameter | Outer hair cells | Divergent: each Type II neuron innervates ~10 OHCs; function likely nociceptive/damage detection |
> - provides a high degree of redundancy and allows for a wide dynamic range of intensity coding, since individual fibres contacting the same inner hair cell have different thresholds and spontaneous rates. In contrast, the Type II afferent fibres that innervate outer hair cells are thin, unmyelinated, and sparsely distributed, with each Type II neuron branching to contact approximately 10 outer hair cells. Their function is not fully established but likely relates to nociceptive signalling and the detection of acoustic damage.
> - originates predominantly from the contralateral medial superior olivary complex and projects directly to the **cell bodies of the outer hair cells**. Its principal neurotransmitter is **acetylcholine**, acting on nicotinic receptors. Activation of the MOC pathway modulates outer hair cell electromotility, effectively reducing cochlear amplifier gain. This system provides protection against noise-induced damage and improves the detection of signals in background noise.
> - originates predominantly from the ipsilateral lateral superior olivary complex and terminates not on hair cells directly but on the **afferent dendrites beneath the inner hair cells**. It employs multiple neurotransmitters including **dopamine, GABA, acetylcholine, and enkephalins**. The LOC system modulates afferent firing rates and is thought to protect against glutamate excitotoxicity at the IHC–afferent synapse.
> - wide), relatively thick, and stiff, properties that cause it to resonate at **high frequencies** (up to 20,000 Hz). At the **apex**, the membrane widens to approximately **0.5 mm**, becomes thinner and more flexible, and resonates at **low frequencies** (down to 20 Hz).
> - (place theory of hearing): each specific location along the basilar membrane has a characteristic frequency to which it responds maximally. The traveling wave initiated by stapes vibration at the oval window propagates from base to apex along the basilar membrane. At a location determined by the frequency of the stimulus, the traveling wave reaches its point of maximum displacement and then rapidly decays. The location of this maximum determines the perceived pitch of the sound. This tonotopic organization — the spatial mapping of frequency along the cochlear partition — is faithfully maintained and refined at every relay station of the ascending auditory pathway, from the cochlear nuclei through to the primary auditory cortex.
> - (which gives rise to the spiral modiolar artery supplying most of the cochlea, and the vestibulocochlear artery) and the **anterior vestibular artery**. Critically, the cochlea is an **end-organ** with no collateral circulation, making it highly vulnerable to ischemic insults. Venous drainage follows the spiral modiolar vein, which drains into the vein of the cochlear aqueduct, ultimately emptying into the inferior petrosal sinus.

> - via the cochlear aqueduct (perilymphatic duct), which connects the scala tympani to the subarachnoid space of the posterior cranial fossa. In the **scala vestibuli**, perilymph is derived primarily from **blood plasma filtrate** through ultrafiltration across the blood-labyrinth barrier. Importantly, perilymph is not simply filtered CSF — its composition differs slightly from CSF, with higher protein content in the scala tympani. The cochlear aqueduct may serve more as a pressure equalization route than as a major fluid conduit.
> - is the primary source of endolymph potassium and the generator of the endocochlear potential. It is the only epithelium in the body that contains its own blood supply in the form of intraepithelial capillaries. The stria vascularis is composed of three distinct cell layers, each with specialized roles in potassium transport.
> - are the epithelial cells facing the endolymphatic space (luminal surface). They are the cells that ultimately secrete K+ into endolymph through **KCNQ1/KCNE1 channels** on their apical membrane. On their basolateral surface, they actively accumulate K+ via the **Na+/K+ ATPase** pump and the **NKCC1 (Na-K-2Cl) cotransporter**.
> - occupy the middle layer and are melanocyte-derived cells containing melanin granules. They are critical for generating the endocochlear potential through their **Kir4.1 (KCNJ10) potassium channels**, which maintain the intrastrial potential — the voltage difference within the stria that drives K+ secretion.
> - form the deepest layer, facing the spiral ligament. They are connected by **tight junctions** that create a barrier between the intrastrial space and the spiral ligament, and they communicate with fibrocytes of the spiral ligament via **gap junctions**.
> - (formed by connexin 26 and connexin 30). From the basal cells, K+ passes into the **intrastrial space**. Intermediate cells take up K+ and release it via **Kir4.1 channels**, generating the intrastrial potential. Marginal cells then take up K+ from the intrastrial space via their **basolateral Na+/K+ ATPase** and **NKCC1 cotransporter**. Finally, K+ is secreted into the endolymph through **apical KCNQ1/KCNE1 channels** on the marginal cells.
> - — formed by **connexin 26 (GJB2)** and **connexin 30 (GJB6)** — that connect the supporting cells into a functional syncytium. Through this gap junction network, K+ is transported laterally to reach the **fibrocytes of the spiral ligament** (Types I, II, IV, and V), which actively take up K+ via Na+/K+ ATPase and NKCC1 transporters. From the spiral ligament fibrocytes, K+ passes through gap junctions to the **basal cells of the stria vascularis**, and is then recycled through the intermediate and marginal cells of the stria to be **re-secreted into endolymph** via the KCNQ1/KCNE1 channels, completing the cycle.
> - is located in the posterior cranial fossa within a dural fold on the posterior surface of the petrous temporal bone. It is connected to the saccule and utricle via the endolymphatic duct, which passes through the vestibular aqueduct. The endolymphatic sac serves multiple functions essential for inner ear homeostasis: it is the **primary site of endolymph volume regulation** (endolymph absorption), it maintains **endolymph pressure homeostasis**, it contains immunocompetent cells providing **inner ear immune defence**, and it performs **waste removal** through phagocytic activity and debris clearance.
> - in the scala media relative to perilymph. It is generated primarily by the stria vascularis, specifically through the activity of Kir4.1 channels in the intermediate cells. The endocochlear potential is essential for hair cell mechanotransduction because it contributes to the total electrochemical driving force for K+ entry into hair cells. With the hair cell resting potential at approximately -45 to -70 mV and the endocochlear potential at +80 mV, the total driving force across the apical membrane of the hair cell reaches approximately **150 mV** — an enormous gradient that ensures rapid K+ influx when MET channels open.
> - | Most common cause of genetic SNHL (autosomal recessive); disrupts K+ recycling through gap junctions | Non-syndromic SNHL; loss of EP; hair cell degeneration |
> - | Disrupts gap junction K+ recycling (similar to GJB2) | Non-syndromic SNHL |
> - | Pendrin dysfunction impairs endolymph Cl-/HCO3- exchange and pH regulation; associated with EVA | Fluctuating/progressive SNHL + EVA + goiter |
> - | Mutations in KCNQ1 or KCNE1 (stria vascularis and cardiac myocytes) | Congenital profound SNHL + long QT syndrome |
> - | Kir4.1 channel dysfunction in intermediate cells | EAST/SeSAME syndrome (epilepsy, ataxia, SNHL, tubulopathy) |
> - | Endolymphatic hydrops from disturbed endolymph absorption | Episodic vertigo, fluctuating SNHL, tinnitus, aural fullness |
> - | Enter hair cells through MET channels; damage hair cells and stria | Irreversible SNHL (OHCs more susceptible; high-frequency first) |
> - | Damages OHCs, stria vascularis, and spiral ganglion neurons | Bilateral high-frequency SNHL |
> - | Strial atrophy reduces EP; sensory hair cell loss | Progressive bilateral SNHL |
> - ## Part C: Auditory Pathway (Q62)
> - achieved through fibre crossing at multiple levels (ensuring that unilateral lesions above the cochlear nuclei rarely cause significant hearing loss), **tonotopic organization** maintained faithfully at every relay station from cochlea to cortex, and **multiple relay stations** that allow progressively more complex processing of auditory information at each successive level.
> - in company with the facial nerve (CN VII), the superior and inferior vestibular nerves, and the labyrinthine artery.
> - at the pontomedullary junction, in the region of the cerebellopontine angle. This is an **obligatory synapse** — every ascending fibre must relay here, making the cochlear nuclei a critical bottleneck in the auditory pathway. The cochlear nuclei are subdivided into the **dorsal cochlear nucleus (DCN)**, the **anteroventral cochlear nucleus (AVCN)**, and the **posteroventral cochlear nucleus (PVCN)**. Tonotopic organization is maintained within each subdivision, and the different subnuclei process different aspects of the auditory signal — the AVCN is particularly important for preserving temporal information, while the DCN contributes to spectral processing and sound localization in the vertical plane.
> - in the pons. The SOC is of particular importance because it is the **first point in the auditory pathway where input from both ears converges** — that is, the first site of binaural integration. This binaural input makes the SOC critical for **sound localization**. The lateral superior olive (LSO) detects **interaural level differences (ILD)**, which are the primary cue for localizing high-frequency sounds (above approximately 1,500 Hz), since short wavelengths produce a significant head shadow effect causing intensity differences between the two ears. The medial superior olive (MSO) detects **interaural time differences (ITD)**, the primary cue for localizing low-frequency sounds (below approximately 1,500 Hz), where phase differences between the two ears can be reliably detected. The medial nucleus of the trapezoid body (MNTB) provides inhibitory (glycinergic) input to the LSO from the contralateral ear, assisting in the computation of interaural level differences.
> - in the midbrain tectum. The inferior colliculus is an **obligatory relay station** for the vast majority of ascending auditory fibres — nearly all pathways converge here, making it the principal integration centre of the auditory midbrain. The inferior colliculus participates in multimodal processing and is a key component of the acoustic startle reflex pathway.
> - of the thalamus, the final subcortical relay station. The MGB has three divisions: the **ventral division** maintains strict tonotopic organization and serves as the specific auditory relay to cortex; the **dorsal division** is involved in multisensory processing; and the **medial division** contains broadly tuned neurons involved in auditory attention and arousal.
> - (transverse temporal gyrus) on the superior temporal gyrus, corresponding to **Brodmann areas 41 and 42**. The primary auditory cortex contains a tonotopic map, with low frequencies represented laterally and high frequencies medially. Further processing occurs in the auditory association areas, including **Wernicke's area** (Brodmann area 22) on the dominant hemisphere, which is essential for language comprehension.
> - (ventral acoustic stria) | Major decussation — fibres from VCN cross to contralateral SOC |
> - projections from the auditory cortex to the medial geniculate body allow cortical feedback modulation of thalamic relay. **Corticocollicular** projections from the auditory cortex to the inferior colliculus modulate midbrain processing. The **olivocochlear bundle (OCB)**, running from the superior olivary complex to the cochlea, provides the final efferent link — the MOC bundle to the outer hair cells and the LOC bundle to the afferent dendrites beneath the inner hair cells. The descending pathway as a whole serves functions including selective auditory attention, suppression of background noise, protection from acoustic trauma, and improvement of signal-to-noise ratio.
> - — inability to process auditory information despite intact peripheral hearing |

These present with conductive hearing loss in the setting of a normal tympanic membrane and absent stapedial reflexes, and may closely mimic [[Otosclerosis|otosclerosis]] clinically.

> - is an epidermoid cyst (keratinizing squamous epithelium) arising behind an intact tympanic membrane from epithelial rests (epidermoid formation). > - is an aberrant artery arising from the petrous internal carotid artery that passes through the obturator foramen of the stapes to supply the territory normally served by the middle meningeal artery. The stapedial artery normally regresses by the 10th week of fetal life. It presents with pulsatile tinnitus and conductive hearing loss due to stapes fixation; on otoscopy, a vessel may be visible crossing the promontory and passing through the stapes crura. CT demonstrates an absent ipsilateral foramen spinosum.
> - occurs when the ICA takes an anomalous lateral course traversing the middle ear cavity. It presents as a pulsatile retrotympanic mass with pulsatile tinnitus and conductive hearing loss. > - describes a jugular bulb that protrudes superiorly into the hypotympanum or middle ear cavity, potentially lacking bony covering (dehiscent). > - refers to the absence of bony covering of the facial nerve canal, most commonly over the **oval window** in the tympanic segment, though it may also occur at the geniculate ganglion. > - results from failure of canalization of the first pharyngeal cleft, producing bony or membranous atresia of the external auditory canal. > - ## Part E: Facial Recess (Posterior Tympanotomy) (Q62)
> - is a triangular depression in the posterior wall of the middle ear (posterior tympanum), defined by the relationship between two nerves and a bony landmark. It is bounded **superiorly** by the **fossa incudis** (the recess containing the short process of the incus, also called the incudal buttress), **laterally** by the **chorda tympani nerve** (a branch of CN VII that exits from the vertical/mastoid segment of the facial nerve), and **medially** by the **facial nerve** itself in its mastoid (vertical/descending) segment within its bony canal. The facial recess provides a direct **surgical corridor** from the mastoid to the middle ear without traversing the external auditory canal.
> - (facial recess approach) involves opening the facial recess from the mastoid side to gain access to the middle ear. It is a fundamental approach in modern otologic surgery with several important indications. The most common current indication is **cochlear implant electrode insertion**, where the electrode is passed through the facial recess to reach the round window or a cochleostomy site. Other indications include **cholesteatoma surgery** (assessment and removal of disease in the middle ear during canal wall up mastoidectomy), **second-look procedures** (inspection of the middle ear after prior cholesteatoma surgery), **[[Ossiculoplasty CSF Otorrhea Cochleostomy|ossiculoplasty]]** (access to the ossicular chain for reconstruction), **round window drug delivery** (access for intratympanic therapy devices), and **removal of middle ear pathology** such as glomus tympanicum or congenital cholesteatoma.
> - (continuous EMG) throughout the procedure to prevent inadvertent injury. The facial nerve must be positively identified before any drilling. Thin bone over the facial nerve should be preserved rather than skeletonized unless absolutely necessary. The **chorda tympani may be sacrificed** if necessary for adequate access, resulting in **taste disturbance** (loss of taste to the ipsilateral anterior two-thirds of the tongue) and reduced salivary secretion from the ipsilateral submandibular and sublingual glands. In cases with anatomic variants such as an anteriorly placed facial nerve or an unusually narrow facial recess, an alternative approach may be required.
> - is an important posterior tympanic recess that lies **medial** to the facial nerve, between the facial nerve laterally and the promontory/round window niche medially. Unlike the facial recess, the sinus tympani is **not accessible through the posterior tympanotomy** because it lies medial to the facial nerve. It is a common site for residual cholesteatoma due to its hidden location and is better visualized with angled endoscopes (30-degree or 70-degree). It is distinguished from the facial recess by its position medial (rather than lateral) to the facial nerve.
