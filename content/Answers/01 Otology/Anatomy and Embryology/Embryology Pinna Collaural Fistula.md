## Part A: Embryological Development of the Pinna (Q38)

### Timeline of Pinna Development

The development of the pinna begins during the fifth to sixth week of gestation, when six mesenchymal proliferations known as the **hillocks of His** appear around the margins of the first pharyngeal groove (cleft). Hillocks 1 through 3 are derived from the **first pharyngeal arch** (mandibular arch), while hillocks 4 through 6 arise from the **second pharyngeal arch** (hyoid arch). These hillocks undergo progressive growth and fusion so that by approximately 60 days (8-9 weeks), a rudimentary pinna is formed.

By the **12th week** of gestation, the pinna achieves a recognizable adult configuration. Growth continues, and by the **20th week** the pinna is positioned in its definitive location, though it continues to enlarge. At birth, the pinna is not fully developed — the cartilage remains soft and pliable. The pinna reaches approximately 85-90% of its final adult dimensions by around **9 years** of age. In the elderly, elongation of the lobule continues throughout life due to loss of elastic tissue and gravitational effects.

> **Key Point:** The auricle initially develops in the upper cervical region and migrates cranially and posteriorly to its definitive position as the mandible grows. Failure of this migration results in low-set ears (seen in several syndromic conditions).

### Hillocks of His and Their Derivatives

The six hillocks give rise to specific anatomical structures of the auricle. This classification remains the most widely accepted standard for examination purposes.

| Hillock Number | Pharyngeal Arch | Anatomical Structure Formed |
|:--------------:|:---------------:|:---------------------------|
| **1** | First (mandibular) | **Tragus** |
| **2** | First (mandibular) | **Crus of helix** (helical root) |
| **3** | First (mandibular) | **Helix** (ascending part) |
| **4** | Second (hyoid) | **Antihelix** (superior portion) |
| **5** | Second (hyoid) | **Antitragus** |
| **6** | Second (hyoid) | **Helix** (lower part / descending helix) and **lobule** |

> **Important Note:** The **majority of the auricle** is derived from the **second pharyngeal arch** (hyoid arch). The **tragus** is the principal first arch contribution to the pinna. This has clinical significance because first arch syndromes (e.g., Treacher Collins) may spare major portions of the pinna while second arch disorders may cause more extensive auricular malformations.

> **Controversy:** There is debate about the precise contribution of each hillock. Some authors (Streeter, 1922) proposed slightly different assignments. However, the six-hillock model remains the most widely accepted and is the standard for examination purposes.

### Development of the External Auditory Canal

The external auditory canal (EAC) originates from the first pharyngeal groove (cleft), which deepens medially toward the first pharyngeal pouch. During development, a solid core of ectodermal cells known as the **meatal plug** grows inward from the floor of the groove. This meatal plug undergoes canalization (hollowing out) by the **28th week** of gestation to form the patent EAC. Failure of canalization results in **congenital aural atresia**, which is often associated with microtia and middle ear malformations.

The **tympanic membrane** forms at the medial end of the EAC, at the point where the ectoderm of the first pharyngeal groove meets the endoderm of the first pharyngeal pouch, with intervening mesoderm. Accordingly, the tympanic membrane comprises three embryologically distinct layers:

1. **Outer (lateral) epithelial layer** — derived from the ectoderm of the first pharyngeal groove
2. **Middle fibrous layer** — derived from the mesoderm between the first groove and the first pouch
3. **Inner (medial) mucosal layer** — derived from the endoderm of the first pharyngeal pouch

## Part B: Structures Derived from the First Pharyngeal Arch (Q38)

The first pharyngeal arch (mandibular arch) gives rise to structures innervated by the **trigeminal nerve (CN V)**. Its cartilaginous core, **Meckel's cartilage**, serves as the scaffold for several important skeletal derivatives.

### Comprehensive Table of First Arch Derivatives

| Category | Derivatives |
|----------|-------------|
| **Cartilage (Meckel's cartilage)** | Head of malleus, neck of malleus (except manubrium), body of incus, anterior malleolar ligament, sphenomandibular ligament |
| **Bones** | Mandible (by membranous ossification lateral to Meckel's cartilage), maxilla, zygomatic bone, squamous part of temporal bone, palatine bone, vomer |
| **Muscles of mastication** | Temporalis, masseter, medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid |
| **Other muscles** | Mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor veli palatini, tensor tympani |
| **Nerve supply** | Trigeminal nerve (CN V) — mandibular division (V3) primarily; also maxillary division (V2) for upper jaw derivatives |
| **Blood vessel** | Maxillary artery (terminal part of the first aortic arch artery) |
| **Ear structures** | Tragus, crus of helix, ascending helix (hillocks 1-3); malleus head and neck; body of incus |
| **Middle ear contribution** | Tubotympanic recess (first pharyngeal pouch) — forms middle ear cavity, Eustachian tube, mastoid antrum |
| **Tympanic membrane** | Outer ectodermal layer (from first groove); inner endodermal layer (from first pouch) |

### Comparison: First vs Second Pharyngeal Arch Derivatives

| Feature | First Arch (Mandibular) | Second Arch (Hyoid) |
|---------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Cartilage** | Meckel's cartilage | Reichert's cartilage |
| **Ossicles** | Malleus (head, neck), incus (body) | Stapes superstructure, long process of incus, manubrium of malleus |
| **Ligaments** | Anterior malleolar ligament, sphenomandibular ligament | Stylohyoid ligament |
| **Bony structures** | Mandible, maxilla, zygomatic, squamous temporal | Stapes footplate, styloid process, lesser horn of hyoid, upper body of hyoid |
| **Muscles** | Mastication muscles, mylohyoid, anterior digastric, tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini | Muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior belly of digastric, platysma |
| **Nerve** | Trigeminal (CN V) | Facial nerve (CN VII) |
| **Artery** | Maxillary artery | Stapedial artery (usually regresses), hyoid artery |
| **Pinna contribution** | Hillocks 1-3 (tragus, crus of helix, ascending helix) | Hillocks 4-6 (antihelix, antitragus, descending helix, lobule) |

> **Clinical Correlation:** First arch syndrome results in mandibular hypoplasia, malar hypoplasia, malformed ossicles (malleus/incus), auricular deformities (primarily tragus), and Eustachian tube abnormalities. Second arch syndrome causes [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]], stapes fixation, and auricular anomalies affecting the antihelix and antitragus.

## Part C: Congenital Anomalies of the Pinna (Q38)

Congenital anomalies of the pinna encompass a broad spectrum of malformations, ranging from minor cosmetic variations to severe structural absence. These are usefully categorized by the type of abnormality — size, shape, position, and hillock fusion defects — as well as by their syndromic associations.

### Anomalies of Size

**Microtia** refers to an underdeveloped pinna of varying severity, graded from I to IV (see classification below). Its incidence is approximately 1 in 6,000-12,000 live births, with a male preponderance (M:F = 2.5:1). The right ear is more commonly affected, and the condition is unilateral in 79-93% of cases. **Anotia** represents the most severe form (Grade IV microtia), in which there is complete absence of the pinna — this is the rarest presentation. In contrast, **macrotia** denotes an abnormally large pinna that may occur in isolation or as part of Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome.

#### Grading of Microtia (Marx/Weerda Classification)

| Grade | Description | Associated Features |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Grade I** | Small but all components recognizable; mild reduction in size | EAC usually present; mild middle ear anomalies |
| **Grade II** | Some recognizable structures present; vertical remnant of cartilage and skin | EAC stenosis or atresia common; ossicular malformations |
| **Grade III** | Only a soft tissue remnant (peanut-shaped lobule); no recognizable auricular structures | EAC atresia with middle ear malformation almost always present |
| **Grade IV (Anotia)** | Complete absence of auricular tissue | Severe EAC atresia and middle ear malformation |

### Anomalies of Shape and Configuration

**Prominent ears (bat ears)** are the **most common pinna anomaly requiring surgery**. They are characterized by an increased cephaloauricular angle (>25-30 degrees) or an underdeveloped antihelical fold, and they follow autosomal dominant inheritance. **Lop ear** presents with a folded or drooping superior pole of the helix due to deficient superior helical cartilage, while **cup ear (constricted ear)** has a hooded or cupped appearance of the helix with reduced auricular height and possibly a tight helical rim.

**Cryptotia (pocket ear)** is a distinctive anomaly in which the superior portion of the auricular cartilage is buried under the temporal skin — the cartilaginous framework is present but hidden beneath the overlying skin. **Stahl's ear** features a third crus (an extra cartilaginous fold) extending across the scapha, giving a pointed "Spock ear" appearance. **Question mark ear (Cosman ear)** presents with a cleft between the lobule and the lower helix creating a distinct gap. **Shell ear** shows an absent or deficient antihelix with a deepened concha, and **satyr ear** has a pointed upper pole of the helix.

### Anomalies of Position

**Low-set ears** describe a pinna positioned below a horizontal line drawn from the lateral canthus of the eye. This finding is clinically significant because it is associated with renal anomalies (Potter sequence) and chromosomal abnormalities. **Posteriorly rotated ears** have an abnormal axis of the pinna and are associated with various syndromes.

### Anomalies Related to Hillock Fusion Defects

**Preauricular sinus (pit)** is an ectodermal tract located anterior to the ascending helix, resulting from incomplete fusion of hillocks — particularly hillocks 1 and 2. It follows autosomal dominant inheritance with incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, and it may become recurrently infected. **Preauricular appendages (tags)** represent accessory hillocks — skin-covered cartilaginous nodules distributed along a line from the tragus to the angle of the mouth (the first arch line). These tags may contain a cartilage core. **Collaural fistula** is a first branchial cleft anomaly discussed in detail in Part D below.

### Syndromic Associations with Pinna Anomalies

Several syndromes feature characteristic pinna anomalies as part of their phenotype:

- **Treacher Collins syndrome** (mandibulofacial dysostosis) is a first arch disorder presenting with microtia, EAC atresia, and preauricular tags alongside malar and mandibular hypoplasia, downslanting palpebral fissures, and lower eyelid coloboma. It follows autosomal dominant inheritance (TCOF1 gene).
- **Goldenhar syndrome** (oculoauriculovertebral spectrum / hemifacial microsomia) involves the first and second arches, causing unilateral microtia with preauricular tags and pits. Associated features include epibulbar dermoid, vertebral anomalies, and facial asymmetry; it occurs sporadically.
- **CHARGE syndrome** involves multiple arches and presents with low-set, cup-shaped ears with reduced or absent lobule, along with coloboma, heart defects, atresia choanae, retardation of growth, and genital anomalies. It is caused by CHD7 gene mutation.
- **Down syndrome (Trisomy 21)** features small, round, low-set ears with an overfolded helix, in addition to the characteristic facies, intellectual disability, and cardiac anomalies.
- **Turner syndrome (45,X)** shows low-set, posteriorly rotated ears along with short stature, webbed neck, and lymphedema.
- **DiGeorge syndrome (22q11 deletion)** is a third and fourth arch disorder presenting with low-set, small ears along with cardiac anomalies, absent thymus/parathyroids, and cleft palate.
- **Branchio-oto-renal (BOR) syndrome** involves the first and second arches and features preauricular pits, cup-shaped ears, and microtia. Associated findings include branchial fistulae, renal anomalies, and hearing loss; it is caused by mutations in the EYA1 gene.

## Part D: Collaural Fistula — Embryology (Q80)

### Definition

A **collaural fistula** is a first branchial cleft anomaly — a congenital epithelium-lined tract that extends from the external ear canal region to the skin of the upper neck, pre-auricular, or post-auricular area. The tract characteristically passes **through or near the parotid gland** and has an **intimate relationship with the facial nerve**.

### Embryological Basis

During normal development, the first pharyngeal groove (cleft) deepens to form the external auditory canal. This groove is lined by ectoderm externally and is bounded by the first arch anteriorly and the second arch posteriorly. A collaural fistula arises from the **failure of complete obliteration** of the ventral portion of the first pharyngeal groove during embryogenesis. As a result, an epithelium-lined tract persists, forming either a sinus (with a single opening) or a complete fistula (with two openings — one at the EAC and one at the neck or skin surface).

The tract characteristically descends from the EAC region through or around the parotid gland to the upper neck, frequently passing between branches of the facial nerve. Because the facial nerve (the nerve of the second pharyngeal arch) develops in intimate relationship with the first pharyngeal groove, the fistula tract is invariably closely related to the nerve. This anatomical relationship is of paramount surgical importance.

### Incidence

First branchial cleft anomalies account for **less than 1%** of all branchial anomalies — second cleft anomalies are by far the most common at approximately 90%. There is a female preponderance. These anomalies usually present in childhood or young adulthood with recurrent periauricular or upper neck infections.

### Work and Work Classification (1972)

This is the most widely used classification for first branchial cleft anomalies and distinguishes two types based on embryological composition.

| Feature | Type I | Type II |
|---------|--------|---------|
| **Embryological origin** | Ectodermal only (duplication anomaly of membranous EAC) | Ectodermal + mesodermal (duplication anomaly of membranous EAC AND pinna) |
| **Histology** | Lined by **squamous epithelium only**; no skin appendages | Contains **skin appendages** (hair follicles, sebaceous glands) and **cartilage** |
| **Location** | Pre-auricular or post-auricular region; medial to concha; typically superficial | Opening usually inferior — in pre-auricular area, angle of mandible, or upper neck (anterior triangle) |
| **Tract course** | Parallel to and medial to the EAC; relatively superficial | May form a true fistula from the floor of the EAC to the upper neck; passes through or intimately related to parotid gland |
| **Relationship to facial nerve** | Superficial to the facial nerve in most cases | Passes **superficial to, deep to, or between branches** of the facial nerve |
| **Presentation** | Pre-/post-auricular cyst or sinus; recurrent periauricular abscess | Recurrent upper neck or pre-auricular abscess; discharging sinus; may present as parotid mass |
| **Frequency** | Less common | More common (accounts for ~90% of first cleft anomalies) |

### Olsen Classification (Alternative)

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Cyst** | No external or internal opening; presents as a mass in parotid or periauricular region |
| **Sinus** | Single opening either at skin (external) or EAC (internal) |
| **Fistula** | Complete tract with two openings — one at EAC floor and one at skin of neck |

### Relationship of Tract to Facial Nerve

The tract may relate to the facial nerve in several patterns. It may pass **superficial to the nerve** (lateral to the main trunk and all branches), **deep to the nerve** (medial to the main trunk), or **between branches** — passing between the upper temporofacial and lower cervicofacial divisions, which is the most common pattern in Type II anomalies. In some instances, the tract may be **encased by terminal branches** of the nerve.

> **Critical Point for Surgery:** The facial nerve relationship is the single most important surgical consideration. No attempt at excision should be made without formal identification and dissection of the facial nerve, typically through a parotidectomy approach.

## Part E: Investigation of Collaural Fistula (Q80)

A systematic approach to the investigation of a suspected collaural fistula involves clinical assessment followed by targeted imaging and, where indicated, tissue sampling.

### Clinical Assessment

1. **Detailed clinical history:** The patient typically reports recurrent swelling or abscess formation in the pre-auricular, post-auricular, or upper neck region, with intermittent discharge from an opening near the ear or upper neck. There is often a history of previous incision and drainage procedures. The onset is usually in childhood or young adulthood.
2. **Clinical examination:** An external opening may be visible on a line between the tragus and the hyoid bone (along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid). A dimple, pit, or discharging sinus may be noted near the tragus or in the upper neck. The tract may be palpable as a cord-like structure. Parotid swelling may be present during episodes of infection. Otoscopy may reveal an opening or pit at the osseocartilaginous junction on the floor of the EAC near the tympanic membrane, with or without granulation tissue at the opening.

### Imaging and Laboratory Investigations

3. **HRCT temporal bone:** High-resolution CT identifies bony EAC anatomy, delineates the tract course in relation to bony landmarks, identifies associated EAC or middle ear anomalies, and is essential for surgical planning.
4. **MRI (with gadolinium):** Provides superior soft tissue delineation, defines the relationship of the tract to the parotid gland and facial nerve, differentiates from parotid tumors, and identifies the full extent of the tract including any cystic component.
5. **Fistulography / Sinography:** Water-soluble contrast is injected through the external opening under fluoroscopy or CT guidance. This delineates the entire course of the tract, demonstrates communication with the EAC if the fistula is complete, and helps plan the extent of surgery.
6. **Ultrasound:** May show the tract in relation to the parotid gland and is useful for differentiating from other cystic neck lesions (branchial cyst, thyroglossal cyst, lymphangioma). It serves as a useful initial imaging modality in children.
7. **FNAC (Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology):** If presenting as a mass, FNAC rules out parotid neoplasm. Aspirate from a first cleft cyst shows squamous epithelial cells and keratinous debris.

### Differential Diagnosis

Several conditions must be considered during the diagnostic workup:

- **Parotid tumor ([[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|pleomorphic adenoma]]):** Presents as a solid mass; FNAC is diagnostic; no tract to EAC is present.
- **Second branchial cleft cyst/fistula:** The opening lies along the anterior border of the SCM at the junction of the middle and lower third of the neck; there is no relation to the EAC.
- **Preauricular sinus:** Presents as a pit anterior to the ascending helix; does not extend into the neck or parotid.
- **Parotid abscess:** Has an acute presentation without a chronic tract or EAC communication.
- **Infected sebaceous cyst:** Superficial with no deep tract and no EAC communication.
- **[[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]] of the EAC:** Identified by bone erosion on CT and keratin debris within the EAC.

## Part F: Management of Collaural Fistula (Q80)

### Conservative Management

Management of a collaural fistula depends on the clinical presentation. An **asymptomatic incidental finding** may be managed with observation and patient education regarding the risk of future infection. When **acute infection** occurs, treatment includes systemic antibiotics covering Staphylococcus and Streptococcus, with incision and drainage if an abscess forms; pus should be sent for culture and sensitivity. Before any definitive surgical intervention, infection must be fully resolved, with a minimum interval of **6 weeks** after the last episode of infection.

### Definitive Surgical Management

Surgical excision is the definitive treatment and is indicated for recurrent infections, persistent discharge, or a confirmed fistula/sinus. The surgery should be performed electively after complete resolution of infection (at least 6 weeks after the last infective episode). A **superficial parotidectomy approach** with formal facial nerve identification is mandatory. Intraoperative facial nerve monitoring is strongly recommended. The goal is complete excision of the entire fistula tract from the external opening to its medial termination (EAC or deeper).

#### Surgical Technique — Step-by-Step

1. **Incision:** A **modified Blair incision** (standard parotidectomy incision) is made, extending pre-auricularly below the lobule and into an upper neck crease. An elliptical skin incision is placed around the external opening of the fistula if present in the neck.
2. **Raise skin flaps:** The anterior skin flap is elevated to expose the parotid gland. The great auricular nerve is preserved if possible.
3. **Identify the facial nerve:** The **main trunk of the facial nerve** is identified at the stylomastoid foramen using standard landmarks — the tragal pointer, tympanomastoid suture, and posterior belly of the digastric muscle. Alternatively, peripheral branches may be identified and traced retrograde to the main trunk.
4. **Superficial parotidectomy:** If the tract passes through the parotid gland (especially in Type II anomalies), a superficial parotidectomy is performed, dissecting parotid tissue off the facial nerve branches.
5. **Trace the fistula tract:** The entire tract is followed from the external opening (neck/skin) to its medial termination (EAC floor, concha, or deeper). The tract may pass superficial to, deep to, or between branches of the facial nerve.
6. **Excise the tract completely:** The entire epithelium-lined tract is removed including any cartilaginous component. It may be necessary to excise a portion of the EAC floor cartilage. A cuff of normal tissue should be included around the tract.
7. **Methylene blue injection:** Methylene blue may be injected through the external opening pre-operatively to help delineate the tract intraoperatively.
8. **EAC reconstruction:** If EAC floor cartilage is excised, reconstruction is performed to prevent stenosis.
9. **Closure:** The wound is closed in layers. A suction drain is placed and a pressure dressing is applied.

#### Post-operative Care

Following surgery, a suction drain is maintained for 24-48 hours. A pressure dressing is applied for the first 24 hours, followed by routine wound care. Facial nerve function should be checked immediately post-operatively. Peri-operative antibiotics are administered, with a post-operative course if indicated. Regular follow-up is essential to detect recurrence, and otoscopy should be performed to check EAC healing.

#### Factors Affecting Surgical Outcome

Successful surgical management depends on several factors. **Previous incision and drainage or incomplete excision** causes scarring that makes dissection more difficult and increases the risk of both facial nerve injury and recurrence. **Incomplete excision** carries a high recurrence rate and is the most common reason for surgical failure. **Failure to identify the facial nerve** increases the risk of both nerve injury and incomplete excision. **Acute infection at the time of surgery** increases surgical difficulty and complication rates, and surgery should therefore be deferred until infection has resolved. **Type II anomalies** have more complex anatomy with a deeper course through the parotid, leading to higher surgical difficulty.

> **Recurrence rates:** Without facial nerve identification and formal parotidectomy approach, recurrence rates are reported as high as 20-30%. With proper technique including facial nerve identification, recurrence is reduced to less than 5%.

1. Most common anomaly needing surgery — prominent ears

6. Surgery: Superficial parotidectomy approach with MANDATORY facial nerve identification
7. Modified Blair incision; complete tract excision; nerve monitoring recommended
8. High recurrence without complete excision and facial nerve identification
