## Part A: Endoscopic Middle Ear Anatomy — Overview (Q44)

### The Middle Ear Cleft — Endoscopic Perspective

The middle ear (tympanic cavity) is an air-filled, mucosa-lined space within the temporal bone. The endoscopic transcanal approach provides a wide-angle, magnified view of the middle ear anatomy that surpasses the microscopic view in several areas, particularly the retrotympanum, anterior epitympanum, and hypotympanum.

The tympanic cavity is divided into three regions based on relationship to the tympanic membrane:
- **Epitympanum (attic):** above the superior margin of the TM
- **Mesotympanum:** at the level of the TM (largest portion)
- **Hypotympanum:** below the inferior margin of the TM

### Mesotympanum — Six Walls

#### 1. Lateral Wall (Membranous Wall)

The lateral wall of the mesotympanum is formed primarily by the tympanic membrane (TM), which consists of three layers: squamous epithelium externally, a fibrous layer in the middle, and a mucosal lining internally. Superior to the TM, the scutum (outer attic wall) forms the lateral bony wall of the epitympanum. Its sharp inferior edge is visible endoscopically and is notably the first structure eroded in attic [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]]. The fibrocartilaginous annulus anchors the TM to the tympanic sulcus and is deficient superiorly at the notch of Rivinus, corresponding to the pars flaccida area.

Key endoscopic landmarks on the lateral wall (TM surface) include the umbo (tip of malleus handle), the light reflex (cone of light) in the anteroinferior quadrant, the handle (manubrium) of the malleus, the lateral process of the malleus (most lateral point), the pars flaccida (Shrapnell's membrane) superior to the lateral process, and the anterior and posterior malleolar folds.

#### 2. Medial Wall (Labyrinthine Wall)

The medial wall is the most anatomically complex surface of the mesotympanum. The promontory, a rounded elevation overlying the basal turn of the cochlea, dominates this wall. The tympanic plexus (Jacobson's nerve, a branch of CN IX) runs on the promontory surface.

Posterosuperior to the promontory lies the oval window (fenestra vestibuli), which contains the stapes footplate and measures approximately 3.25 mm x 1.75 mm. Posteroinferior to the promontory and below the subiculum lies the round window niche (fenestra cochleae), protected by an overhanging bony lip (crista fenestrae cochleae) and containing the round window membrane.

The facial nerve canal (tympanic segment) runs horizontally above the oval window and below the lateral semicircular canal prominence. This is the most commonly dehiscent segment, with approximately 25-57% of temporal bones showing some dehiscence. The cochleariform process (processus cochleariformis) is a bony spicule anterior to the oval window that acts as a pulley for the tensor tympani tendon and marks the tympanic genu of the facial nerve.

Two additional bony ridges are critically important. The ponticulus connects the promontory to the pyramidal eminence, separating the oval window area above from the sinus tympani below. The subiculum connects the promontory to the styloid eminence, separating the sinus tympani above from the round window niche below.

#### 3. Anterior Wall (Carotid Wall)

The anterior wall features the protympanum, an anterior extension housing the tympanic orifice of the Eustachian tube (ET). The tensor tympani semicanal lies above the ET, separated by the septum canalis musculotubarii, and the processus cochleariformis marks its termination. Deep to the anterior wall lies the carotid canal containing the internal carotid artery, which may be dehiscent.

#### 4. Posterior Wall (Mastoid Wall)

The posterior wall contains several important structures. The aditus ad antrum is the superior opening connecting the epitympanum to the mastoid antrum. The pyramidal eminence (pyramid) is a conical projection containing the stapedius muscle, with its tendon emerging from the apex to attach to the stapes head. The facial recess is a triangular area bounded by the fossa incudis superiorly, the chorda tympani laterally, and the facial nerve medially. The sinus tympani is a deep recess medial to the facial nerve (detailed in Parts C and D). The fossa incudis is a small depression accommodating the short process of the incus and its posterior ligament.

#### 5. Superior Wall (Tegmental Wall)

The tegmen tympani is a thin bony plate separating the middle ear from the middle cranial fossa dura. It may be dehiscent, serving as a route for intracranial spread of infection and [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]. It is continuous with the tegmen mastoideum posteriorly.

#### 6. Inferior Wall (Jugular Wall)

The hypotympanum has a variable depth of 2-10 mm and overlies the jugular bulb. A high-riding jugular bulb may protrude into the mesotympanum. Air cells may extend into the floor (hypotympanic air cells).

### Epitympanum (Attic) — Endoscopic Anatomy

The epitympanum contains the head of the malleus, the body and short process of the incus, the posterior ligament of the incus (in the fossa incudis), the superior ligament of the malleus, the lateral ligament of the malleus (attaching to the scutum), the anterior ligament of the malleus (to the petrotympanic fissure), and the origin of the chorda tympani.

The epitympanum is bounded superiorly by the tegmen tympani, laterally by the scutum (outer attic wall) and the pars flaccida, and medially by the lateral semicircular canal prominence, the facial nerve (tympanic segment above), and the tegmen. Its anterior extent communicates with the supratubal recess (anterior epitympanic space), the posterior wall opens into the aditus ad antrum, and inferiorly it communicates with the mesotympanum via the tympanic isthmus.

#### Tympanic Isthmus (Isthmus Tympani)

The tympanic isthmus is a critical anatomical bottleneck connecting the epitympanum to the mesotympanum. The anterior tympanic isthmus (anterior isthmus) lies between the tensor tympani tendon anteriorly and the long process of incus/stapes posteriorly, and is the narrower of the two. The posterior tympanic isthmus (posterior isthmus) lies between the long process of incus anteriorly and the posterior wall of the tympanic cavity posteriorly, and is the wider passage. Blockage of the isthmus by mucosal oedema, cholesteatoma matrix, or granulation tissue leads to selective attic dysventilation — a key factor in retraction pocket formation and cholesteatoma pathogenesis.

### Key Landmarks Visible on Transcanal Endoscopy

**With 0-degree endoscope:**
- Entire TM with all four quadrants
- Handle of malleus, umbo, lateral process, anterior and posterior malleolar folds
- Long process of incus (through posterosuperior TM or after tympanotomy flap elevation)
- Incudostapedial (IS) joint
- Stapes superstructure (head, anterior and posterior crura, footplate)
- Promontory with tympanic plexus
- Oval window with stapes footplate
- Round window niche (often partially visible)
- Pyramidal eminence with stapedius tendon
- Chorda tympani nerve (crossing from posterior wall, running lateral to incus long process, medial to malleus neck)
- Tympanic segment of facial nerve (above oval window)
- Cochleariform process

**Additional visualization with 30-degree and 45-degree endoscopes:**
- Sinus tympani (deep recesses medial to the facial nerve)
- Posterior sinus, lateral tympanic sinus
- Complete round window membrane (behind the bony overhang)
- Anterior epitympanum and supratubal recess
- Hypotympanum and jugular bulb dome
- Protympanum and ET orifice
- Anterior crus of stapes (sometimes hidden from 0-degree view)

## Part B: Endoscopic Instruments for Ear Surgery (Q44)

### Endoscopes — Specifications

A fundamental distinction between endoscopic and microscopic ear surgery is that the surgeon holds the endoscope in one hand (non-dominant) and instruments in the other (dominant hand). This one-handed operating technique necessitates specialised instruments designed for ergonomic single-hand use.

Rigid endoscopes used for ear surgery are typically 14 cm in length (shorter than the 18 cm sinus endoscopes, though the latter may be used if unavailable). The most commonly used diameters are 3 mm (standard for adults, offering the best balance of image quality and workspace) and 2.7 mm (a common alternative). A 4 mm endoscope provides the best image quality but requires a wide external auditory canal, while a 1.9 mm endoscope is reserved for children, narrow canals, and viewing through perforations.

| Endoscope Angle | Role |
|---|---|
| **0-degree** (straight) | Primary scope for most procedures; initial examination and forward-viewing |
| **30-degree** | Inspection of sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, hypotympanum, and around corners |
| **45-degree** | Deep recesses of retrotympanum, sinus tympani |
| **70-degree** | Rarely used; very specific deep recesses |

### Camera and Visualization System

The visualization system comprises an HD (1080p) or 4K camera (3-chip cameras provide superior colour reproduction), a xenon or LED light source connected via fibreoptic cable, and a high-definition monitor positioned at eye level directly in front of the surgeon. A video recording system is typically integrated with the camera unit. Anti-fog measures are essential and include anti-fog solution (e.g., FRED — fog reduction elimination device), warm saline irrigation, or rubbing the endoscope tip on mucosa.

### Surgical Instruments — Designed for One-Handed Technique

#### Suction Devices
- **Standard suction tips** (various gauges) for blood and fluid aspiration
- **Curved/angled suction tips** for access to recesses while maintaining endoscope position
- **Suction-irrigation cannula** (double-barrelled) for simultaneous suction and irrigation; maintains a clear field and is critical for bleeding control
- **Suction-dissector** combining suction and elevation function

#### Dissecting and Elevating Instruments
- **Micro-curettes** (angled: 45, 90 degrees) for cholesteatoma matrix removal and bone curettage
- **Sickle knife** for tympanomeatal flap incision
- **Round knife** for mucosal incision and adhesion release
- **Micro-elevators/dissectors** (various angles) for flap elevation and tissue dissection
- **Needle** (straight and curved) for fine dissection and scarring release

#### Cutting and Grasping Instruments
- **Endoscopic micro-scissors** (straight and curved) for tissue cutting and adhesion release
- **Micro-cup forceps** for tissue grasping and biopsy
- **Alligator forceps** for grasping larger tissue pieces and packing
- **Micro-grasping forceps** for delicate tissue handling

#### Drilling and Bone Work
- **Micro-drill with angled handpiece** for bone removal (scutum, canal wall, RW niche overhang); the handpiece must be angled to avoid conflict with the endoscope
- **Micro-curettes (bone)** for limited bone removal without a drill

#### Specialized Instruments
- **Endoscopic needle holder** for suturing in endoscopic [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|myringoplasty]]
- **Endoscope holder/arm** to free both hands (for two-handed technique); used selectively
- **Bipolar cautery (endoscopic)** for haemostasis during endoscopic surgery
- **Laser fibre (KTP/diode)** for selective tissue ablation and stapedotomy

### Advantages of Endoscopic Over Microscopic Approach
- Wide-angle view (field of view up to 140 degrees with angled scopes)
- No need for post-auricular incision in many cases (fully transcanal)
- Direct visualization of sinus tympani, retrotympanum, protympanum, anterior epitympanum
- Reduced bone removal (no canalplasty usually needed)
- Better cosmesis (no external scar)
- Less post-operative pain and faster recovery

### Limitations
- One-handed surgery (unless endoscope holder used)
- Frequent lens soiling with blood — requires cleaning
- Loss of depth perception (2D image, though 3D endoscopes are emerging)
- Thermal effect of light source on inner ear structures
- Learning curve for surgeons trained in microscopic technique

## Part C: Posterior Mesotympanic Spaces — Retrotympanum (Q52, Q74)

### Definition and Boundaries of the Retrotympanum

The retrotympanum is the posterior compartment of the mesotympanum, representing the most complex anatomical region of the middle ear. It is the area most difficult to visualize with the operating microscope but is well-suited for endoscopic exploration.

The retrotympanum is bounded superiorly by the pyramidal eminence, ponticulus, and fossa incudis. Inferiorly, it extends to the subiculum, styloid eminence, and jugular wall. Its medial boundary is formed by the promontory and labyrinthine bone (posterior semicircular canal and cochlea), while laterally it is limited by the facial nerve (mastoid/vertical segment), tympanic membrane, and annulus. Anteriorly the retrotympanum merges with the promontory and medial wall of the mesotympanum, and posteriorly it is bounded by the mastoid bone and facial nerve canal.

### Classification of Retrotympanic Spaces (Marchioni Classification)

The retrotympanum is divided into distinct spaces by bony ridges (ponticulus and subiculum) and the facial nerve canal. These spaces are named and classified according to the Marchioni system, which has become the standard endoscopic anatomical framework.

### Detailed Description of Each Space

#### 1. Sinus Tympani (ST) — The Key Retrotympanic Space

The sinus tympani is the most clinically important retrotympanic recess. It is bounded superiorly by the ponticulus and inferiorly by the subiculum. Laterally, it is defined by the facial nerve (mastoid/vertical segment), while medially it extends toward the labyrinthine bone overlying the posterior semicircular canal. Anteriorly it is limited by the promontory and posteriorly by the mastoid bone.

The sinus tympani is the most common hidden recess harbouring residual or recurrent cholesteatoma. It cannot be visualized with the operating microscope via posterior tympanotomy because the facial nerve blocks the view. The endoscopic approach (30-degree or 45-degree scope transcanal) provides direct visualization. Its depth is variable, ranging from 1 to 7 mm, with deeper types being more difficult to access surgically. See Part D for the detailed Marchioni classification.

#### 2. Posterior Sinus (PS)

The posterior sinus lies lateral to the facial nerve — in contrast to the sinus tympani, which lies medial to it. Superiorly it is bounded by the fossa incudis, and inferiorly by the ponticulus (variably). Its medial boundary is formed by the pyramidal eminence and the facial nerve (mastoid segment), while laterally it is limited by the chorda tympani nerve and the tympanic annulus.

The posterior sinus sits between the pyramidal eminence medially and the chorda tympani laterally. It may harbour residual cholesteatoma and is accessible via both the posterior tympanotomy (microscopic) and the transcanal (endoscopic) approaches.

#### 3. Facial Recess (FR) / Posterior Tympanotomy Area

The facial recess is a triangular space bounded superiorly by the fossa incudis and short process of incus, medially by the facial nerve (mastoid segment), and laterally by the chorda tympani nerve. Inferiorly it merges with the posterior sinus.

This space between the chorda tympani (lateral) and facial nerve (medial) is the surgical access route for [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]] (electrode insertion via the round window). It is also used for the posterior tympanotomy approach for cholesteatoma clearance and serves as a key landmark in mastoid surgery for identifying the facial nerve position.

#### 4. Lateral Tympanic Sinus (LTS)

The lateral tympanic sinus is a small space bounded medially by the facial nerve (mastoid segment), laterally by the tympanic annulus and tympanic membrane, and posteriorly by the posterior canal wall bone. It is an uncommon site for cholesteatoma and may be better appreciated endoscopically than with the operating microscope.

#### 5. Round Window Niche / Sinus (Fossula Fenestrae Cochleae)

The round window niche is bounded superiorly by the subiculum, inferiorly by the floor of the tympanic cavity (jugular wall), medially and anteriorly by the promontory (otic capsule bone encasing the cochlea), and posteriorly by the bony overhang (crista fenestrae cochleae). It contains the round window membrane (secondary tympanic membrane).

The round window niche is the site of cochlear implant electrode insertion (round window approach) and is examined during perilymph fistula assessment. Its bony overhang often requires drilling for adequate exposure during cochlear implant surgery. See Part F for detailed measurements.

#### 6. Subtympanic Sinus (Subcochlear Sinus / Infracochlear Recess)

The subtympanic sinus lies below the round window niche and above the jugular bulb. It is bounded superiorly by the round window niche and cochlear floor, inferiorly by the jugular bulb, medially by labyrinthine bone, and laterally by the facial nerve (mastoid segment).

This space communicates with the infralabyrinthine air cells and represents a potential pathway for disease extension between the middle ear and the petrous apex. Its depth and pneumatization are variable.

### Summary Table of Retrotympanic Spaces

*FN = facial nerve; CT = chorda tympani; CI = cochlear implant; RW = round window*

## Part D: Sinus Tympani — Types and Grades (Q52)

### Marchioni Classification of Sinus Tympani

The sinus tympani is classified based on its depth relative to the facial nerve canal. The depth determines surgical accessibility and the risk of residual cholesteatoma. The reference line for classification is drawn along the posterior surface of the facial nerve canal (mastoid segment), and the depth of the sinus tympani is measured as the distance the sinus extends medial (deep) to this line.

### Classification Details

**Type A (Shallow):** The sinus tympani does not reach the line of the posterior wall of the facial nerve canal. This is the most common type. It is easily visualized and accessed, and endoscopic clearance is straightforward.

**Type B (Moderate depth):** The sinus tympani extends to the level of the posterior wall of the facial nerve canal. This is the second most common type. It presents moderate difficulty and requires an angled endoscope (30-degree) for complete visualization.

**Type C (Deep):** The sinus tympani extends significantly medial (deep) to the facial nerve canal line. This is the least common but the most clinically significant type. It cannot be visualized with the microscope and requires a 30-degree or 45-degree endoscope. A retrofacial approach may be necessary.

Bilateral asymmetry is common — the sinus tympani depth may differ between ears in the same patient.

### Dimensions from Cadaveric and Radiological Studies

| Parameter | Range | Mean |
|-----------|-------|------|
| **Depth** (anterior-posterior, measured from FN line) | 1-7 mm | ~3 mm |
| **Width** (superoinferior, between ponticulus and subiculum) | 2-4 mm | ~3 mm |
| **Height** (mediolateral) | Variable | ~2-3 mm |

### Clinical and Surgical Significance

The sinus tympani is the single most common site for residual cholesteatoma after mastoid surgery. Type C (deep) sinus tympani presents the greatest challenge for disease clearance.

The operating microscope cannot visualize the sinus tympani adequately through a posterior tympanotomy because the facial nerve lies between the surgeon and the sinus. The endoscope (transcanal, 30-degree or 45-degree) looks directly into the sinus tympani from the mesotympanum, providing complete visualization. This is the single most compelling anatomical argument for endoscopic ear surgery in cholesteatoma.

Pre-operative high-resolution CT temporal bone (axial and coronal) can delineate the depth and extent of the sinus tympani, allowing pre-operative surgical planning.

**Surgical approaches for deep (Type C) sinus tympani cholesteatoma:**
1. Endoscopic transcanal with 30-degree or 45-degree endoscope (first-line)
2. Retrofacial approach (posterior to the facial nerve via mastoidectomy)
3. Combined microscopic-endoscopic approach
4. Infralabyrinthine approach (for very deep extension)

## Part E: Retrotympanum Anatomy — Endoscopic View (Q74)

### Endoscopic Transcanal Approach to the Retrotympanum

The retrotympanum is best visualized endoscopically after elevation of a tympanomeatal flap. A 0-degree endoscope provides the initial view, and angled scopes (30-degree, 45-degree) are used to look into deeper recesses.

### Key Bony Landmarks

#### Ponticulus

The ponticulus is a bony ridge (bar) running from the promontory to the pyramidal eminence. It separates the oval window area above from the sinus tympani below, forming the superior boundary of the sinus tympani. Its prominence is variable — it may be a well-defined ridge or a subtle elevation. It serves as an important surgical landmark when dissecting cholesteatoma from the sinus tympani.

#### Subiculum (Subculum)

The subiculum is a bony ridge running from the promontory to the styloid eminence (just below the pyramidal process). It separates the sinus tympani above from the round window niche below, forming the inferior boundary of the sinus tympani. It is also known as the "crista tympanica posterior" in some nomenclatures.

#### Pyramidal Eminence (Pyramid)

The pyramidal eminence is a conical bony projection on the posterior wall of the mesotympanum. It is hollow and contains the stapedius muscle. The stapedius tendon emerges from the apex and attaches to the posterior surface of the stapes neck/head. It is a landmark for the facial nerve, which runs immediately posterior and lateral to the pyramidal eminence. The ponticulus attaches to its medial base; the subiculum attaches at its inferior base.

#### Styloid Eminence

The styloid eminence is a small bony elevation below and lateral to the pyramidal eminence, related to the styloid complex. The subiculum connects the promontory to this structure.

### Spatial Relationships

The retrotympanum is organized into distinct zones defined by the ponticulus and subiculum. Above the ponticulus lies the oval window area, which contains the stapes and the tympanic segment of the facial nerve running above the oval window. Between the ponticulus and subiculum lies the sinus tympani, the deepest retrotympanic recess situated medial to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve. Below the subiculum lies the round window niche, which contains the round window membrane and sits above the hypotympanum. Lateral to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve lie the posterior sinus and facial recess, both situated between the facial nerve and the chorda tympani/annulus. Below the round window niche lies the subtympanic sinus, positioned above the jugular bulb and communicating with the infracochlear space.

### Facial Nerve Relationships in the Retrotympanum

The facial nerve has a critical relationship with retrotympanic structures. The tympanic segment runs horizontally above the oval window on the medial wall of the epitympanum/mesotympanum. It is separated from the oval window by a thin bony canal that may be dehiscent. At the pyramidal (second) genu, the nerve turns posteroinferiorly at the level of the pyramidal eminence, marking the transition from the horizontal (tympanic) to the vertical (mastoid) segment. The mastoid (vertical) segment then descends vertically behind the posterior wall of the mesotympanum, lying lateral to the sinus tympani and medial to the posterior sinus and facial recess.

## Part F: Round Window Membrane — Position, Relationships, and Measurements (Q74)

### Anatomy of the Round Window Region

The round window (fenestra cochleae) is the secondary opening of the cochlea into the middle ear. It is closed by the round window membrane (secondary tympanic membrane).

#### Round Window Membrane Characteristics

The round window membrane is roughly circular to oval in shape, with a diameter of approximately 1.5-2 mm and a thickness of approximately 70 micrometres. It consists of three layers: an outer mucosal layer, a middle fibrous layer, and an inner endothelial layer. The membrane is oriented posteroinferiorly and laterally — it is not directly visible en face from the external auditory canal — and is angled approximately 45 degrees from the horizontal plane. It is concave on the niche side and sits at the fundus of the round window niche. The bony overhang of the crista fenestrae cochleae (posterior lip of the RW niche) shields it from direct view.

#### Round Window Niche

The round window niche is a conical funnel that narrows medially toward the RW membrane. It has a depth of 1.5-2.5 mm and its opening faces posteriorly and slightly inferiorly. The crista fenestrae cochleae provides a bony overhang that often requires drilling for cochlear implant electrode insertion. A mucosal fold or fibrous tissue (false membrane) may cover the true niche opening, simulating the RW membrane — this must be identified and removed for CI surgery.

### Key Measurements — Round Window Membrane to Adjacent Structures

| Relationship | Distance | Notes |
|-------------|----------|-------|
| **RW membrane to stapes footplate (oval window)** | ~3.7 mm | Separated by promontory, ponticulus, and subiculum |
| **RW membrane to facial nerve (tympanic segment)** | ~2-3 mm | Variable with FN dehiscence |
| **RW membrane to facial nerve (mastoid segment)** | ~2 mm (at closest) | Vertical FN descends behind and lateral to the RW niche |
| **RW membrane to jugular bulb** | 1-7 mm (mean ~4 mm) | Highly variable; high-riding bulb may be as close as 1 mm |
| **RW niche depth (opening to membrane)** | 1.5-2.5 mm | Depth of the bony funnel |
| **RW membrane to carotid canal** | ~3-4 mm | ICA lies anteromedial to the RW |
| **RW membrane to cochlear aqueduct opening** | ~1 mm | Transmits perilymph communication to subarachnoid space |
| **RW membrane diameter** | ~1.5-2 mm | Generally accepted range |
| **Distance from tympanic annulus to RW** | ~4-5 mm | Distance traversed during transcanal approach |
| **RW membrane to cochlear basal turn** | Immediate | RW membrane directly overlies scala tympani of basal turn |

### Surgical Significance of Round Window Measurements

#### 1. Cochlear Implantation

The round window approach is increasingly preferred over cochleostomy for electrode insertion. The bony overhang (crista fenestrae cochleae) usually requires drilling to expose the full membrane. The RW membrane leads directly into the scala tympani, making it ideal for atraumatic electrode insertion and hearing preservation. A false membrane (mucosal fold) may be present and must be differentiated from the true RW membrane. The round window insertion angle determines the electrode trajectory within the cochlea.

#### 2. High Jugular Bulb Considerations

The jugular bulb-to-RW distance of 1-7 mm is the most variable and clinically important measurement. A high-riding jugular bulb may obscure the RW niche completely, limit access for CI electrode insertion, risk vascular injury during drilling of the RW niche overhang, or require alternative approaches (extended facial recess, transattic). Pre-operative HRCT temporal bone is essential to assess jugular bulb position.

#### 3. Perilymph Fistula Assessment

The RW membrane is a potential site of perilymph fistula. Endoscopic visualization with a 30-degree scope provides a superior view of the membrane. Active perilymph leak presents as clear fluid pooling in the RW niche.

#### 4. Endoscopic Round Window Visualization

With a 0-degree endoscope, the RW niche opening is visible but the membrane may be partially hidden by the bony overhang. A 30-degree endoscope allows the RW membrane to be seen en face, permitting assessment of its integrity. The 45-degree endoscope provides the most complete view of the deep RW niche.

#### 5. Landmark-Based Identification of the Round Window

The RW is identified intraoperatively using the following sequential landmarks:
1. Identify the promontory (largest bulge on the medial wall)
2. Follow the promontory posteroinferiorly
3. Identify the subiculum (bony ridge running from promontory to styloid eminence)
4. The RW niche lies immediately below the subiculum
5. The ponticulus (bony ridge above) separates the RW region from the oval window region
6. The RW membrane is at the fundus (deepest point) of the niche, facing posteroinferiorly
