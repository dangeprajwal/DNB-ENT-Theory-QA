## Part A: Middle Ear Mucosal Folds — Overview (Q110)

### Concept

The middle ear (tympanic cavity) is lined by a continuous respiratory-type mucosa (ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells in the hypotympanum and mesotympanum; cuboidal/flat epithelium in the epitympanum). As this mucosa drapes over the ossicles, tendons, ligaments, chorda tympani nerve, and blood vessels traversing the tympanic cavity, it forms mucosal folds (plicae).

### Key Principles

The mucosal folds of the middle ear are formed when the continuous mucosal lining reflects over structures that cross the tympanic cavity, creating thin, sheet-like folds analogous to peritoneal folds in the abdomen. These folds collectively divide the middle ear into a superior compartment (the epitympanum or attic) and inferior compartments (the mesotympanum and hypotympanum). Where folds thicken and incorporate fibrous tissue, they become true ligaments — for example, the superior malleal ligament is simply a thickened superior malleal fold.

The folds collectively form an incomplete partition known as the tympanic diaphragm between the epitympanum and mesotympanum. This partition is perforated only by narrow openings called the tympanic isthmi. The spaces and passages created by these folds are of critical clinical importance because they determine ventilation pathways, govern disease spread, and create hidden areas where cholesteatoma can develop and persist.

## Part B: Major Mucosal Folds and Ligaments (Q110)

### Malleal Folds

The malleus is associated with four major mucosal folds, each of which plays a distinct role in compartmentalizing the middle ear.

- **Anterior malleal fold (anterior malleolar fold)** runs from the anterior process of the malleus to the anterior tympanic wall in the region of the petrotympanic (glaserian) fissure. It defines the anterior boundary of Prussak's space and may contain the anterior tympanic artery and the chorda tympani nerve as it enters the middle ear.

- **Lateral malleal fold (lateral malleolar fold)** extends from the lateral process of the malleus to the tympanic sulcus (the rim of the bony canal). This fold defines the superior boundary of Prussak's space and corresponds to the visible anterior and posterior malleolar folds seen on otoscopic examination of the tympanic membrane. Functionally, it separates the pars flaccida above from the pars tensa below.

- **Superior malleal fold (superior malleolar ligament)** ascends from the head of the malleus superiorly to the tegmen tympani, suspending the malleus from the roof of the epitympanum. When this fold thickens and incorporates fibrous tissue, it functions as the superior malleal ligament.

- **Tensor fold (tensor tympani fold)** extends from the tensor tympani tendon and cochleariform process to the malleus neck, running horizontally across the epitympanum. This is one of the most clinically important folds because it acts as a critical barrier separating the anterior epitympanic space from the posterior epitympanic space and is central to cholesteatoma pathophysiology.

### Incudal Folds

The incus is associated with four mucosal folds that contribute to the architecture of the epitympanum and the tympanic diaphragm.

- **Superior incudal fold (superior incudal ligament)** ascends from the body of the incus to the tegmen tympani, suspending the incus from the roof. When thickened with fibrous tissue, it becomes the superior incudal ligament.

- **Lateral incudal fold** extends from the body of the incus to the lateral wall of the epitympanum (scutum). This fold forms part of the lateral boundary of the epitympanum and contributes to the tympanic diaphragm.

- **Medial incudal fold** passes from the body and short process of the incus to the medial wall of the epitympanum, contributing to the tympanic diaphragm on its medial aspect.

- **Posterior incudal fold** runs from the short process of the incus to the fossa incudis (posterior attic wall). It defines the posterior boundary of the posterior epitympanic space and serves as an important landmark between the epitympanum and the aditus ad antrum.

### Other Important Folds

Several additional mucosal folds and ligaments are of anatomical and clinical relevance. The **stapes fold** is the mucosal covering over the stapes superstructure; it is a minimal fold and does not form a significant barrier. The **chorda tympani fold** is created by the mucosa draping over the chorda tympani nerve as it crosses the middle ear; it is a minor fold, though the chorda tympani itself defines the lateral boundary of the facial recess. The **lateral malleal ligament** runs from the malleus neck to the scutum (lateral attic wall). Although not a true mucosal fold but rather a fibrous ligament, it defines the superior boundary of Prussak's space in some classifications.

## Part C: Middle Ear Spaces Created by Mucosal Folds (Q110)

### Epitympanic Spaces

#### 1. Prussak's Space (Superior Recess of TM / Lateral Epitympanic Space)

Prussak's space is the single most important space in cholesteatoma pathogenesis. It is bounded superiorly by the lateral malleal fold (lateral malleolar ligament), inferiorly by the lateral process of the malleus, laterally by the pars flaccida (Shrapnell's membrane), medially by the neck of the malleus, anteriorly by the anterior malleal fold, and posteriorly by the posterior malleal fold (posterior pouch of von Troeltsch area).

This space is the most common site of origin of pars flaccida cholesteatoma, which is itself the commonest type of cholesteatoma. Prussak's space is poorly ventilated, being dependent on isthmus patency for aeration. Retraction of the pars flaccida into this space represents the earliest visible sign of developing cholesteatoma. The space contains loose areolar tissue and may contain a small vessel.

#### 2. Anterior Epitympanic Space (Anterior Attic / Supratubal Recess)

The anterior epitympanic space is bounded inferiorly by the tensor fold (tensor tympani fold), superiorly by the tegmen tympani, posteriorly by the head of the malleus (communicating with the posterior epitympanum), anteriorly by the anterior epitympanic wall (merging with the supratubal recess), medially by the medial attic wall (tegmen of the tensor tympani canal), and laterally by the scutum (lateral attic wall).

This space contains the head of the malleus and is very poorly ventilated — its ventilation depends on the anterior tympanic isthmus (anticus). Cholesteatoma can extend here from Prussak's space, and this area is a common hidden site for residual disease. The tensor fold serves as the critical partition separating this space from the mesotympanum below.

#### 3. Posterior Epitympanic Space (Posterior Attic)

The posterior epitympanic space is bounded inferiorly by the posterior tympanic isthmus (posticus), superiorly by the tegmen tympani, anteriorly by the head of the malleus and body of the incus with the lateral and medial incudal folds, posteriorly by the aditus ad antrum with the posterior incudal fold and fossa incudis, medially by the medial attic wall, and laterally by the lateral attic wall (scutum) and lateral incudal fold.

This space contains the body of the incus and its short process (within the fossa incudis). It communicates posteriorly with the aditus ad antrum and thence with the mastoid antrum. Ventilation occurs primarily through the posterior tympanic isthmus (posticus), which is the larger of the two isthmi. The posterior epitympanic space represents the main route for cholesteatoma spread from the epitympanum to the mastoid.

### The Tympanic Diaphragm

The mucosal folds collectively form an incomplete partition (diaphragm) separating the epitympanum above from the mesotympanum below. This concept is central to understanding epitympanic ventilation and cholesteatoma pathogenesis. The components of the tympanic diaphragm include the tensor fold anteriorly, the lateral malleal fold, the lateral incudal fold, the medial incudal fold, and the mucosal reflections over the ossicles.

#### Perforations of the Diaphragm — The Tympanic Isthmi

The diaphragm is perforated by two narrow openings (isthmi), which are the only routes for air to pass from the mesotympanum to the epitympanum.

The **tympanic isthmus anticus (anterior isthmus)** lies between the tensor fold superiorly and the stapes/oval window region inferiorly. Its boundaries are the tensor fold superiorly, the tympanic segment of the facial nerve and oval window inferiorly, the malleus handle laterally, and the medial attic wall medially. It is the smaller of the two isthmi and is often very narrow or entirely absent. It serves as the anterior ventilation pathway for the epitympanum and may be blocked early in disease by mucosal oedema or cholesteatoma.

The **tympanic isthmus posticus (posterior isthmus)** lies between the posterior incudal fold and short process of the incus superiorly and the medial wall of the middle ear inferiorly. Its boundaries are the short process of the incus and posterior incudal fold superiorly, the posterior canal wall laterally, the medial attic wall medially, and the stapes and facial nerve canal inferiorly. It is the larger of the two isthmi and serves as the principal ventilation route for the epitympanum. Blockage of this isthmus is the key event leading to isolated epitympanic hypoventilation.

#### The Epitympanic Ventilation Bottleneck

The epitympanum is connected to the well-ventilated mesotympanum only through these two narrow isthmi. Blockage of the isthmi — whether by mucosal oedema, granulation tissue, cholesteatoma, or thickened folds — leads to isolated epitympanic negative pressure. This negative pressure causes retraction of the pars flaccida into Prussak's space, initiating cholesteatoma formation. This anatomical bottleneck explains why cholesteatoma preferentially develops in the epitympanum and pars flaccida region.

### Mesotympanic Spaces

The mesotympanum and its surrounding regions contain a number of clinically important spaces defined by mucosal folds, bony landmarks, and neurovascular structures.

- **Anterior pouch of von Troeltsch** lies between the anterior malleolar fold and the tympanic membrane, anterior to the malleus handle. It is bounded superiorly by the anterior malleal fold, laterally by the TM (pars tensa), and medially by the malleus handle. This is a small pouch that may trap small cholesteatoma pearls.

- **Posterior pouch of von Troeltsch** lies between the posterior malleolar fold and the tympanic membrane, posterior to the malleus handle. It is bounded superiorly by the posterior malleal fold, laterally by the TM (pars tensa), and medially by the malleus handle. This pouch is clinically more significant than the anterior pouch because posterosuperior pars tensa retraction pockets form here.

- **Sinus tympani** is located in the posterior mesotympanum, medial to the facial nerve. It is bounded superiorly by the ponticulus, inferiorly by the subiculum, laterally by the facial nerve (mastoid segment), and medially by labyrinthine bone (posterior semicircular canal). The sinus tympani is the most common hidden site for residual cholesteatoma; it is not visible via the microscope through posterior tympanotomy and requires endoscopic visualization.

- **Facial recess (posterior tympanotomy area)** lies in the posterior mesotympanum, lateral to the facial nerve. It is bounded superiorly by the fossa incudis, laterally by the chorda tympani, and medially by the facial nerve (mastoid segment). This space is accessed surgically via posterior tympanotomy and is important for [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] electrode insertion. Cholesteatoma may extend into this area.

- **Posterior sinus** lies between the facial recess and the sinus tympani. It is bounded superiorly by the fossa incudis, medially by the pyramidal eminence, and laterally by the chorda tympani and tympanic annulus. This intermediate space may harbour residual cholesteatoma.

- **Hypotympanum** lies below the level of the inferior margin of the tympanic membrane annulus. It is bounded superiorly by the floor of the mesotympanum, inferiorly by the jugular bulb, laterally by the inferior tympanic bone, and medially by the inferior promontory. The hypotympanum has variable depth (2–10 mm). Cholesteatoma is rarely primary here but may extend into this space. A high jugular bulb can limit surgical access.

- **Protympanum** is the deep anterior space near the tympanic orifice of the Eustachian tube. It is bounded superiorly by the tensor tympani canal, inferiorly by the carotid canal area, medially by the carotid canal, and laterally by the anterior tympanic bone. This space is poorly visualized with a microscope. Cholesteatoma may extend anteriorly into this area, and the internal carotid artery lies in close proximity.

## Part D: Ventilation Pathways of the Middle Ear (Q110)

### Normal Ventilation Route

The middle ear cleft is ventilated by a sequential pathway that begins at the nasopharynx and terminates at the mastoid air cell system. Air passes from the nasopharynx through the Eustachian tube into the protympanum and then the mesotympanum, which is freely ventilated as a large space. From the mesotympanum, air must pass through the two narrow tympanic isthmi (anticus and posticus) — the critical bottlenecks — to reach the epitympanum (attic). From the epitympanum, air passes posteriorly through the aditus ad antrum into the mastoid antrum and finally into the mastoid air cells, which represent the terminal ventilation reservoir.

### Key Concepts

The mesotympanum is well-ventilated, being directly connected to the Eustachian tube via the protympanum. In contrast, the epitympanum is a poorly ventilated space connected to the mesotympanum only through the narrow tympanic isthmi. The posterior isthmus (posticus) is the larger and more important of the two and serves as the main ventilation pathway for the epitympanum.

Blockage of the isthmi by any pathology — mucosal oedema, polyps, granulation tissue, or cholesteatoma matrix — leads to selective epitympanic hypoventilation. This selective hypoventilation triggers a cascade: gas absorption from the epitympanic mucosa, negative pressure in the epitympanum, retraction of the pars flaccida into Prussak's space, formation of a retraction pocket, accumulation of squamous debris, and ultimately cholesteatoma development.

The mastoid air cell system is the terminal reservoir of the ventilation pathway. Its pneumatization depends on adequate middle ear ventilation during childhood.

### Eustachian Tube Dysfunction vs Isthmus Blockade

| Feature | ET Dysfunction | Isthmus Blockade |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| Effect on mesotympanum | Negative pressure in entire middle ear | Mesotympanum normally ventilated |
| Effect on epitympanum | Negative pressure (global) | Selective negative pressure in epitympanum ONLY |
| TM retraction | Generalized pars tensa retraction | Pars flaccida retraction (into Prussak's space) |
| Type of cholesteatoma | Pars tensa cholesteatoma (posterosuperior retraction) | Pars flaccida cholesteatoma (most common type) |
| Pathogenic mechanism | Global middle ear hypoventilation | Selective epitympanic compartmentalization |

## Part E: Cholesteatoma Spread in Relation to Middle Ear Spaces (Q110)

### Pars Flaccida Cholesteatoma (Most Common, ~80% of Acquired Cholesteatoma)

#### Origin and Mechanism

Pars flaccida cholesteatoma arises from retraction of the pars flaccida into Prussak's space, driven by selective epitympanic negative pressure resulting from isthmus blockade. The squamous epithelium of the pars flaccida desquamates into the retraction pocket, and keratin debris accumulates because the confined space prevents self-cleaning. Progressive expansion of the cholesteatoma sac occurs through bone erosion, mediated by osteoclastic activity stimulated by the cholesteatoma perimatrix.

#### Stepwise Spread Pattern

1. **Origin:** The pars flaccida retracts into Prussak's space, involving the pars flaccida, the lateral malleal fold, and the neck of the malleus.
2. **Lateral erosion:** The cholesteatoma erodes the scutum (outer attic wall) laterally. This is the earliest bony erosion visible on CT, appearing as characteristic "blunting" of the scutum on coronal imaging.
3. **Medial spread:** The cholesteatoma extends medially around the neck of the malleus into the epitympanum proper. It may envelop the head of the malleus.
4. **Posterior spread:** Extension occurs posteriorly into the posterior epitympanic space, around the body of the incus, into the aditus ad antrum, and then into the mastoid antrum and mastoid air cells. The long process of the incus is eroded first. This is the classic route of extensive disease.
5. **Inferior spread:** The cholesteatoma extends inferiorly through the tympanic isthmi (or by destroying the tympanic diaphragm folds) into the mesotympanum, involving the ossicular chain, stapes superstructure, and oval window area.
6. **Anterior spread:** Extension occurs anteriorly into the anterior epitympanic space and supratubal recess, towards the Eustachian tube orifice. This can block ET ventilation and involves the tensor fold area.
7. **Deep (retrotympanic) spread:** Extension occurs posteroinferiorly and medially into the sinus tympani (medial to the facial nerve), the facial recess, and the retrofacial area. These locations are hidden from microscopic view.
8. **Superior spread:** The cholesteatoma erodes superiorly through the tegmen tympani into the middle cranial fossa, risking intracranial complications including epidural abscess, meningitis, and temporal lobe abscess.

### Pars Tensa Cholesteatoma (~20% of Acquired Cholesteatoma)

#### Origin and Mechanism

Pars tensa cholesteatoma arises from retraction of the posterosuperior quadrant of the pars tensa, typically into the posterior pouch of von Troeltsch. It is associated with global Eustachian tube dysfunction and chronic negative middle ear pressure. As the retraction pocket deepens, it loses its self-cleaning ability and keratin accumulates.

#### Spread Pattern

1. **Origin:** A posterosuperior retraction pocket forms in the pars tensa, involving the posterior pouch of von Troeltsch and the posterior mesotympanum.
2. **Medial spread:** The cholesteatoma extends medially into the posterior mesotympanum, around the incudostapedial joint. The long process of the incus is the most commonly eroded ossicular structure in all types of cholesteatoma, followed by the stapes superstructure.
3. **Retrotympanic spread:** Extension occurs posteromedially into the sinus tympani, posterior sinus, and facial recess — deep to the facial nerve and very difficult to clear microscopically.
4. **Superior spread:** The cholesteatoma extends superiorly through the posterior tympanic isthmus into the epitympanum, then around the incus body and into the aditus and antrum.
5. **Inferior spread:** Extension occurs inferiorly into the hypotympanum and around the promontory, involving the round window niche and hypotympanic air cells.
6. **Anterior spread:** The cholesteatoma extends anteriorly along the undersurface of the tympanic membrane and around the promontory towards the protympanum and ET orifice area.

### Ossicular Erosion Pattern in Cholesteatoma

| Ossicle/Component | Frequency of Erosion | Mechanism |
|-------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Long process of incus | Most common (first to erode) | Thin structure; vulnerable to pressure necrosis and osteoclastic resorption; tenuous blood supply (end artery) |
| Incudostapedial joint | Very common | Often eroded early; causes conductive hearing loss |
| Stapes superstructure | Common | Crura and head eroded; footplate usually preserved (annular ligament and periosteum protect it) |
| Head of malleus | Less common | Involved in extensive attic cholesteatoma |
| Body of incus | Less common | Large, robust; eroded in advanced disease |
| Malleus handle | Least common | Protected by its attachment to the TM |
| Stapes footplate | Rare | Usually preserved; erosion risks labyrinthine fistula and dead ear |

### Routes of Hidden Cholesteatoma (Residual Disease Sites)

These are areas where cholesteatoma may persist after surgery (residual disease) because they are difficult to visualize and access, particularly with the operating microscope. The endoscope has dramatically improved visualization of all these recesses.

- **Sinus tympani:** This deep recess medial to the facial nerve has variable depth (1–7 mm, Types A–C). The facial nerve blocks the microscopic line of sight through posterior tympanotomy, but a 30–45 degree endoscope provides direct transcanal visualization around the facial nerve.

- **Anterior epitympanum:** This narrow space anterior to the malleus head is partitioned by the tensor fold. The malleus head obscures the microscopic view and the microscope angle is limited, but the endoscope can visualize around the malleus head with angled views.

- **Supratubal recess:** This deep anterior recess above the ET orifice is continuous with the anterior epitympanum. It is very difficult to access from mastoid or canal approaches with the microscope, but an angled endoscope provides access from the mesotympanum.

- **Facial recess:** This narrow triangular space lies in close proximity to the facial nerve, which limits the extent of bone removal during posterior tympanotomy. The endoscope provides a magnified view without requiring additional bone removal.

- **Hypotympanum:** This space below the TM annulus level is not routinely inspected. The microscope, directed straight into the canal, cannot see below the annulus adequately, but the endoscope can look inferiorly around the annulus.

- **Protympanum:** This deep anterior space lies near the internal carotid artery. Anterior tympanic bone limits microscopic access, but the endoscope can visualize around the promontory anteriorly.

- **Retrotympanum (posterior sinus):** Located between the pyramidal eminence and chorda tympani, this space is partially obscured from standard microscopic angles. The endoscope provides a panoramic view of the entire retrotympanum.

### Cholesteatoma Spread — Pathophysiology Summary

Cholesteatoma expansion follows the path of least resistance, spreading along mucosal folds, through natural openings such as the isthmi and aditus, and into pneumatized spaces before eroding bone. The bone erosion mechanism involves the cholesteatoma perimatrix (subepithelial connective tissue layer), which contains osteoclasts, collagenases (MMP-2, MMP-9), and inflammatory mediators (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha, prostaglandins) that stimulate osteoclastic bone resorption. Additionally, the expanding cholesteatoma sac exerts pressure on adjacent bone, leading to ischaemic necrosis and resorption through pressure necrosis.

The direction and extent of cholesteatoma spread is fundamentally dictated by the mucosal fold anatomy — folds act as barriers that deflect the expanding sac along predictable pathways. Blockage of the ventilation pathways (isthmi) by cholesteatoma creates isolated, hypoventilated compartments with persistent negative pressure, which perpetuates the disease process and promotes further expansion. This establishes a self-perpetuating vicious cycle: cholesteatoma blocks the isthmi, leading to further negative pressure, further retraction, further growth, and further isthmus blockade.

### Complications of Cholesteatoma Spread (Related to Specific Spaces)

The complications of cholesteatoma are directly related to the specific spaces and structures involved in disease spread.

- **Conductive hearing loss** results from ossicular erosion — the long process of the incus, incudostapedial joint, and stapes superstructure — within the epitympanum and mesotympanum.

- **Labyrinthine fistula** occurs from erosion of the lateral semicircular canal (the most common site) in the medial attic wall. Less commonly, the posterior semicircular canal or cochlear promontory may be involved.

- **[[Facial Nerve|Facial nerve palsy]]** results from erosion of the fallopian canal (the tympanic segment is most commonly dehiscent) and may occur when cholesteatoma involves the facial recess or sinus tympani area.

- **Sensorineural hearing loss** may result from labyrinthine fistula, labyrinthitis (serous or suppurative), or cochlear erosion.

- **Intracranial complications** arise from tegmen erosion (epidural abscess, subdural abscess, temporal lobe abscess, meningitis), sigmoid sinus erosion (lateral sinus thrombosis), or petrous apex erosion (Gradenigo syndrome).

- **Mastoid abscess** develops from extension through the mastoid cortex. The type of abscess depends on the site of cortical breakthrough: subperiosteal abscess, Bezold's abscess, Citelli's abscess, or Luc's abscess.
