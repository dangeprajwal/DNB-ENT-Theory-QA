# Middle Ear Mechanics and Acoustic Reflex

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the applied anatomy and physiology of acoustic reflex. Discuss two clinical scenarios where acoustic reflex contributes to decision making. Describe middle ear mechanics and acoustic stapedial reflex measurement.
> Source: Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Volume 2, Chapter 48 - Physiology of Hearing; Chapter 51 - Psychoacoustic Audiometry

---

## INTRODUCTION

The middle ear in humans acts as an efficient passive and linear transformer to conduct acoustic energy from the tympanic membrane to the stapes footplate at the oval window and to the cochlea. Its fundamental function is to provide critical modifications to sound energy by providing mechanical advantages to overcome the impedance encountered when sound is conducted from an air-filled medium (middle ear cavity) to a fluid-filled medium (perilymphatic and endolymphatic fluid in the cochlea). The acoustic reflex is an integral component of middle ear function that provides protection to the cochlea and modulates sound transmission.

---

## PART A: MIDDLE EAR MECHANICS

### Basic Principles of Sound Transmission

The middle ear apparatus consists of three main components:

- Tympanic membrane
- Ossicular chain (malleus-incus-stapes complex)
- Middle ear muscles (tensor tympani and stapedius)

Essentially, acoustic energy is transferred from a low-impedance, high-velocity medium to a high-impedance, low-velocity medium through ossicular coupling.

### Middle Ear Transfer Function

The greatest transfer function of the middle ear occurs at the resonant frequency between 1 and 3 kHz and is determined by the mass and stiffness of the system. The middle ear may be considered as a system with band-pass filtering action.

| Factor | Effect on Sound Transmission |
|--------|------------------------------|
| Stiffness | Limits low-frequency sounds |
| Mass | Limits high-frequency sounds |
| Resonant frequency | 1-3 kHz (most efficient) |

### Stiffness and Mass Components

Stiffness (elasticity) is determined by:

- Tympanic membrane
- Ossicular ligaments
- Middle ear cavity air compression

Mass is governed by:

- Ossicles

### Impedance Matching Mechanisms

The primary mechanisms to match the acoustic impedance at the air-fluid interface are:

#### 1. Surface Area Ratio

The ratio of the tympanic membrane to the stapes footplate is approximately 18:1. This disproportionality contributes to overcoming the acoustic impedance mismatch at the air-fluid interface between the middle and inner ear.

#### 2. Ossicular Lever Action

The geometric length of the malleus is approximately 2.1 times that of the incus. This lever action multiplies force by 2.1 times, and with velocity decreased by a factor of 2.1, the net mechanical advantage (impedance ratio) is 4.4.

The lever action changes with frequency, being most efficient at 2 kHz.

#### 3. Tympanic Membrane Mechanics

The tympanic membrane provides multiple integrated multilever mechanical advantage through its non-uniform structure. Key features include:

- Canal side exhibits uniphasic response with little pressure variation
- Middle ear side is multiphasic with significant pressure variations
- Incident sound creates a travelling wave collected mainly at the rim
- Energy conducted to umbo and coupled to manubrium of malleus
- At lower frequencies: uniform membrane movement to malleus
- At higher frequencies: more complex movement with partial shunting

### Ossicular Chain Function

The three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes) are connected by synovial joints for transmitting acoustic stimulus from tympanic membrane to oval window.

#### Axis of Rotation

The malleus and incus rotate around an anterior-posterior axis passing through the centre of gravity of the ossicles.

#### Frequency-Dependent Movement

| Frequency | Ossicular Movement Pattern |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Low frequencies | Relatively less motion between malleus and incus |
| High frequencies | More complex motion with slippage between bones |
| Stapes at low freq | Simple piston-like movements |
| Stapes at high freq | Complex spatial movements along footplate axes |

### Pressure Gain and Impedance Match

At the resonant frequency of 2.7 kHz:

| Parameter | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Sound pressure loss at cochlea | ~35 dB |
| External canal gain | 9 dB |
| Middle ear gain | 26.6 dB |
| Total gain | 35.6 dB |

The impedance match is closest at the resonant frequency, with the external and middle ear transfer function very closely matched to the sound pressure loss.

### The Middle Ear Windows

#### Oval Window

- Articulates with stapes footplate
- Site of preferential distribution of sound energy
- Pressure difference with round window is fundamental for cochlear travelling wave

#### Round Window

- Covered by secondary tympanic membrane
- Provides pressure release for cochlear fluid movement
- Participates in absorption and secretion of perilymph (semipermeable membrane)
- Used for intratympanic drug delivery
- Alternative approach for [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] insertion

#### Third Window Phenomenon

Pathological third windows (real or virtual) can shunt part of the preferential distribution:

- Real: Perilymph fistula, X-linked gusher
- Virtual: Dilated vestibular aqueduct, semicircular canal dehiscence

Effect: Air-conduction thresholds decrease, bone-conduction thresholds increase, creating a spurious "false" air-bone gap.

## PART B: ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY OF ACOUSTIC REFLEX

### Middle Ear Muscles

There are two middle ear muscles with function in humans:

#### 1. Tensor Tympani

- Nerve supply: Trigeminal nerve (Vth cranial nerve)
- Attachment: Malleus manubrium
- Action: Pulls malleus anteriorly
- Electrical activity: Very low in response to sound

Activated by:

- Tactile stimulation of EAC and face
- Pneumatic pressure on eyelids
- Sudden forced opening/closing of eyelids
- Swallowing
- Head movements
- Anticipation of loud sounds
- Startle response

Disorder: Tensor tympani syndrome - manifests as audible thump in ear (rare)

#### 2. Stapedius Muscle

- Nerve supply: Facial nerve (VIIth cranial nerve)
- Attachment: Stapes neck
- Action: Pulls annular ligament in footplate
- Response: Primarily to high-intensity, low-frequency sounds (~0.8 kHz)

### The Stapedial (Acoustic) Reflex

Stapedius contraction is responsible for the stapedial reflex which:

- Moves stapes approximately 50 microns
- Increases stiffness of ossicular chain
- Attenuates sound transmission to inner ear by up to 30 dB

#### Reflex Threshold

The reflex is elicited at 80-90 dB SL intensity range.

#### Reflex Latency

The stapedius tendon takes approximately 100-200 ms to contract fully. This implies it cannot protect the cochlea from intense short-impulse noise (e.g., gunshot), although a noise primer just before may be otoprotective.

### Functions of the Acoustic Reflex

| Function | Mechanism |
|----------|-----------|
| Cochlear protection | Attenuates high-intensity sounds to preserve dynamic range |
| Speech enhancement | Reduces low-frequency masking of high-frequency consonants |
| Self-voice modulation | Protects ear from one's own voice during vocalization |

The middle ear system at lower frequencies generates maximum impedance to incident sounds, and the reflex reduces output from high-intensity low-frequency sounds that may mask high-frequency speech consonant sounds at the base of the cochlea.

### Neural Pathway of Acoustic Reflex

The neuronal circuitry of the reflex crosses over to the other side. Therefore, the reflex can be elicited by both ipsilateral and contralateral stimuli delivered to one ear.

#### Reflex Arc Components

1. Afferent limb: Cochlea - Auditory nerve - Cochlear nucleus
2. Central connections: Superior olivary complex (bilateral)
3. Efferent limb: Facial nerve nucleus - Facial nerve - Stapedius muscle

## PART C: ACOUSTIC STAPEDIAL REFLEX MEASUREMENT

### Technique

Measurement is performed through tympanometry with additional testing for the acoustic stapedial reflex. The reflex is elicited by brief pure tones or noise bursts, and the change in middle ear compliance is measured.

### Acoustic Reflex Threshold (ART)

The lowest intensity of sound that triggers the reflex is the acoustic reflex threshold. This provides an objective measure of loudness recruitment in addition to subjective uncomfortable loudness level measurement.

### Interpretation of Results

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---------|----------------|
| Absent reflex, normal ME pressure | [[Otosclerosis|Otosclerosis]] characteristic |
| Elevated reflex threshold | Cochlear hearing loss |
| Reflex decay | Retrocochlear pathology |
| Preserved reflex with air-bone gap | Third window pathology |

### Response Patterns

Several possible response patterns reflect different types of hearing losses:

- Conductive hearing loss: Absent or elevated reflex
- Sensory (cochlear) hearing loss: Reflex may be preserved with recruitment
- Neural hearing loss: Reflex decay present

## PART D: CLINICAL APPLICATIONS OF ACOUSTIC REFLEX

### Clinical Scenario 1: Screening for Auditory Neuropathy Spectrum Disorder (ANSD)

The most important utility of measuring the stapedial reflex is to screen for auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder in cases of hearing/listening problems with preserved otoacoustic emissions.

Clinical presentation:

- Poor speech discrimination out of proportion to pure tone thresholds
- Present OAEs indicating normal outer hair cell function
- Absent or abnormal acoustic reflex
- Abnormal ABR

The acoustic reflex is useful to test for auditory neuropathy in the clinical setting as it requires neural synchrony. The postauricular myogenic response (PAMR) can augment suspicion before ABR confirmation.

Decision-making: If OAEs are present but acoustic reflex is absent, this strongly suggests ANSD and warrants ABR testing for confirmation.

### Clinical Scenario 2: Diagnosis of Otosclerosis

An absent or abnormal stapedial reflex with normal middle ear pressure is characteristic of otosclerosis.

Clinical presentation:

- Progressive conductive hearing loss
- Normal otoscopy (flamingo flush may be seen)
- Type As tympanogram (reduced compliance)
- Absent acoustic reflex despite normal middle ear pressure

Decision-making: In a patient with conductive hearing loss and absent acoustic reflex but normal tympanometry, otosclerosis should be suspected. This helps differentiate from ossicular discontinuity where reflexes may be absent due to interrupted chain.

### Clinical Scenario 3: Detection of Third Window Pathology

In third window pathology (e.g., superior semicircular canal dehiscence), the reflex may be preserved despite a significant air-bone gap on pure-tone audiometry.

This contrasts with true middle ear pathology where:

- Air-bone gap present
- Acoustic reflex absent

Decision-making: Preserved acoustic reflex in presence of air-bone gap suggests third window phenomenon rather than middle ear pathology, prompting high-resolution CT imaging.

### Clinical Scenario 4: Assessment of Retrocochlear Pathology

The stapedial reflex threshold and decay have been used in assessment of [[Vestibular Schwannoma|acoustic neuroma]]. However, sensitivity is relatively low compared to PTA asymmetry. High stimulus levels required pose a hazard to residual hearing.

Reflex decay test:

- Sustained stimulus presented at 10 dB above reflex threshold
- Decay of more than 50% in 10 seconds suggests retrocochlear lesion

### Clinical Scenario 5: Cochlear Implant Programming

The stapedial reflex can be electrically evoked in users of cochlear implants to obtain information about their dynamic ranges. This objective measure helps optimize programming parameters.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The middle ear acts as an impedance-matching transformer converting low-impedance air vibrations to high-impedance fluid vibrations with approximately 35 dB pressure gain.
>
> 2. Impedance matching is achieved through surface area ratio (18:1 TM to stapes footplate) and ossicular lever action (2.1:1).
>
> 3. The middle ear resonant frequency is 1-3 kHz where maximum transfer efficiency occurs.
>
> 4. Stiffness limits low-frequency transmission while mass limits high-frequency transmission.
>
> 5. The stapedius muscle (facial nerve) is the dominant reflex muscle, while tensor tympani (trigeminal nerve) has minimal acoustic function.
>
> 6. Acoustic reflex threshold is 80-90 dB SL with latency of 100-200 ms, providing up to 30 dB attenuation.
>
> 7. The reflex pathway crosses midline, allowing ipsilateral and contralateral testing.
>
> 8. Three functions of acoustic reflex: cochlear protection, speech enhancement, self-voice modulation.
>
> 9. Absent reflex with normal middle ear pressure is characteristic of otosclerosis.
>
> 10. Preserved reflex with air-bone gap suggests third window pathology rather than middle ear disease.
>
> 11. Absent acoustic reflex with preserved OAEs is a hallmark of auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder.
>
> 12. Reflex decay of more than 50% in 10 seconds suggests retrocochlear pathology.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. The acoustic reflex cannot protect against impulsive noise (gunshot) due to 100-200 ms latency - damage occurs before reflex contracts.
>
> 2. In bats, the acoustic reflex completely disarticulates the ossicular chain during echolocation - an evolutionary adaptation for self-protection.
>
> 3. Third window pathology creates a "false" air-bone gap with preserved acoustic reflexes, distinguishing it from true conductive loss.
>
> 4. The round window's semipermeable membrane allows intratympanic drug delivery to achieve effective intralabyrinthine concentrations.
>
> 5. Carhart's notch (2 kHz BC dip) in otosclerosis reflects loss of the ossicular component of bone conduction at the middle ear resonant frequency.
>
> 6. The stapedial reflex may be audible, generating objective [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] due to stapedial tendon myoclonus.
