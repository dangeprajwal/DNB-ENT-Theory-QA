## Part A: CI Candidacy Criteria — Adults (Q4, Q33, Q65, Q116)

### NICE Guidelines (England & Wales)
CI should be considered for any person with severe to profound hearing loss who does not gain adequate benefit from acoustic hearing aids:

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| Audiological threshold | ==**9e update:** Ability to hear only sounds louder than **80 dB HL at 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 3 kHz and 4 kHz** bilaterally without hearing aids== *(previously specified only 2 & 4 kHz)* |
| Hearing aid trial | Minimum **3 months** (unless inappropriate or contraindicated) |
| Adequate benefit (adults) | ==**9e update:** **Phoneme score < 50% on the Arthur Boothroyd (AB) Word test** presented at 70 dBA== *(replaces BKB sentence testing in 9e criteria)* |
| Adequate benefit (children) | Failure to gain speech, language and listening skills appropriate to age, developmental stage and cognitive ability |
| Assessment | By **multidisciplinary team** (MDT); tests adapted for disabilities and communication ability |
| Bilateral CI | Recommended for **all children** and for adults who are **blind** or have other disabilities increasing reliance on auditory stimuli as a primary sensory mechanism for spatial awareness |

### Assessment Process
- **Audiological:** PTA, speech audiometry (BKB sentences), ABR (validates behavioural responses, detects central problems/absent cochlear nerve, rules out non-organic hearing loss)
- **Medical:** Fitness for general anaesthesia (especially elderly); local anaesthetic CI possible for unfit adults
- **Otological:** Exclude active middle ear infection/cholesteatoma (contraindication); perforations should be closed; previous CWD mastoid requires obliteration + blind sac closure
- **Imaging:** MRI and/or CT (see Part D)
- **Psychological:** Counselling for realistic expectations; assessment of preparedness for surgery and rehabilitation

### Special Adult Groups

**The Elderly:**
- Rising average age means candidates increasingly in 70s-80s
- Perform well — similar speech perception and QoL improvement as younger recipients
- Link between hearing impairment and cognitive decline — CI may prevent cognitive decline and improve social interaction

**Far-Advanced [[Otosclerosis|Otosclerosis]] (FAO):**
- First described by House & Sheehy (1961) — otosclerosis with profound hearing loss
- CI outcomes in FAO equivalent to other aetiologies; some are best performers (otosclerosis generally spares sensory elements)
- Higher risk of non-auditory stimulation (facial nerve stimulation) — manageable by deactivating responsible electrodes
- *Cross-reference: `Otosclerosis_Comprehensive_Answer.md` for FAO pathology*

**Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2):**
- Hearing loss from bilateral [[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]]s or their treatment
- If cochlear nerve preserved during tumour excision → CI preferred over ABI (more reliable outcomes)
- Intra-operative testing with eABR or cochlear nerve action potentials determines CI suitability
- UK: 4 specialist NF2 centres with agreed protocol

**Single-Sided Deafness (SSD):**
- CI provides significant audiological benefit + [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] improvement
- Improvement particularly in speech recognition in noise
- UK: Not yet funded for SSD (cost-effectiveness not established)

**Residual Hearing / Electric-Acoustic Stimulation (EAS):**
- Modern techniques allow hearing preservation in some cases
- Combined hearing aid + CI in same ear for "ski-slope" losses
- Specially designed short electrodes (e.g., Cochlear Hybrid) — basal turn only
- Many surgeons prefer medium/standard atraumatic electrode — hearing often preserved; if lost, full frequency coverage maintained
- *Cross-reference: `Electroacoustic_Stimulation_Hearing_Preservation_CI.md` for EAS details*

## Part B: CI Candidacy Criteria — Children (Q33, Q65, Q116, Q28)

### Audiological Criteria (Children)
- ==**9e update:** Same NICE thresholds — **≥80 dB HL at 500 Hz, 1 kHz, 2 kHz, 3 kHz and 4 kHz** bilaterally; failure to benefit from hearing aids==
- Adequate benefit defined as: speech, language and listening skills appropriate to age, developmental stage and cognitive ability
- Assessment adapted for age and abilities
- ==**9e emphasis: Newborn hearing screening enables identification early; implantation at or before 12 months of age gives optimal outcomes**==

### Age at Implantation
- **Preferred age in UK:** Around **12 months**
- Emerging evidence that **6-12 months** may be optimal (safe in experienced centres)
- **Sensitive period:** ~2-3 years during which central auditory system shows greatest neural plasticity
- Earlier CI → better language development; children implanted <18 months show similar language development rate as normal hearing peers
- Children implanted after 3 years may struggle to catch up
- Universal Newborn Hearing Screening (UNHS) enables early identification of profound hearing loss
- *Cross-reference: `Auditory_Pathway_Neuropathy_Comprehensive.md` Part B for neural plasticity/critical period*

### Evaluation of a Child for CI (Q33)
1. **Audiological assessment:** PTA (conditioned), ABR, OAE, aided audiometry, speech perception testing appropriate to age
2. **Medical evaluation:** Fitness for GA; syndromic associations
3. **Otological assessment:** Middle ear status; previous surgery
4. **Imaging:** MRI (preferred initial modality in prelingually deaf infants — identifies cochlear/nerve aplasia); CT selectively to avoid radiation
5. **Developmental/psychological assessment:** Cognitive level, additional disabilities, communication needs
6. **Speech and language assessment:** Baseline skills; communication mode
7. **Educational assessment:** Teacher of the deaf input; school setting
8. **Family counselling:** Realistic expectations; commitment to rehabilitation; complex social/cultural/peer issues
9. **MDT discussion:** All findings reviewed; decision-making collaborative

### Additional Disabilities
- Initially excluded; now increasingly assessed and implanted
- Cognitive impairment, developmental delay, visual impairment, ASD
- May never develop oral communication but CI may still improve QoL
- Traditional assessment tools may be unsuitable — rely on MDT experience
- Careful parental counselling essential

### Auditory Neuropathy Spectrum Disorder (ANSD)
- Prevalence up to 10% in children with permanent hearing loss
- Severe-profound ANSD → CI effective rehabilitation
- Hearing may improve over time → observation period recommended (most children observed until at least 6 months for behavioural assessments)
- *Cross-reference: `Auditory_Pathway_Neuropathy_Comprehensive.md` Parts C-E for ANSD diagnosis/management*

### Anatomical Abnormalities
- Present in ~20% of congenital profound SNHL
- Cochlear nerve absent/hypoplastic → careful electrophysiological assessment → may need ABI instead of CI
- Associated anomalies: abnormal facial nerve position, CHARGE syndrome
- Additional surgical planning required

### Simultaneous Bilateral CI (NICE)
- Recommended for children
- Recommended for adults who are blind or with disabilities increasing reliance on auditory stimuli

## Part C: Benefits of Bilateral Cochlear Implantation (Q4)

### Binaural Hearing Advantages
Patients with bilateral CIs have improved outcomes compared to unilateral CI:

| Benefit | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sound localization** | Ability to determine direction of sound source |
| **Spatial acuity** | Enhanced spatial awareness |
| **Speech in noise** | Improved speech recognition in challenging listening environments (background noise) |
| **Head shadow effect** | Overcome by having implants on both sides |
| **Squelch effect** | Binaural processing improves signal-to-noise ratio |
| **Summation effect** | Bilateral stimulation produces louder perceived signal |

### Evidence
- Advantages improve over time, often years, following implantation
- Quality of life improved in bilateral vs unilateral recipients
- In sequentially implanted cases: relative QoL increase higher after first implant
- Bimodal hearing (CI + contralateral hearing aid) also shows advantages over unilateral CI alone

### NICE Recommendations for Bilateral CI
- **Simultaneous bilateral CI recommended for:**
  - All children
  - Adults who are blind
  - Adults with other disabilities that increase reliance on auditory stimuli as primary sensory mechanism for spatial awareness

### Sequential vs Simultaneous
- Simultaneous: Single surgery, single anaesthetic, reduced overall cost
- Sequential: Two separate procedures; allows experience with first CI before committing to second; inter-implant interval important (shorter = better)
- Manufacturers increasingly working with hearing aid companies to optimize bimodal hearing when only one CI inserted

## Part D: Imaging Assessment — HRCT and MRI (Q89)

### MRI in CI Surgery

**Indications:**
- Preferred initial imaging modality in prelingually deafened infants
- Identifies cochlear nerve aplasia/hypoplasia (critical — makes CI impossible)
- Assesses cochlear fluid signal (absent in obliterated cochlea)
- Detects retrocochlear pathology in adults (e.g., acoustic neuroma)
- Preferred modality in adults for initial assessment

**Findings to identify:**
| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Absent/hypoplastic cochlear nerve | CI contraindicated; consider ABI |
| Absent cochlear fluid signal | Cochlear obliteration (post-meningitic) |
| Inner ear malformations | Surgical planning modification |
| Vestibular schwannoma | Differential diagnosis; NF2 management |
| Cerebral pathology | Central processing assessment |

### CT (HRCT) in CI Surgery

**Indications:**
- Improves identification of bony anatomy and abnormalities
- Selective use in infants (avoid radiation to developing brain/orbits)
- Routine in some centres for adults

**Findings to identify:**
| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Cochlear ossification | Post-meningitis; may need drill-out or double-array electrode |
| Inner ear anomalies (Mondini, common cavity, IP-II) | Surgical approach modification |
| Cochlear aperture patency | Predicts cochlear nerve presence |
| Middle ear pathology | Infection/cholesteatoma must be eradicated before CI |
| Anatomical variants: low dura, anterior sigmoid sinus | Surgical planning |
| Temporal bone pneumatization | Mastoidectomy planning |
| Facial nerve course | Identify aberrant course; avoid injury |

### Complementary Roles
- **MRI → soft tissue detail:** nerve, fluid, tumours, brain
- **CT → bony detail:** ossification, anatomy, middle ear disease
- Both MRI and CT may be helpful in meningitic deafness (predict ossification degree)
- CT may be done if MRI shows abnormality requiring bony delineation

### MRI Compatibility Post-CI
- Latest generation devices compatible with 1.5T MRI without magnet removal
- MED-EL rotating magnet: allows 3T MRI with magnet in situ
- Stronger fields may need magnet removal — newer designs reducing this need
- Small area of artefact around receiver-stimulator

## Part E: Types of Middle Ear Implant (Q65)

### Classification of Active Middle Ear Implants (AMEIs)

| Type | Transduction | Implantability | Device Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electromagnetic | Coil & magnet; FMT vibrates | Semi-implantable | **Vibrant Soundbridge (VSB)** (MED-EL) |
| Electromagnetic | Probe drives incus body | Semi- / Fully implantable | **MET / Carina** (Otologics→Cochlear) |
| Piezoelectric | Voltage-dependent ceramic deformation | Fully implantable | **Envoy Esteem** |
| Electromagnetic | Direct acoustic to inner ear | Semi-implantable | **DACI/Codacs** (Cochlear) |

### Key Devices

**Vibrant Soundbridge (VSB):**
- Most widely implanted AMEI worldwide
- FMT coupled to incus long process (classic), short process, stapes head, footplate or round window
- CE marked for SNHL (1998), conductive/mixed loss in adults (2008) and children (2009)
- Approach: transmastoid posterior tympanotomy

**MET/Carina (Cochlear):**
- MET: semi-implantable; electromagnetic probe coupled to incus body via atticotomy
- Carina: fully implantable version with subcutaneous microphone and rechargeable battery
- Various coupling sites: incus, round window, footplate
- Device failure rate 17.6% (mostly charging-related)

**Envoy Esteem:**
- Fully implantable piezoelectric device
- Sensor on incus body (internal microphone) + driver cemented to stapes head
- Requires disarticulation of ossicular chain (removal of lenticular process)
- FDA approved 2010; CE marked 2006
- Complications: facial weakness 8%, taste disturbance 30%, explantation/revision 13%

### Vibroplasty Classification (Beleites)
| Type | Coupling | Indication |
|---|---|---|
| A | AMEI on intact ossicular chain | Mild-moderate SNHL |
| B | AMEI + ossicular remnant (stapes/footplate) | Mixed HL |
| C | AMEI on round window membrane | Conductive/mixed HL |
| D | AMEI directly to inner ear fluid (oval window) | Profound mixed HL |

### MEI vs CI vs BCD Candidacy
- MEI: Moderate-severe SNHL failing conventional hearing aids; or mixed HL with failed hearing aid/stapedotomy/BCI
- CI: Severe-profound SNHL (>90 dB HL at 2 & 4 kHz) with <50% BKB score
- BCD: Conductive/mixed (BC thresholds <45 dB percutaneous, <25 dB passive transcutaneous), SSD, atresia
- *Cross-reference: `Bone_Conduction_MEI_Devices_Comprehensive.md` for BCD details*

## Part F: Complications of CI Surgery (Q116)

### Classification
- **Major:** Requires major surgical intervention or results in permanent disability
- **Minor:** Manageable by medical measures or minor procedure

### Complication Rates
- **Paediatric (Bhatia et al., 300 cases):** Major 2.3%, Minor 16%
- **UK Paediatric Bilateral Audit (1397 bilateral CIs):** Major 0.9%, Minor 6.5% (excluding device failure)

### Specific Complications

**Vertigo:**
- ~30% of adult recipients experience transient dizziness/vertigo
- [[BPPV|BPPV]]: More frequent post-CI than general population; Dix-Hallpike positive from implanted ear; standard BPPV treatment effective
- Patients >70 years more prone to permanent vestibular injury
- Lower incidence in paediatric population
- Bilateral CI: risk of bilateral vestibular hypofunction — vestibular assessment may guide ear selection

**Device Failure:**
- Not strictly a surgical complication; device failure rate ~3.7-3.8%
- 50% non-functioning electrodes = device failure
- Trauma-linked; sudden or insidious ("soft failure")
- Reimplantation safe and effective; variable post-reimplantation performance
- Paediatric recipients likely to need multiple implant surgeries in lifetime

**Facial Nerve:**
- Immediate post-operative palsy due to direct/thermal trauma; facial nerve monitoring commonly used
- Gibbin et al. (1524 patients): 4 partial palsies (2 complete recovery), 1 complete palsy (partial recovery)
- Permanent disability rate: **0.2%**
- UK paediatric audit: 2 temporary partial palsies (0.2%), no permanent palsies
- **Facial nerve stimulation:** 7-25% of cases; more frequent in otosclerosis; manageable by deactivating responsible electrodes

**CSF Gusher:**
- Occurs in abnormal inner ears (X-linked deafness most common); lesser leaks in LVAS
- Management: raise patient's head; insert electrode when flow slows; pack muscle/soft tissue around insertion site; lumbar drain if persistent

**Electrode Tip Fold-Over:**
- May involve few electrodes or significant portion of array
- May not be recognized at surgery; detected on post-operative imaging
- Options: leave if minor; revision — remove and reinsert (confirm with intra-operative imaging); use second device if non-functional

**Magnet Displacement:**
- Trauma over implant can displace magnet from silicone pocket
- External component fails to connect; palpable magnet in abnormal position
- X-ray confirms diagnosis
- Requires revision surgery

**Wound Complications:**
- Infection, haematoma, wound breakdown, flap necrosis
- Meticulous surgical technique: avoid incision directly over receiver-stimulator

**Meningitis:**
- Risk increased with active middle ear infection/cholesteatoma at time of CI
- Infection must be eradicated before implantation
- Cochlear abnormalities (common cavity) may increase risk

**Cochlear Ossification (Challenging Cases):**
- Post-meningitis most common; ranges from small basal turn bone to complete obliteration
- Partial ossification: drill through, identify duct distally; partial insertion or dual-array electrode
- Complete ossification: may need ABI instead
- Imaging may not fully predict degree of ossification (delay between imaging and surgery may allow progression)

## Part G: 🆕 Future Advances in Cochlear Implantation (Scott-Brown 9e — New Section)

> [!example] 🆕 New in Scott-Brown 9th Edition (Ch 38 Key Messages)
> Alongside continually improving electrode design, processing strategies and surgical techniques, future advances in cochlear implantation include:
>
> - **Totally implantable device** — no external processor or coil; internal microphone + rechargeable battery
> - **Improved neural stimulation strategies** — current focusing, virtual channels, closed-loop stimulation using cochlear feedback
> - **Increasing use of robots** — robotic-assisted electrode insertion for millimetre-precision, controlled-speed insertion; reduces insertion trauma and helps hearing preservation
> - **Routine use of drug-eluting electrodes** — dexamethasone-eluting arrays to reduce intracochlear fibrosis and preserve residual hearing
> - **Inner-ear treatment with gene or stem cell therapies** — regeneration of hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons; combined therapy alongside implantation
>
> **Unmet need highlighted:** One of the greatest challenges in CI remains the substantial unmet need in both developed and developing countries — cost, access to MDT, and long-term rehabilitation infrastructure remain barriers even where the surgery itself is available.

### 9e Key Messages Summary (Broomfield & Pai, 2026)
1. CI has transformed management of severe-to-profound hearing loss
2. Candidacy criteria differ between countries and are constantly evolving
3. Modern criteria increasingly include residual hearing, SSD, complex needs, anatomical abnormalities
4. Surgery is usually via cortical mastoidectomy + posterior tympanotomy; hearing preservation techniques increasingly used; serious complications rare
5. Newborn hearing screening + implantation ≤ 12 months = optimal outcomes for congenital SNHL
6. Unmet need remains the greatest global challenge
