# NIHL and Noise Pollution

## Questions Covered
- **Q36 (2021):** Define noise pollution. What are the effects of noise pollution? Describe its prevention.
- **Q68 (2022):** Define NIHL. What are clinical features, diagnostic tests, guidelines for prevention of NIHL and management of this problem?
- **Q125 (2025):** Describe the auditory effects of noise pollution. Discuss the Hearing conservation program for occupational noise-induced hearing loss. What is 60 percent rule?

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology 8th Edition, Volume 2 — Chapter 53 (Prevention of Hearing Loss) & Chapter 57 (Noise-Induced Hearing Loss and Related Conditions)

---

## Part A: Definitions (Q36, Q68)

### Noise Pollution
Noise pollution is defined as unwanted or harmful sound in the environment that causes discomfort, disturbance or damage to health. Sources include industrial, environmental (traffic, construction), and recreational (personal music devices, clubs, concerts, firearms, motor sports).

### Noise-Induced Hearing Loss (NIHL)
NIHL refers to a reduction in auditory acuity as a consequence of excessive noise exposure. It may be:
- **Temporary Threshold Shift (TTS):** Reversible hearing loss following noise exposure
- **Permanent Threshold Shift (PTS):** Irreversible hearing loss from repeated or prolonged exposure
- **Acoustic Trauma:** Immediate hearing loss from a single exposure to intense sound

### Noise Measurement
- **Decibels (dB):** Logarithmic scale; A-weighting [dB(A)] approximates human hearing sensitivity
- **Noise Immission Level (NIL):** NIL = Lepd + 10 log10 T (where Lepd = daily noise exposure in dB(A), T = exposure in years)
- **Equal Energy Principle (Burns & Robinson):** If 85 dB(A) is safe for 8 hours, then 88 dB(A) (doubling intensity) is safe for only 4 hours

### Permissible Exposure Limits
| Exposure Level | Action Required |
|---|---|
| <80 dB(A) | Unlikely to cause hearing damage regardless of duration |
| 80 dB(A) — First action level | Noise survey, employee education, provide hearing protection (voluntary use) |
| 85 dB(A) — Second action level | Hearing protection compulsory; regular audiometric monitoring |
| 130 dB(A) | Causes damage after even short exposure in virtually all individuals |
| 140 dB — Peak action level | Immediate risk from impulse noise |

*UK Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005 (replaced 1989 regulations; each action level 5 dB lower than previous)*

## Part B: Effects of Noise Pollution (Q36, Q125)

### Auditory Effects
1. **Temporary Threshold Shift (TTS):** Reversible; implies metabolic cochlear mechanisms; recovery possible if exposure ceases
2. **Permanent Threshold Shift (PTS):** Irreversible; implies structural damage; progressive with continued exposure
3. **Acoustic Trauma:** Single-event intense exposure causing immediate hearing loss; may rupture TM and disrupt ossicles in blast injuries
4. **[[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]]:** Present in approximately 50% of NIHL cases; may occur as earliest symptom
5. **Hyperacusis:** Found in 40% of tinnitus sufferers; increasingly recognized accompanying symptom

### Pathophysiology of NIHL
The cochlea is the predominant site of pathological damage:

**Metabolic mechanisms:**
- Excessive glutamate release causing excitotoxicity at IHC-afferent nerve synapse
- Reduced cochlear blood flow with high-intensity noise
- Free radical formation — reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS) trigger cell death pathways

**Structural mechanisms:**
- Depolymerization of actin filaments in stereocilia
- Swelling of stria vascularis, afferent nerve endings and supporting cells
- Outer hair cells (OHC) are particularly vulnerable

**Apoptosis and necrosis:**
- Both play a role in NIHL; OHC death continues after cessation of noise (implicating apoptotic mechanisms)
- Caspases (cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases) involved in cochlear hair cell apoptosis

**Synaptopathy ("Hidden Hearing Loss"):**
- Synaptic connections between IHCs and spiral ganglion cells are especially vulnerable
- May occur even when hair cells remain intact; audiometric thresholds unchanged but difficulty hearing in background noise

### Non-Auditory Effects
| System | Effects |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension, ischemic heart disease |
| Psychological | Annoyance, sleep disturbance, stress, anxiety, depression |
| Cognitive | Impaired concentration, learning difficulties |
| Communication | Speech interference, social isolation |
| Social | Withdrawal, marital stress, loss of confidence |
| Occupational | Reduced work performance, absenteeism |

### Predisposing Factors
- Genetic susceptibility (Ahl gene in mice; humans — still elusive)
- Interaction with age-related hearing loss (adjacent frequency thresholds worsen faster)
- Smoking, diabetes, cardiovascular disease
- Blue eye colour — greater TTS and PTS
- Ototoxic agents (synergistic with noise): aminoglycosides, cisplatin, industrial solvents (toluene)

## Part C: Clinical Features and Diagnosis of NIHL (Q68)

### Clinical Features
- **Demographics:** Far more common in males; usually middle-aged
- **Early symptoms:** Difficulty hearing in background noise; lack of clarity rather than loss of volume
- **Progression:** Television volume increased; telephone difficulty; frequent requests to repeat; social withdrawal
- **Tinnitus:** Common early accompanying symptom; post-exposure tinnitus useful diagnostic surrogate for TTS
- **Hyperacusis:** Found in 40% of tinnitus sufferers with NIHL
- **Bilateral and symmetrical** hearing loss (asymmetry uncommon except in military — head shadow effect with shoulder-borne weapons; >10 dB asymmetry at same frequency requires MRI to exclude [[Vestibular Schwannoma|acoustic neuroma]])

### Diagnostic Tests

**Pure Tone Audiometry (PTA):**
- Cornerstone of investigation
- Classical pattern: High-frequency SNHL with notch at 4 kHz or 6 kHz, with recovery at 8 kHz ("boiler-maker's notch")
- Test frequencies: standard + 3 kHz and 6 kHz
- Loss below 2 kHz is extremely uncommon — suggests other cochlear pathology
- The classic notch is often absent, especially with concurrent age-related hearing loss
- The 4 kHz notch alone is NOT diagnostic of NIHL (found in populations with no noise exposure)

**Coles, Lutman and Buffin (CLB) Guidelines:**
- Widely used since 2007 for medicolegal diagnosis of NIHL
- Probability test analyzing audiogram shape to determine likelihood of NIHL
- Non-conformity does not exclude diagnosis

**Tympanometry:**
- Confirms normal middle ear function
- Conductive hearing loss is not due to NIHL (may even provide some protection)

**Other investigations:**
- Otoacoustic emissions (OAE): Reduced DPOAEs and decreased contralateral suppression with early noise damage; not yet reliable for routine clinical use
- Cortical evoked response audiometry: For suspected non-organic hearing loss
- MRI: If significant asymmetry (>10 dB at same frequency)
- Speech audiometry: Difficulty with discrimination in noise

### Diagnostic Framework (Three Components)
Any hearing loss in suspected NIHL comprises three parts:
1. **Age-related component** (removed using NPL tables/ISO 1999)
2. **Noise-induced component** (attributed based on exposure and audiometric pattern)
3. **Idiopathic degenerative component** (remainder)

## Part D: Prevention and Hearing Conservation Program (Q36, Q68, Q125)

### Three-Pronged Approach to Prevention

**1. Engineering Controls (Reduction at Source):**
- Quieter machinery design and maintenance
- Sound isolation and acoustic enclosures
- Vibration dampening
- Modification of industrial processes

**2. Administrative Controls:**
- Noise surveys and mapping
- Limiting exposure time (rotation of workers)
- Employee education and awareness programs
- Regular audiometric monitoring (mandatory in hazardous industries)
- Maintaining noise exposure records
- Warning signage in noisy areas

**3. Personal Hearing Protection (Last Resort):**

| Type | Attenuation | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Earplugs (foam/silicone/acrylic) | ~10-15 dB (real-world) | Cheap; useful with helmets/goggles; harder to fit correctly |
| Earmuffs | ≥15 dB (real-world) | More reliable; easier proper fit; generally preferred |
| Active Noise Reduction (ANR) | Very effective at low frequencies (~1000 Hz) | Electronic; expensive; military/aircraft use; 180° out-of-phase sound |

*Real-world performance significantly poorer than laboratory scores due to incorrect fitting.*

### Components of a Hearing Conservation Program (Q125)
1. **Noise survey:** Assessment and mapping of noise levels in workplace
2. **Audiometric monitoring:** Baseline and regular (annual) audiometry for all exposed workers; early identification of TTS
3. **Hearing protection:** Provision, selection, training in use and maintenance
4. **Education and training:** Awareness of noise hazards, proper use of protection
5. **Engineering and administrative controls:** Primary prevention measures
6. **Record keeping:** Documentation of noise levels, exposure, audiometry results
7. **Medical referral:** For workers showing threshold changes

### UK Regulatory Framework
- Health and Safety at Work Act 1974 — statutory duty to minimize risks
- Noise at Work Regulations 1989 (replaced by Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005)
- European directive: Action levels 5 dB lower than previous regulations
- Prescribed occupational disease since 1974 — DSS compensation for >10 years' exposure

## Part E: The 60 Percent Rule / 60-60 Rule (Q125)

The **60/60 Rule** is a guideline for safe use of personal music devices:
- Listen at no more than **60% of maximum volume**
- For no more than **60 minutes** at a time

### Rationale
- Personal music players can produce outputs exceeding 100 dB(A) at maximum volume
- At 60% volume, most devices produce approximately 80-85 dB(A) — within safe limits
- Limiting duration to 60 minutes allows recovery from any TTS
- Particularly important for younger populations who are regular users of earphones/headphones

### WHO Safe Listening Guidelines
- WHO estimates that 1.1 billion young people worldwide are at risk from unsafe listening practices
- Recommended safe listening limits: 85 dB(A) for 8 hours; 100 dB(A) for 15 minutes
- WHO recommends noise-limiting features in personal audio devices
- Regular hearing check-ups recommended for regular users

## Part F: Management of NIHL (Q68)

### Key Principle
**There is no cure for established NIHL.** Once PTS has occurred, hair cell loss is irreversible. Management is aimed at prevention of further damage and rehabilitation.

### Non-Specific Management (Mild High-Tone Loss)
- Optimize acoustic environment: reduce background noise, face-to-face conversation
- Explanation of the problem — legitimization of hearing loss
- Practical measures: infrared TV headphones, volume-controllable telephones, louder doorbells with flashing lights/vibrating pagers
- Lip-reading classes
- Rehabilitation programs including psychological counselling; include partner/spouse
- Address social withdrawal, loss of confidence, depression

### Specific Management (More Severe Loss)
- **Hearing aids:** Binaural digital hearing aids provide significant benefit; follow-up and rehabilitative support essential for maximum benefit and compliance
- **Tinnitus management:** Modern neurophysiological methods (tinnitus retraining therapy: cognitive counselling + sound therapy including hearing aids/white-noise generators) report 60-70% improvement rates
- **Hyperacusis management:** Responds well to similar treatment strategies as tinnitus
- **Acoustic shock treatment:** Primarily psychological; TRT helpful for associated tinnitus

### Medicolegal Aspects
**Disability calculation:**
- DSS/Industrial Injuries: Based on average of 1, 2 and 3 kHz: [(4 x better ear + 1 x worse ear) / 5]
- Entry point: 50 dB HL = 20% disability
- 100% disability (complete deafness) — full compensation
- Must demonstrate: excessive noise exposure, consequent hearing loss, foreseeable risk, claim within 3 years of awareness

*Cross-reference: `Audiometric_Testing_Comprehensive.md` Part H for detailed hearing disability percentage calculation and WHO classification*

### Future Directions
- Antioxidant therapy: N-acetylcysteine, glutathione, alpha lipoic acid (mainly animal studies)
- Sound conditioning (protection against future noise exposure)
- Hair cell regeneration research
- Gene therapy
- Understanding and managing synaptopathy ("hidden hearing loss")
