## INTRODUCTION

Neonatal hearing screening represents one of the most significant advances in paediatric audiology, enabling early identification and intervention for permanent childhood hearing impairment (PCHI). At least 1 in 1000 children is born with permanent bilateral hearing impairment of 40 dB HL or greater. The rationale for early detection is compelling: if PCHI is identified before 6 months of age and habilitation started soon thereafter, the adverse effects on speech, language, and cognitive development are significantly lessened.

The two primary screening technologies are otoacoustic emissions (OAEs) and automated auditory brainstem response (AABR). Understanding the physiological basis of these tests, particularly OAEs and cochlear microphonics, is essential for effective screening programme implementation.

## PART A: OTOACOUSTIC EMISSIONS (OAEs)

### Definition and Physiological Basis

Otoacoustic emissions are low-intensity sounds generated by the active movement of the outer hair cells (OHCs) of the cochlea. They represent acoustic energy that is emitted from the cochlea back through the middle ear into the external auditory canal, where they can be recorded using sensitive microphone systems.

The physiological mechanism underlying OAEs involves the cochlear amplification process:

1. Sound-induced travelling waves along the basilar membrane stimulate outer hair cells
2. OHCs exhibit unique fast reversible axial length changes (electromotility) driven by the motor protein **prestin**
3. This active process amplifies and fine-tunes the signal before it reaches the inner hair cells
4. The excess energy released by this cochlear amplification generates sound waves emitted as OAEs

This phenomenon was predicted by Thomas Gold in 1948 and demonstrated by David Kemp in 1978. OAEs provide an objective measure of the "health" of the outer hair cells and can be recorded from probe microphones fitted in the external ear canal.

### Characteristics of OAEs

- Present in ears with normal cochlear function
- Absent if there is damage to the outer hair cells
- Can be affected by middle ear pathology (fluid/debris)
- Do not require neural pathway integrity
- Provide frequency-specific information about cochlear function
- Non-invasive and quick to perform

### Types of Otoacoustic Emissions

#### 1. Spontaneous Otoacoustic Emissions (SOAEs)

**Definition:** Low-level sounds emitted from the cochlea in the absence of any external acoustic stimulation.

**Characteristics:**
- Present in approximately 40-60% of normal-hearing individuals
- More common in females than males
- More common in right ears than left ears
- Typically narrow-band signals at specific frequencies
- Clinical utility is limited due to variable presence

#### 2. Transient Evoked Otoacoustic Emissions (TEOAEs)

**Definition:** Emissions evoked by brief acoustic stimuli such as clicks or tone bursts.

**Stimulus characteristics:**
- Broadband click stimuli at 80-86 dB SPL
- Responses to typically 260 stimuli are averaged
- Time frame: first 20 ms after stimulus application

**Clinical features:**
- Provide broadband assessment of cochlear function
- Frequency range typically 1-4 kHz
- Present when hearing thresholds are better than 30-40 dB HL
- Most commonly used for neonatal screening (as AOAE - Automated OAE)
- Quick to perform (typically 2-5 minutes per ear)
- Low consumable costs

#### 3. Distortion Product Otoacoustic Emissions (DPOAEs)

**Definition:** Emissions generated when two pure tones of different frequencies (f1 and f2) are presented simultaneously.

**Mechanism:**
- Non-linear interaction of the two primary tones in the cochlea
- Most prominent distortion product is at frequency 2f1-f2
- Optimal f2/f1 ratio is approximately 1.22

**Clinical features:**
- Frequency-specific assessment possible
- Can test wider frequency range (up to 8 kHz)
- Better high-frequency assessment than TEOAEs
- Useful for monitoring ototoxicity
- Takes longer than TEOAE testing

#### 4. Stimulus Frequency Otoacoustic Emissions (SFOAEs)

**Definition:** Emissions evoked at the same frequency as a continuous pure-tone stimulus.

**Characteristics:**
- Technically more difficult to record
- Requires separation of emission from stimulus
- Primarily used in research settings
- Limited clinical application currently

### Comparison of OAE Types

**TEOAEs:**
- Broadband click stimulus
- Quick screening tool
- Frequency range 1-4 kHz
- Best for screening purposes

**DPOAEs:**
- Two-tone stimulus
- Frequency-specific
- Extended frequency range
- Better for diagnostic and monitoring purposes

## PART B: COCHLEAR MICROPHONICS

### Definition

Cochlear microphonics (CM) are electrical potentials generated in the cochlea that mimic the acoustic properties of the stimulus. They are an electrophysiological response generated primarily from the outer hair cells to acoustic signals.

### Characteristics

- Start at near-zero latency (appear immediately after stimulus)
- Represent early components of cochlear response
- Generated primarily by outer hair cells
- Invert in polarity when stimulus phase is reversed (rarefaction to condensation)
- No latency shift with intensity decrease
- Small in amplitude and susceptible to electroacoustic artefact

### Recording Technique

Cochlear microphonics are incorporated into auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing software. To evaluate for CM:

1. ABR is evoked using rarefaction and condensation clicks at suprathreshold levels
2. CM appears as peaks that **invert in amplitude** when stimulus polarity is switched
3. Observation of this inversion is consistent with detection of CM

### Clinical Significance

**In Auditory Neuropathy Spectrum Disorder (ANSD):**

CM testing is critical for diagnosing ANSD, which is characterized by:
- Normal to raised pure-tone thresholds
- Poor speech discrimination
- Delayed or absent ABRs
- **Normal OAEs and cochlear microphonics**

The presence of CM with absent ABR indicates:
- Normal preneural (outer hair cell) function
- Disruption at inner hair cells, synapses, or auditory nerve

### Pitfalls in CM Recording

**Stimulus artefact confusion:**
- Artefact arises from the transducer, not the cochlea
- Shows similar morphology to CM
- Also inverts with polarity change
- Risk increases at stimulus levels above 80 dB nHL
- Tube insert earphones with longer tubing can separate artefact from CM in time

### Comparison: CM, Compound Action Potential, and OAE

| Feature | Cochlear Microphonic | Compound Action Potential | OAE |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------------|-----|
| Source | Primarily OHCs | Auditory nerve | OHCs |
| Measurement | Electrical recording | Electrical recording | Acoustic recording |
| Polarity inversion | Yes, follows stimulus | No | N/A |
| Latency shift | No | Yes, with intensity | N/A |

## PART C: NEONATAL HEARING SCREENING

### Rationale for Newborn Screening

**Prevalence:**
- At least 1 in 1000 children born with bilateral PCHI ≥40 dB HL
- Prevalence increases through childhood (progressive/acquired losses)

**Consequences of late identification:**
- Compromised communication skills
- Literacy difficulties
- Behavioural problems
- Reduced educational achievement
- Mental health issues
- Impaired family dynamics
- Reduced quality of life

**Benefits of early identification:**
- Confirmation of PCHI by 9 months associated with better receptive language
- Earlier fitting of hearing aids
- Earlier [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] referral
- Informed communication methodology choices
- Timely family support

### Current Screening Tests

#### 1. Automated Otoacoustic Emissions (AOAE)

**Technology:**
- Portable hand-held screening instruments
- Automated detection algorithms
- Uses transient or distortion product emissions
- No staff judgement required for pass/refer decision

**Advantages:**
- Quick (typically 2-5 minutes)
- Minimally invasive
- Low consumable costs
- High sensitivity for cochlear dysfunction

**Disadvantages:**
- Affected by outer/middle ear debris or fluid
- Will miss auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder
- Cannot assess neural pathway integrity

#### 2. Automated Auditory Brainstem Response (AABR)

**Technology:**
- Measures neural response from brainstem
- Automated detection algorithms
- Electrodes placed on baby's head

**Advantages:**
- Assesses entire auditory pathway including neural component
- Detects auditory neuropathy
- Not affected by middle ear debris as much

**Disadvantages:**
- More expensive consumables
- Takes longer than AOAE
- Requires electrode placement

### Screening Protocols

#### Well Baby Protocol (Two-stage, Two-technology)

**Stage 1:** Transient AOAE screen (maximum 2 attempts)
- Pass in both ears = Overall PASS
- Refer = Proceed to Stage 2

**Stage 2:** AABR screen
- Pass in both ears = Overall PASS
- Refer in one or both ears = Audiological assessment referral

#### NICU/SCBU Protocol (>48 hours admission)

Both AOAE and AABR screens are performed because:
- Higher prevalence of PCHI in NICU population
- Auditory neuropathy predominantly found in NICU babies
- AOAE alone would miss ANSD cases

**Outcomes:**
- Fail AABR (one or both ears) = Audiological referral
- Pass AABR, Fail AOAE (both ears) = Risk factor surveillance at 8 months

## PART D: UNIVERSAL vs TARGETED SCREENING

### Targeted (High-Risk) Screening

**Definition:** Screening only babies with identified risk factors (approximately 10% of birth cohort).

**Risk factors include:**
- NICU admission >48 hours
- Family history of childhood hearing loss
- Craniofacial anomalies (e.g., cleft palate)
- Syndromes associated with hearing loss
- Congenital infections (CMV, rubella, toxoplasmosis)
- Hyperbilirubinaemia requiring exchange transfusion
- Ototoxic medications
- Mechanical ventilation >5 days

**Advantages:**
- Lower initial cost
- Smaller population to screen
- Resources concentrated on high-risk group

**Limitations:**
- 40-50% of babies with PCHI have NO risk factors
- Maximum 60% of true cases identified
- Family history difficult to implement effectively
- Higher loss to follow-up
- Poorer screening coverage
- Lower positive predictive value

### Universal Newborn Hearing Screening (UNHS)

**Definition:** Screening all newborn babies regardless of risk factor status.

**Advantages:**
- Identifies ALL congenital cases (not just 60%)
- Better coverage rates (>98%)
- Earlier median age of identification (~7 weeks)
- Lower loss to follow-up rates
- Higher positive predictive value
- Cost-effective in long-term
- Parental preference (80-90% want it)
- Avoids parental anxiety from delayed detection

**Performance metrics (English NHSP):**
- Coverage: 98.1% by 4 weeks corrected age
- Refer rate: 2.5% (0.5% bilateral)
- Yield: 1.01/1000 for bilateral PCHI
- Median age of identification: ~7 weeks

### Comparison Table

| Parameter | Universal Screening | Targeted Screening |
|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Population screened | All babies (100%) | High-risk only (~10%) |
| Cases identified | ~100% | ~50-60% |
| Coverage achievable | >98% | Variable |
| Cost per case found | Lower long-term | Higher |
| Loss to follow-up | Lower | Higher |
| Parental acceptance | High (>99%) | N/A |

## PART E: THE 1-3-6 RULE

### Definition

The 1-3-6 rule represents the recommended timeline benchmarks for early hearing detection and intervention (EHDI) programmes:

**1 Month:** Hearing screening completed
**3 Months:** Audiological diagnosis confirmed
**6 Months:** Intervention initiated (hearing aids fitted, early intervention started)

### Rationale

Research evidence demonstrates that identification of PCHI before 6 months of age and early intervention leads to:
- Better receptive language scores at age 8 years
- Better speech outcomes
- Improved educational achievement
- Better psychosocial development

### Components of the Timeline

**By 1 Month - Screening:**
- All babies screened before hospital discharge or within first month
- Rescreening of initial refers completed
- Referral to audiology for screen-positive cases

**By 3 Months - Diagnosis:**
- Comprehensive audiological evaluation completed
- Type, degree, and configuration of hearing loss determined
- Medical evaluation for aetiology
- Communication with family about findings

**By 6 Months - Intervention:**
- Hearing technology fitted (hearing aids, [[Bone Conduction MEI Devices|BAHA]], cochlear implant evaluation)
- Enrolment in early intervention programme
- Family support and counselling initiated
- Communication methodology decisions supported

### Current Performance

**English NHSP data:**
- 87% of screen referrals enter follow-up by 4 weeks
- 95% enter follow-up by 6 months
- Median age of identification: approximately 7 weeks

## PART F: AUDIOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT OF NEWBORNS

### Electrophysiological Tests

#### 1. Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR)

**Purpose:** Threshold estimation and neural pathway assessment

**Technique:**
- Click or tone-burst stimuli
- Electrodes on forehead and mastoids
- Can be performed during natural sleep in first 6 months

**Applications:**
- Threshold estimation
- ANSD diagnosis (with CM evaluation)
- Neural pathway integrity assessment

#### 2. Auditory Steady-State Response (ASSR)

**Advantages:**
- Frequency-specific thresholds
- Can test at higher intensities than ABR
- Useful for severe-profound losses

**Limitations:**
- Affected by state of arousal
- May be problematic in difficult-to-test populations

### Objective Cochlear Tests

#### 1. Otoacoustic Emissions

- As described above
- Confirms OHC function
- Combined with ABR for comprehensive assessment

#### 2. Acoustic Reflexes

- Middle ear muscle reflex testing
- Absent in conductive loss and ANSD
- Useful adjunct to OAE and ABR

### Behavioural Tests

#### Behavioural Observation Audiometry (BOA)

**Age range:** 0-6 months

**Technique:**
- Observe behavioural responses to calibrated sounds
- Eye widening, startle, arousal changes
- Cessation or changes in activity

**Limitations:**
- Subjective interpretation
- Cannot determine thresholds accurately
- High false-negative rate

#### Visual Reinforcement Audiometry (VRA)

**Age range:** 6-30 months (can sit with support, typically 5-7 months onwards)

**Technique:**
- Sound presented, head turn reinforced by visual reward
- Can obtain ear-specific and frequency-specific information
- More reliable than BOA

### Assessment Protocol for Screen Referrals

1. **History:** Risk factors, developmental milestones, family history
2. **Otoscopy:** Middle ear status
3. **Tympanometry:** Middle ear function (age-appropriate probe tone)
4. **OAE:** Cochlear (OHC) function
5. **ABR with CM:** Neural pathway and preneural function
6. **Behavioural testing:** Age-appropriate (BOA, VRA)

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. OAEs are low-intensity sounds generated by outer hair cells via electromotility driven by prestin protein
> 2. Four types of OAE: SOAE (spontaneous), TEOAE (transient evoked), DPOAE (distortion product), SFOAE (stimulus frequency)
> 3. TEOAEs use broadband click, DPOAEs use two-tone stimulus (2f1-f2)
> 4. Cochlear microphonics are electrical potentials from OHCs that invert with stimulus polarity change
> 5. ANSD diagnosis requires: normal OAE/CM + absent/abnormal ABR
> 6. Universal screening identifies 100% of cases vs 50-60% for targeted screening
> 7. 40-50% of babies with PCHI have NO risk factors - major limitation of targeted screening
> 8. NICU babies need both AOAE and AABR due to higher ANSD prevalence
> 9. 1-3-6 Rule: Screen by 1 month, diagnose by 3 months, intervene by 6 months
> 10. Coverage of English NHSP exceeds 98% with median identification age of 7 weeks
> 11. OAEs are absent with OHC damage but can be affected by middle ear pathology
> 12. OAEs remain present after auditory nerve section - they reflect cochlear, not neural, function


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. OAEs test the "cochlear amplifier" (OHCs) - they do not assess the neural pathway; ABR is needed for complete assessment
> 2. A baby passing OAE but failing AABR should raise suspicion of auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder
> 3. Middle ear fluid/debris can cause OAE failure even with normal cochlear function - always check middle ear status
> 4. The 1-3-6 rule has been updated to 1-2-3 in some programmes (screen by 1 month, diagnose by 2 months, intervene by 3 months)
> 5. Cochlear microphonic inversion with polarity change distinguishes it from stimulus artefact - but artefact also inverts, so latency and morphology must be considered
> 6. NICU graduates need both OAE and ABR because OAE alone misses auditory neuropathy, which is more common in this population
