## INTRODUCTION

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is an otological emergency for which definitive aetiology and treatment remains controversial, but prompt recognition and management have been shown to improve hearing outcomes and quality of life. The estimated annual incidence is 5-30 per 100,000 persons and 99% of cases are unilateral.

SSNHL often affects healthy individuals and is a frightening symptom that prompts the individual to seek urgent medical attention. There is a wide age distribution with an average of 50-60 years and no sex preference. The aetiology is identified in less than 5-10% of cases, with the vast majority being idiopathic. Early recognition and prompt management may improve hearing recovery.

---

## PART A: DEFINITIONS

### Sudden Hearing Loss

Sudden hearing loss is defined as the rapid onset, occurring over a 72-hour period, of a subjective sensation of hearing impairment in one or both ears.

### Audiometric Criteria

A hearing loss of **30 dB or more**, over **at least three contiguous audiometric frequencies**, that develops over **72 hours or less**. The hearing loss is defined in relation to the contralateral side as premorbid audiometry is rarely available.

### Idiopathic Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (ISSNHL)

ISSNHL is defined as SSNHL with **no identifiable cause** in spite of thorough investigation.

### Levels of Certainty About "Newness" of Hearing Loss

- **Very certain:** Patient had previous audiometric evaluation
- **Certain:** No prior otologic history, premorbid hearing felt normal bilaterally
- **Fairly certain:** Long-standing hearing problem, current episode subjectively poorer
- **Uncertain:** Clinician suspects pre-existing loss but never documented

## PART B: AETIOLOGY

### Overview

Several factors have been postulated as central to the aetiology of ISSNHL. Possible causes include labyrinthine viral infection, vascular insult, intracochlear membrane rupture, and autoimmune inner-ear disease.

### 1. Viral Aetiology

Viruses, particularly the herpes family, can cause SNHL in acute infection and reactivation of latent virus could cause SSNHL.

**Evidence supporting viral cause:**

- Serological studies show seroconversion (27-63% of patients) for CMV, influenza B, mumps, rubella, varicella zoster, EBV, HSV, enterovirus
- Temporal bone histopathology shows greater loss of neurons in apical spiral ganglion compared to basal region without corresponding loss of hair cells - pattern consistent with viral damage
- Animal studies demonstrate viral penetration of inner ear
- Isolation of virus and viral antigens in perilymph of affected patients

**Evidence against viral cause:**

- Large Taiwanese study showed SSNHL is rare following herpes zoster attack
- Risk of SSNHL not increased in recent herpes zoster compared to matched population
- Epidemiological, serological and histopathological evidence is inconclusive

### 2. Vascular Aetiology

The cochlea is supplied by an end artery and hence susceptible to damage by vascular occlusion, making a vascular cause a popular theory.

**Evidence supporting vascular cause:**

- Some studies show association with prothrombotic genetic polymorphisms
- Patients with cardiovascular disease have higher thrombophilic allelic mutations
- Sickle-cell anaemia and Waldenstrom macroglobulinaemia associated with higher SSNHL risk (usually reversible)
- Strokes involving anterior inferior cerebellar artery associated with auditory and vestibular symptoms
- Risk of stroke higher in SSNHL population
- SSNHL reported following cardiopulmonary bypass

**Evidence against vascular cause:**

- Patients with ISSNHL are no more likely than general population to have hyperviscosity disorder
- Multiple haemostatic parameters not of decisive importance in pathogenesis
- Animal studies inducing vascular hearing loss show cochlear fibrosis and ossification - not seen in SSNHL temporal bones

### 3. Intracochlear Membrane Rupture

Rupture of intracochlear membrane was proposed as a cause but evidence is only coincidental. Temporal bone studies of SSNHL patients found **no evidence** of Reissner or basilar membrane rupture.

### 4. Autoimmune Aetiology

SNHL has been reported in many systemic autoimmune disorders:

- Wegener's granulomatosis
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Polyarteritis nodosum
- Sjögren's syndrome
- Cogan's syndrome
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Ulcerative colitis
- Relapsing polychondritis

**Autoimmune Inner Ear Disease (AIED):**

McCabe (1979) described AIED - a rapidly progressive bilateral SNHL that responds to steroid therapy. The cause is thought to be antibodies or immune cells that damage the inner ear.

**Heat shock protein 70 (hsp70):**

- Antibodies to 68 kDa antigen (now known as hsp70) detected in 35% of patients with progressive SNHL
- Presence correlated with responsiveness to steroid treatment
- Sensitivity 42%, Specificity 90%, Positive predictive value 91%

**Problem with autoimmune theory:** One would expect autoimmune condition to affect both ears, but ISSNHL is usually unilateral.

### 5. Ménière's Disease

The first presentation of Ménière's disease may be as SSNHL and therefore important to consider in differential diagnosis.

### Possible Causes of SNHL (Box 60.1)

**Infectious:** Meningitis, encephalitis, herpes viruses, mumps, measles, HIV, Lyme disease, rubella, syphilis, toxoplasmosis

**Traumatic:** Barotrauma, perilymph fistula, intense noise, decompression sickness, temporal bone fracture, ear surgery

**Neoplastic:** CPA tumours ([[Vestibular Schwannoma|acoustic neuroma]], meningioma), leukaemia, myeloma

**Autoimmune:** Wegener's, RA, Sjögren's, polyarteritis nodosa, SLE, Cogan's syndrome, AIED

**Toxic:** Aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, NSAIDs, salicylates, platinum chemotherapy, general anaesthesia

**Circulatory:** Vascular disease, vertebrobasilar insufficiency, sickle-cell disease, cardiopulmonary bypass

**Neurologic:** Multiple sclerosis, focal pontine ischaemia, migraine

**Metabolic:** Hyperlipidaemia, thyrotoxicosis, diabetes

## PART C: CLINICAL ASSESSMENT

### History

Key points to cover:

**Onset and timing:**
- Patients often first notice hearing loss on awakening
- Better prognosis associated with short history
- Sudden onset of blockage or fullness warrants prompt investigation

**Characteristics of hearing loss:**
- Premorbid hearing level
- Unilateral or bilateral (bilateral suggests autoimmune or ototoxicity)
- Degree of loss (poorer prognosis with profound loss)

**Associated symptoms:**
- [[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]] - frequently associated, causes significant anxiety and depression
- Vertigo - present in 30-40% of cases, poor prognostic indicator
- Aural fullness - may indicate endolymphatic hydrops

**Risk factors:**
- Trauma, straining, diving, flying, intense noise exposure
- Previous or concurrent viral infections
- Past medical history of diseases associated with hearing loss
- Previous ear surgery
- Full drug history (ototoxicity)

### Examination

**Otoscopy:** Exclude middle ear effusions, infections, [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]], wax impaction

**Tuning fork tests:** Distinguish conductive from sensorineural hearing loss (Weber lateralizes to better ear, Rinne positive in affected ear)

**Fistula test:** May identify perilymph fistula

**Neurological examination:** Cranial nerves and cerebellar signs essential

### Investigations

**Essential:**

- **Pure-tone audiometry:** May indicate prognosis (downward sloping = poorer outcome)
- **MRI with gadolinium enhancement:** Exclude acoustic neuroma, evaluate for MS and CVA

**Regarding acoustic neuroma:**
- 10-20% of patients with acoustic neuromas report sudden decrease in hearing
- Prevalence of CPA tumours in SNHL patients: 2.7-10.2%

**If MRI contraindicated:** Fine-cut CT temporal bones with contrast

**Blood tests (if clinically indicated - NOT routine):**
- FBC, ESR, U&E, lipid profile, glucose
- Thyroid function tests
- Clotting screen, VDRL
- Lyme serology
- Autoantibodies (ANA, anticardiolipin, lupus anticoagulant, ANCA)

**Note:** American Academy of Otolaryngology guidelines make strong recommendation AGAINST routine laboratory testing.

## PART D: PROGNOSIS

### Spontaneous Recovery

Around **50%** of cases have spontaneous recovery with no treatment. The high spontaneous recovery rate (32-65%) has made validation of empirical treatments difficult.

### Prognostic Factors

**Four factors affect recovery:**

1. **Time since onset:** Earlier presentation = better prognosis
2. **Age:** Worse prognosis in over 60s
3. **Vertigo:** Poor prognostic indicator
4. **Audiogram pattern:** Profound hearing loss and downward-sloping audiogram = poorer prognosis

### Timeline of Recovery

- Improvement rate in first 2 weeks may predict long-term outcome
- Spontaneous improvement most likely during first 2 weeks
- Late recovery has been reported but is rare
- 78.2% of delayed recoveries occur within 1 month
- Only one patient in large series showed recovery beyond 6 months
- 6 months is reasonable follow-up period

## PART E: MEDICAL MANAGEMENT

### 1. Systemic Corticosteroids

**Evidence:**

- Widely used as standard treatment
- Systematic reviews reveal no evidence of benefit over placebo
- Wilson et al. (1980) found steroids had significant effect on recovery of hearing in patients with 40-90 dB loss
- Nosrati-Zarenoe et al. (prospective RCT): No significant difference between oral steroids and placebo
- Recent meta-analysis: Slight but not statistically significant improvement with steroids

**Recommended regimen:**

- Oral prednisolone **1 mg/kg/day** (maximum **60 mg daily**)
- Duration: **10-14 days**
- Early treatment within first 2 weeks associated with greatest recovery
- Minimal benefit after 4-6 weeks

**Dose equivalence:**

- 60 mg prednisolone = 48 mg methylprednisolone = 10 mg dexamethasone

**Side effects:**

*Short-term:* Gastritis, increased blood sugar, increased hunger, behaviour changes, insomnia, irritability, weight gain, salt/water retention, hypertension

*Severe (rare):* Pancreatitis, bleeding, cataracts, myopathy, avascular necrosis, diabetes, osteoporosis, opportunistic infections

**Contraindications/caution:**

- Insulin-dependent or poorly controlled diabetes
- Labile hypertension
- Tuberculosis
- Peptic ulcer disease
- Prior psychiatric reactions to corticosteroids

### 2. Intratympanic Corticosteroids

**Advantages:**

- Reduction in systemic side effects
- Higher inner ear steroid levels
- Rarely causes changes in serum glucose
- Safe in patients with cataracts, myasthenia gravis, glaucoma

**Evidence:**

- Useful for patients who cannot tolerate systemic steroids or are refractory
- IT + oral steroids shows trend towards greater improvement but not significant over oral alone
- IT steroids as sole treatment: improvement in 31/34 patients (Filipo et al.)
- Largest RCT (250 patients): Oral and IT steroids equally effective

**Commonly used steroids:**

- Dexamethasone: 10-24 mg/mL
- Solumedrol (methylprednisolone sodium succinate): 30-40 mg/mL

**Administration methods:**

- Transtympanic needle injection (most common)
- Through grommet or myringotomy
- MicroWick or microcatheter
- Hydrogel applications, nanoparticles

**Complications:**

- Transient dizziness
- Ear pain
- Tinnitus
- Persistent tympanic membrane perforation (main risk)
- Possible vasovagal episode during injection

### 3. Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy (HBOT)

**Mechanism:** Delivers 100% oxygen at pressure >1 atmosphere, increasing tissue oxygenation and potentiating response to ischaemia.

**Evidence:**

- Cochrane review: Chance of 25% improvement significantly increased (NNT = 5)
- 50% improvement not significantly increased
- More benefit in severe to profound loss
- Should be used within 3 months of onset

**Complications:**

- Ear, sinus and lung damage from pressure changes
- Temporary worsening of short-sightedness
- Claustrophobia
- Oxygen poisoning (rare)
- Eustachian tube dysfunction (17-45%)

**Disadvantages:** Expensive, time-consuming, not readily available

### 4. Other Therapies (No Evidence of Benefit)

- **Antivirals:** Acyclovir, valacyclovir - no significant improvement
- **Vasodilators:** Papaverine, pentoxifylline, nifedipine, ginkgo biloba - no difference from controls
- **Volume expanders:** Hydroxyethyl starch, dextran - no difference from controls
- **Prostacyclin:** No significant difference from saline
- **Procaine:** No significant difference

### 5. Salvage Therapy

For patients refractory to systemic steroids:

**Intratympanic steroids:**
- Meta-analysis shows statistically significant improvement
- Improvement rates: 8-100%
- Dexamethasone tends to show better outcomes than methylprednisolone
- Injection better than round window catheter

**HBOT as salvage:** Also investigated with some benefit

## PART F: OUTCOMES ASSESSMENT AND FOLLOW-UP

### Follow-up Requirements

- All patients need follow-up audiological assessment
- May reveal other aetiologies (ISSNHL could be first presentation of another condition)
- Identifies patients with ongoing hearing loss
- Identifies candidates for hearing rehabilitation or salvage therapy

### Audiological Assessment

Compare at each appointment:
- Pure-tone audiometry (PTA)
- Speech recognition threshold (SRT)
- Word recognition score (WRS)

### Definition of Recovery (Wilson et al.)

- **Complete:** Follow-up PTA or SRT improved to within 10 dB of pre-SSNHL levels
- **Partial:** Improvement but not to within 10 dB

### Rehabilitation

- Early discussion of rehabilitation options reduces patient anxiety
- Temporary aiding during treatment may be beneficial
- Consider in patients with permanent hearing loss

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. SSNHL is defined as 30 dB or more hearing loss over at least 3 contiguous frequencies within 72 hours
> 2. ISSNHL is SSNHL with no identifiable cause after thorough investigation
> 3. Annual incidence: 5-30 per 100,000; 99% unilateral; average age 50-60 years
> 4. Aetiology identified in only 5-10% of cases - majority are idiopathic
> 5. Four main aetiological theories: viral, vascular, membrane rupture, autoimmune
> 6. Spontaneous recovery occurs in 50% (range 32-65%) of cases
> 7. Four prognostic factors: time since onset, age (>60 worse), vertigo (poor), audiogram pattern
> 8. MRI with gadolinium is essential to exclude acoustic neuroma (2.7-10.2% prevalence in SNHL)
> 9. Standard treatment: Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day (max 60 mg) for 10-14 days
> 10. Early treatment within 2 weeks associated with greatest recovery; minimal benefit after 4-6 weeks
> 11. IT steroids useful for patients who cannot tolerate systemic steroids or are refractory
> 12. HBOT may benefit severe-profound loss if used within 3 months of onset


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. SSNHL often presents as a "blocked" or "full" ear - this common symptom can be underestimated, delaying diagnosis and treatment
> 2. Vertigo is present in 30-40% of SSNHL cases and is a poor prognostic indicator - always ask about it
> 3. Routine blood tests are NOT recommended by AAO-HNS guidelines - investigate based on clinical suspicion
> 4. The psychological impact of sudden hearing loss is significant - recognize and address anxiety and depression
> 5. Recovery in the first 2 weeks predicts long-term outcome - follow up early
> 6. IT steroids are an excellent alternative for diabetics and others who cannot tolerate systemic steroids
