# TINNITUS

> [!question] Questions
> Define and classify tinnitus. Describe the pathophysiology of tinnitus. Enumerate the various causes of pulsatile tinnitus.
> Source: Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Volume 2, Chapter 61 - Tinnitus and Hyperacusis

---

## INTRODUCTION

Tinnitus is defined as the conscious expression of a sound that originates in an involuntary manner in the head of its owner, or may appear to do so. The word originates from the Latin term "tinnire" meaning to ring. While many subjects describe the sound as a ring, this is only one of many descriptions reported. The sound perceived does not have to be ringing, does not have to be continuous, and in some cases can even represent complex sounds such as musical tunes. The majority of people with tinnitus report subjective non-pulsatile tinnitus with no specific associated pathological process other than age-associated hearing loss, often referred to as subjective idiopathic tinnitus.

The prevalence of persistent spontaneous tinnitus in the adult population is approximately 10.1%, with 5% describing their tinnitus as moderately or severely annoying and 0.5% reporting that it had a severe effect on their ability to lead a normal life. Studies from various countries show similar figures: Italy 14.5%, US 14.9%, and Sweden 15.8%. Tinnitus is perceived in both ears or in the middle of the head by approximately half of sufferers, with left-sided tinnitus slightly more common than right-sided when unilateral.

## PART A: DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION OF TINNITUS

### Definitions

Several definitions of tinnitus have been proposed in the literature:

- The conscious expression of a sound that originates in an involuntary manner in the head of its owner, or may appear to do so (McFadden)
- The sensation of hearing a sound in the absence of an external stimulus
- A sound sensation in the absence of an external or internal acoustical source or electric stimulation

### Classification Systems

#### Based on Sound Character

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Pulsatile | Rhythmic sound synchronized or not with pulse |
| Non-pulsatile | Continuous or intermittent non-rhythmic sound |

#### Based on Perception

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Subjective | Only the patient perceives the sound |
| Objective | Sound can be perceived by others |

#### Classification of Pulsatile Tinnitus

| Type | Characteristic |
|------|----------------|
| Synchronous | Sound in synchrony with arterial pulse |
| Non-synchronous | Rhythmic but not pulse-synchronous |

#### Based on Sound Type

The sounds reported are mostly simple sounds including:

- Whistling
- Humming
- Ringing
- Tones
- Complex sounds including voices or music (indistinct, without specific meaning)

### Epidemiological Classification

| Category | Prevalence |
|----------|------------|
| Persistent spontaneous tinnitus | 10.1% |
| Moderately/severely annoying | 5% |
| Severe effect on normal life | 0.5% |

## PART B: PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF TINNITUS

The pathophysiology of tinnitus involves both peripheral and central auditory mechanisms. Although the link between high-frequency hearing loss and tinnitus might suggest a cochlear origin, most modern research underscores the importance of central auditory pathways in both the development and maintenance of distressing tinnitus.

### The Ignition-Promotion Concept

Pathological events create an "ignition site" (where initial signal is generated) and subsequent central mechanisms termed "promotion" maintain the tinnitus. Pathological events which create an ignition site do not inevitably generate tinnitus - the central promotion must also be present. This theory has clinical relevance: if tinnitus distress is maintained by central auditory pathways, it may be futile trying to correct peripheral auditory pathology.

### Peripheral Mechanisms

#### 1. Discordant Damage of Cochlear Hair Cells

Outer hair cells are more susceptible than inner hair cells to damage by certain agents including noise and aminoglycoside antibiotics. In areas where outer hair cells have been damaged but inner hair cells remain, the tectorial membrane is no longer supported by the outer hair cells and can sag onto the inner hair cells, causing them to depolarize.

#### 2. Calcium Channel Dysfunction

Calcium is fundamental to several functions of cochlear hair cells. Some drugs known to cause tinnitus, including salicylates and quinine, affect intracellular calcium levels. Noise also affects the concentration of intracellular calcium, suggesting calcium flux may be implicated in tinnitus generation.

#### 3. Glutamate Receptor Dysfunction

Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter in the auditory system. Key points include:

- AMPA receptors are the main receptors on auditory nerve fibres under inner hair cells
- Glutamate in large quantities is toxic to nerve fibres (glutamate excitotoxicity)
- NMDA receptor blockade can be protective against both salicylate-induced and noise-induced tinnitus in animal models

### Central Mechanisms

#### 1. Increased Spontaneous Firing

There is always a degree of electrical activity in the auditory system even without sound input. Damage to the ear results in reduced activity in the auditory nerve, which downregulates inhibitory processes in higher auditory centres, generating increased spontaneous activity in the auditory cortex perceived as tinnitus.

#### 2. Increased Central Neural Synchrony

Spontaneous neural activity in the auditory cortex is normally random. When this activity becomes synchronized, this signals that a sound is present. If the peripheral auditory system is damaged, spontaneous cortical activity tends to become more synchronized, which may give rise to tinnitus.

#### 3. Reorganization of Cortical Auditory Map

The auditory system is tonotopically organized from cochlea to cortex. When the peripheral auditory system is damaged:

- Neurons that received inputs from damaged cochlear areas tune to nearest adjacent active frequency input
- This results in over-representation of frequencies adjacent to areas of damage
- Increased neural activity at those frequencies may produce tinnitus

### Tinnitus Models

#### Hallam's Habituation Model (1984)

Tinnitus is caused by some neurophysiological disturbance in the auditory system at any point between periphery and cortex. Normally, the central auditory system should habituate to this activity. In high autonomic arousal states, this habituation process fails and tinnitus becomes intrusive.

#### Jastreboff's Neurophysiological Model (1990)

In addition to events within the classical auditory system, tinnitus involves altered activity within:

- Limbic system
- Reticular system
- Autonomic nervous system

This model forms the basis for Tinnitus Retraining Therapy (TRT), combining directive counselling and sound therapy to counteract pathological positive feedback and promote habituation.

### Tinnitus Modulation

Tinnitus can be influenced by stimuli from outside the auditory system:

- Many patients can modulate their symptom by touching their face
- Clenching teeth can alter tinnitus
- Changing gaze direction affects tinnitus in some patients

This highlights the importance of links between the auditory system and other somatosensory pathways.

## PART C: CAUSES OF PULSATILE TINNITUS

Pulsatile tinnitus is a form where the perception of sound is not continuous but takes on the form of a pulsation, clicking, or fluttering. It is classified as either synchronous or non-synchronous with the patient's heartbeat.

### Synchronous Pulsatile Tinnitus (Pulse-Synchronous)

#### Arterial Causes

- Atherosclerotic carotid artery disease
- Arteriovenous fistula
- Arteriovenous malformation
- Intracranial aneurysm
- Fibromuscular dysplasia of the carotid artery
- Dissection of the carotid artery
- Vascular anomalies of the ear
- Vascular compression of the VIIIth nerve

#### Venous Causes

- Jugular bulb abnormalities (high riding, dehiscent)
- Dural venous sinus stenosis
- Abnormal condylar or mastoid emissary veins
- Idiopathic venous hum (essential tinnitus)

#### Microvascular/Tumor Causes

- [[Glomus Tumour|Glomus tumour]] (paraganglioma)
- Paget's disease
- Cholesterol granuloma of middle ear
- Meningioma of middle ear
- Cavernous haemangioma
- Histiocytosis X

#### Circulatory/Systemic Causes

- Increased cardiac output states:
  - Anaemia
  - Thyrotoxicosis
  - Pregnancy
- Aortic murmurs

#### Perceptual Causes

- Conductive hearing loss (heightened awareness)
- Cochlear trauma

#### Other Causes

- Benign intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)
- Superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome

### Non-Synchronous Pulsatile Tinnitus

#### Middle Ear Myoclonus

- Tensor tympani muscle contraction
- Stapedius muscle contraction
- Produces clicking or buzzing noise

#### Palatal Myoclonus

- Essential palatal myoclonus (idiopathic) - commonly associated with tinnitus
- Symptomatic palatal myoclonus (brainstem lesions)
- Irregular clicking at 1-2 per second
- Involuntary palatal movements visible on examination

### Investigation of Pulsatile Tinnitus

| Investigation | Indication |
|---------------|------------|
| Blood tests | Exclude anaemia, thyrotoxicosis |
| Contrast CT temporal bone | Retrotympanic mass on otoscopy |
| Duplex carotid USS | Suspected atherosclerotic disease |
| MRA/CTA | Vascular pathology |
| Formal angiography | Recalcitrant cases |
| Lumbar puncture | Suspected intracranial hypertension |
| Ophthalmological exam | Papilloedema in intracranial HTN |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Tinnitus is defined as the conscious perception of sound originating involuntarily in the head, with a prevalence of approximately 10% in adults.
>
> 2. Classification includes: pulsatile vs non-pulsatile, subjective vs objective, and synchronous vs non-synchronous for pulsatile types.
>
> 3. The ignition-promotion concept explains that peripheral damage creates the ignition site while central mechanisms maintain the distressing tinnitus.
>
> 4. Peripheral mechanisms include discordant hair cell damage, calcium channel dysfunction, and glutamate receptor abnormalities.
>
> 5. Central mechanisms include increased spontaneous firing, increased neural synchrony, and cortical tonotopic map reorganization.
>
> 6. Jastreboff's neurophysiological model involves the limbic system, reticular system, and autonomic nervous system in addition to the auditory pathway.
>
> 7. Synchronous pulsatile tinnitus causes include arterial (atherosclerosis, AV fistula), venous (jugular bulb anomalies), and tumors (glomus).
>
> 8. Benign intracranial hypertension is increasingly recognized as an important cause of pulsatile tinnitus, particularly in young overweight women.
>
> 9. Non-synchronous pulsatile tinnitus is commonly due to middle ear or palatal myoclonus.
>
> 10. Superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome can present with pulsatile tinnitus in addition to dizziness.
>
> 11. Tinnitus modulation by somatosensory stimuli (facial touch, teeth clenching, gaze) demonstrates auditory-somatosensory pathway interaction.
>
> 12. Investigation of pulsatile tinnitus requires imaging (CT/MRA) to exclude vascular pathology and intracranial hypertension.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Left-sided tinnitus is slightly more common than right-sided when unilateral, though the reason is unknown.
>
> 2. Many cochlear hair cells may be damaged before hearing loss is demonstrated on conventional pure-tone audiometry - "hidden hearing loss."
>
> 3. Tinnitus can exist even after complete destruction of auditory periphery or after auditory nerve section, confirming central mechanisms.
>
> 4. The noise-induced hearing loss increases likelihood of tinnitus, but majority with noise-induced cochlear damage do not have significant tinnitus.
>
> 5. Glomus tumour should be suspected when pulsatile tinnitus is associated with a retrotympanic red/blue mass.
>
> 6. In middle ear myoclonus, both tensor tympani and stapedius tendons should be divided surgically as the responsible muscle cannot be reliably identified.
