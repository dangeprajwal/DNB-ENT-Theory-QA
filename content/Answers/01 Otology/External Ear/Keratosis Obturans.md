# Keratosis Obturans Presenting with [[Facial Nerve|Facial Nerve Palsy]]

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Discuss keratosis obturans presenting with facial nerve palsy. Include its pathology, clinical features, staging, and management.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 76

---

## INTRODUCTION

Keratosis obturans (KO) is a distinct clinical and pathological entity characterized by the accumulation of a large plug of desquamated keratin in the external auditory meatus. It results from abnormal epithelial migration of the ear canal skin. While facial nerve involvement is rare in KO, extension of disease can lead to complications including facial nerve palsy. It must be differentiated from primary auditory canal [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]] (ACC) and benign necrotizing otitis externa (BNOE).

---

## PATHOLOGY

The characteristic pathological finding in keratosis obturans is a geometrically patterned keratin plug within the lumen of the external auditory canal. The keratin squames are shed from the complete circumference of the deep ear canal and become arranged in a laminar (onion skin) pattern. Over time, the accumulating keratin mass causes the bony canal to become "ballooned" or widened due to pressure erosion.

**Pathological Features:**
- Geometrically patterned keratin plug in canal lumen
- Laminar (onion skin) arrangement of desquamated keratin
- Keratin derived from entire circumference of deep canal
- Progressive bony canal expansion (ballooning)
- Intact tympanic membrane standing in relief

---

## AETIOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS

The primary mechanism underlying keratosis obturans is abnormal epithelial migration of the ear canal skin. The epithelium may move in an abnormal direction across the drum with a grossly abnormal migratory pathway, leading to keratin accumulation rather than normal extrusion.

**Two Types of Keratosis Obturans:**

**Inflammatory Type:** This is secondary to an acute problem such as viral infection, causing temporary alteration in epithelial migration. This type is typically cured by a single removal of the keratin plug.

**Silent Type:** This represents persistent disease caused by abnormal separation of keratin with ongoing accumulation. This type requires subsequent repeated removals and long-term surveillance.

**Associated Conditions:**
- Bronchiectasis
- Sinusitis
- Historical association with "wax keratosis" (now less common as lung disease is better controlled)

---

## DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| **Keratosis Obturans** | Abnormal epithelial migration, severe otalgia, conductive hearing loss, younger patients, bilateral possible, canal ballooning |
| **Auditory Canal Cholesteatoma** | Abnormal bone leading to epithelial invasion into bone, mild otalgia, no hearing loss, older patients, unilateral, localized erosion |
| **Benign Necrotizing Otitis Externa** | Avascular bone leading to skin inflammation, blocked feeling, dull pain, necrotic bone visible at floor |
| **Malignant Otitis Externa** | Immunocompromised patient, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, chronic granulating inflammation |

---

## CLINICAL FEATURES

Keratosis obturans typically presents in younger patients with acute severe otalgia, which is the classical presentation. Patients develop conductive hearing impairment due to the obstructing keratin plug. The condition is occasionally bilateral, which helps distinguish it from auditory canal cholesteatoma which is almost always unilateral.

**Symptoms:**
- Acute severe otalgia (classical symptom)
- Conductive hearing loss
- Sensation of ear blockage
- History of recurrent ear symptoms

**Examination Findings:**
- Widened or ballooned ear canal on microscopy
- Large keratin plug filling the canal
- Tympanic membrane appears to stand in relief
- Associated granulation tissue may be present

---

## STAGING SYSTEM

The Nairn classification system stages keratosis obturans based on the extent of disease:

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Epithelial hyperplasia only |
| IIa | Periostitis with erythema |
| IIb | Denuded but not eroded bone |
| III | Canal wall erosion and bony sequestrum |
| IV | Invasion into adjacent structures |

**Stage IV Subclassification:**
- IV-M: Mastoid involvement (automastoidectomy)
- IV-S: Skull base and sigmoid sinus involvement
- IV-J: Temporomandibular joint involvement
- IV-F: Facial nerve canal involvement

---

## FACIAL NERVE INVOLVEMENT

Facial nerve palsy in keratosis obturans is a rare but serious complication that occurs in Stage IV disease (IV-F subclass). The mechanism involves progressive bony erosion extending into the facial nerve canal, leading to compression or direct involvement of the nerve.

**Mechanisms of Facial Nerve Palsy:**
- Extensive bony erosion extending to facial canal
- Automastoidectomy with secondary nerve involvement
- Direct compression by expanding disease
- Inflammatory involvement of the nerve

**Assessment of Facial Nerve Palsy:**
- House-Brackmann grading of paralysis severity
- Urgent CT temporal bones to assess extent
- MRI if soft tissue detail required
- Electroneurography if indicated

---

## INVESTIGATIONS

**Microscopic Examination:** This is essential and should be carried out in clinic or under general anaesthesia. It reveals the widened/ballooned ear canal, the characteristic keratin plug, and the tympanic membrane standing in relief.

**CT Temporal Bones:** This is the key imaging modality and is indicated for staging and assessment of complications. CT demonstrates expansion of the bony external auditory canal, extent of bony erosion, and involvement of adjacent structures including the facial nerve canal, mastoid, and temporomandibular joint.

**Biopsy:** This is mandatory to exclude carcinoma in atypical cases, especially when there is extensive bony destruction or granulation tissue.

---

## MANAGEMENT

### Conservative Management

For uncomplicated keratosis obturans, the primary treatment is removal of the keratin plug under microscopic guidance. This may be performed in clinic or under general anaesthesia depending on the extent of disease and patient factors. Granulations should be treated, and regular aural toilet maintained. Associated respiratory conditions (bronchiectasis, sinusitis) should be addressed.

### Surgical Management

**Indications for Surgery:**
- Recurrent or persistent disease
- Complications including facial nerve involvement
- Extensive bony erosion (Stage III-IV)
- Failed conservative management

**Surgical Approach by Stage:**
- Stage I: Local care or canaloplasty
- Stage II: Canaloplasty with [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]] if needed
- Stage III: Surgical removal of sequestrum and repair
- Stage IV: Extended surgery addressing all involved structures

### Management of Facial Nerve Palsy

When the facial nerve is involved, urgent surgical intervention is required. The approach includes urgent CT/MRI imaging to delineate extent, assessment of paralysis severity using House-Brackmann grading, surgical decompression of the nerve, complete excision of all disease, and mastoidectomy if indicated.

**Surgical Considerations:**
- Intraoperative facial nerve monitoring is essential
- Wide excision of all disease including sequestrum
- Grafting with cartilage and fascia may be required
- Post-operative surveillance for recurrence

---

## COMPLICATIONS

Complications of keratosis obturans result from progressive bony erosion and include automastoidectomy (spontaneous erosion through mastoid), facial nerve palsy (Stage IV-F), temporomandibular joint involvement (anterior wall erosion), and recurrence if the underlying epithelial migration disorder is not addressed.

Importantly, there are no reports of intracranial complications from keratosis obturans in the literature, distinguishing it from malignant otitis externa.

---

## PROGNOSIS

The prognosis depends on the type and stage of disease. Simple inflammatory-type keratosis obturans has excellent prognosis with removal and may be cured by a single intervention. Silent-type recurrent disease requires regular follow-up and repeated clearance. For facial nerve involvement, recovery is expected if nerve continuity is maintained and prompt decompression is performed.
