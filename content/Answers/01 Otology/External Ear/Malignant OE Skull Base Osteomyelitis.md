# Malignant Otitis Externa and Skull Base Osteomyelitis

**Question (Q14, December 2019):** *Describe etiopathogenesis, clinical features and management of malignant otitis externa.*

**Question (Q53, December 2021):** *Describe the clinical features, diagnostic criteria, stages, investigation and management of Malignant Otitis Externa.*

**Question (Q91, October 2023):** *What are the clinical and radiological findings of skull base osteomyelitis necrotizing otitis externa? Briefly describe the management of these cases.*

**Question (Q109, May 2024):** *Discuss the aetiopathogenesis, clinical features, diagnosis and management of skull base osteomyelitis.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 114: Osteitis of the Temporal Bone; Chapter 83/97: Imaging of the Temporal Bone

---

## INTRODUCTION

Malignant otitis externa (MOE), also termed necrotizing otitis externa (NOE), is a severe, invasive infection of the external auditory canal caused primarily by Pseudomonas aeruginosa or Aspergillus species that may progress to skull base osteomyelitis (SBO). First described by Chandler in 1968, it predominantly afflicts elderly diabetics and immunocompromised patients. It is a rare but life-threatening complication of otitis externa in which soft tissue pathogens spread to the periosteum and temporal bone causing necrosis. The infection may progress to involve the facial nerve, carotid artery, jugular vein, and lower cranial nerves. If untreated, it can lead to meningitis, cerebral abscess, and death. The incidence of skull base osteomyelitis is increasing, with Hospital Episodes Statistics showing a rise from 67 cases in 1999-2000 to 421 in 2012-2013, likely due to an increased number of elderly diabetics and greater clinical suspicion.

---

## PART A: ETIOPATHOGENESIS

### Predisposing Factors

Skull base osteomyelitis most frequently affects the elderly and diabetic populations. The prevalence of diabetes ranges from 65% to 100% of patients. Postulated mechanisms for susceptibility in diabetics include endarteritis, microangiopathy and small vessel obliteration, combined with innate defects in humoral immunity.

**Immunocompromising conditions** that predispose to SBO include:

- HIV/AIDS (patients tend to be younger; more likely to have fungal infection, particularly when CD4 counts are less than 50 cells/mm)
- Steroid usage and chemotherapy
- Anaemia, leukaemia, lymphoma, and neoplasia
- Renal transplantation

**In children**, skull base osteomyelitis is rare, occurring in immunocompromised states such as IgA deficiency, acute monocytic leukaemia, iatrogenic neutropenia, and bone marrow transplantation. Diabetes is not as common a risk factor in children.

### Pathophysiology

The infection spreads from the external auditory canal to the skull base through the **fissures of Santorini** (natural defects in the cartilaginous canal floor). At the osteocartilaginous junction, infection spreads medially to the tympanomastoid suture along venous canals and fascial planes, invading connective tissue, cartilage, bone, nerves and blood vessels. The inflammatory process alters the morphology of compact skull base bone, converting it to granulation tissue.

**Specific spread pathways include:**

- Through the petrotympanic fissure to the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and soft tissues below the skull base
- Medially toward the carotid canal
- Inferiorly through soft tissue spaces below the skull base
- Central skull base invasion from direct extension along the petroclival fissure
- As infection progresses to skull base foramina, cranial neuropathies ensue

Additional diabetic susceptibility factors include weakened immune response to Pseudomonas aeruginosa and the high pH of diabetic cerumen.

### Precipitating Event

There is usually a history of ear canal trauma, often after irrigation for wax removal. Self-inflicted trauma has also been implicated, and a strong relationship between laterality of skull base osteomyelitis and handedness has been reported.

## PART B: BACTERIOLOGY AND MICROBIOLOGY

### Primary Pathogen - Pseudomonas aeruginosa

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a Gram-negative obligate aerobe, is the most common bacterial species in skull base osteomyelitis. It is opportunistic and pathogenic only when natural host defences are defective. Its virulence mechanisms include:

- Ability to invade local vasculature causing focal coagulative necrosis
- Mucoid surface layer conferring protection against phagocytosis
- Production of lytic enzymes (collagenases, elastases) and endotoxins causing necrotizing vasculitis
- Production of neurotoxins likely playing a role in cranial neuropathies

Diabetics are particularly susceptible due to poor phagocytosis by polymorphonuclear leucocytes.

### Other Bacterial Pathogens

Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella oxytoca, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Burkholderia cenocepacia, Enterobacter cloacae, and Streptococcus constellatus.

### Fungal Pathogens

Fungal infections are more commonly associated with the immunocompromised (HIV/AIDS) than with diabetics. In contrast to pseudomonal SBO, fungal infections more often originate in the middle ear or mastoid. **Aspergillus fumigatus** is the most common fungal organism and is associated with a worse prognosis than Pseudomonas. Other fungal species include Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus flavus, Candida sp., Malassezia sympodialis, and Scedosporium apiospermum.

## PART C: CLINICAL FEATURES

### Presenting Symptoms

**Otalgia** is the most common presenting symptom (prevalence 75-100%). It is characteristically:

- Nocturnal
- Deep and lancinating
- Disproportionate to clinical signs
- Often resistant to analgesics

**Purulent otorrhoea** is the second most common symptom and sign, found in more than half of cases, within an exquisitely tender and oedematous ear canal.

### Examination Findings

- **Granulation tissue** or exposed bone on the floor of the ear canal (granulations should be sent for histological analysis to exclude malignancy, histiocytosis, and tuberculosis)
- Polyp or granuloma may obstruct the view of the tympanic membrane
- Patients with HIV infection often lack granulation tissue

### Hearing Loss

- **Conductive hearing loss** is usual, caused by external auditory canal obstruction by granulation or oedema
- Sensorineural hearing loss is usually due to pre-existing presbycusis

### Cranial Neuropathies

As bony destruction progresses medially through skull base foramina:

- **Facial nerve (VII)** is most commonly affected (up to 25% of patients) due to its close relation to the external auditory canal
- This is followed by cranial nerves **IX, X, XI, XII** and then **VI and II**
- Children develop facial nerve palsies earlier due to their more medial fissures of Santorini and underdeveloped mastoid process

### Late Complications

Meningitis, cerebral abscess, and sigmoid sinus thrombosis are late signs associated with a poor prognosis.

### Other Features

Parotitis and trismus due to masseter myositis and temporomandibular joint involvement is a rare but described clinical feature.

### Prognostic Factors

A meta-analysis identified factors with statistically significant negative effect on outcome: cranial neuropathies, external auditory canal granulations, bilateral symptoms, and positive Aspergillus cultures.

## PART D: INVESTIGATIONS AND RADIOLOGY

### High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT)

- Valuable for diagnosing bony erosion and reduced density in the skull base
- **Limitation:** Only detects bony demineralization of 30% or greater; a negative CT must be treated with caution as a potential false negative
- Contrast-enhanced CT useful for evaluating extratemporal soft tissue involvement, abscess formation, mastoid and TMJ involvement, and progression to petrous apex and carotid canal
- **Cannot** reliably measure treatment response, distinguish neoplasia from inflammation, or show intracranial/bone marrow involvement
- Persistent cortical changes may be depicted 12 months after treatment initiation

### Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

- Good at soft tissue differentiation and can depict bone marrow oedema
- Better at detecting early central skull base disease and showing bone marrow changes before frank cortical destruction
- Intracranial extension (direct from mastoid/middle ear or via neural foramina) is best identified on MRI
- **Limitation:** Unable to assess treatment response; shows soft tissue and marrow abnormalities months after treatment completion
- Loss of normal fatty marrow signal on standard T1-weighted images indicates early central SBO

### Nuclear Imaging

**Technetium-99m MDP scintigraphy:**

- Almost 100% sensitivity
- Radiotracer accumulates in areas of high osteoblastic activity even in early disease
- Low specificity (also positive in malignancy; histological confirmation important)
- Cannot distinguish active infection from bone remodelling

**Gallium-67 citrate scanning:**

- Accumulates in areas of active inflammation in both soft tissue and bone
- Normalizes with disease resolution, unlike Tc-99m MDP
- **Reliable tool to gauge treatment response** - should be repeated every 4 weeks to monitor antibiotic response until no longer positive
- Most useful for follow-up as imaging returns to normal more rapidly after infection settles

**Indium-111 labelled leucocyte scintigraphy:**

- Detects neutrophil-mediated inflammation
- SPECT (Single Photon Emission CT) creates 3D pictures using Indium-111 or Gallium-67, optimizing anatomical localization

**FDG-PET:**

- Most accurate in confirming or excluding osteomyelitis
- Not significantly better than combination of Tc-99m bone and leucocyte scintigraphy or Gallium-67 scintigraphy
- May not be available in most acute settings

## PART E: MANAGEMENT

### 1. Aural Toilet

Vital to enable control of granulations and pain. Controversy exists regarding use of topical antimicrobial therapy as it may make culture of the pathological organism difficult.

### 2. Systemic Antibiotics

Long-term systemic culture-directed antimicrobial therapy is the **mainstay of treatment**. Key principles:

- Treatment for at least **6-8 weeks** depending on symptom resolution; in advanced cases may continue for months
- Ideally continued as long as the Gallium-67 scan remains positive
- **Fluoroquinolones** have transformed treatment of SBO due to their antipseudomonal activity, good bone penetration, good oral bioavailability, and fewer side effects

**First-line:** Oral ciprofloxacin **750 mg twice daily** as monotherapy

Some experts advocate parenteral antimicrobials initially, converting to oral once CRP and/or ESR start to decline. Addition of rifampicin (600 mg twice daily) has been suggested but no direct evidence of additional benefit.

**For resistance:** The incidence of Pseudomonal resistance to fluoroquinolones is increasing worldwide. Alternatives include:

- Cephalosporins active against pyocyanin (ceftazidime, cefepime)
- Penicillins (ticarcillin, clavulanate)
- Aminoglycosides (in combination or as monotherapy)

**For fungal SBO:** Amphotericin B is the antifungal agent of choice, followed by oral itraconazole after a successful course.

**Ciprofloxacin in children:** Has been safely administered to children with cystic fibrosis. In resistant cases, antipseudomonal beta-lactam agents (ceftazidime, piperacillin, imipenem) with or without an aminoglycoside are used.

### 3. Hyperbaric Oxygen

Reports suggest beneficial effects by increasing partial pressure of oxygen, relieving hypoxia, and enhancing oxidative killing of microbes. However, a Cochrane review found no evidence from randomized controlled trials to support this treatment. It may be considered as adjunctive therapy for refractory cases with unproven efficacy.

### 4. Surgery

Surgery has a **limited role** in management of skull base osteomyelitis:

- Primarily performed to obtain specimens for culture, locally debride granulation tissue, and exclude malignancy
- In selected cases, may be necessary to remove dead sequestra and drain associated abscesses
- Facial nerve decompression for complete facial palsies has unproven therapeutic benefit

### 5. Diabetic Control

Patients with poorly controlled diabetes are more susceptible to developing SBO. Aggressive glycaemic control can be as crucial a management strategy as systemic antimicrobial therapy. However, achieving good glucose control may be difficult in the setting of invasive infection.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Skull base osteomyelitis is a severe infection usually arising from otitis externa through the fissures of Santorini
> 2. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most common causative organism; Aspergillus fumigatus is the most common fungal pathogen and carries a worse prognosis
> 3. It predominantly affects elderly diabetics (prevalence of diabetes 65-100%) and immunocompromised patients
> 4. Otalgia (nocturnal, deep, lancinating, disproportionate to signs, resistant to analgesics) is the most common presenting symptom (75-100%)
> 5. Facial nerve is the most commonly affected cranial nerve (up to 25%), followed by IX, X, XI, XII, then VI and II
> 6. HRCT only detects demineralization of 30% or greater; a negative scan does not exclude early disease
> 7. Gallium-67 scanning is the most reliable tool for monitoring treatment response and should be repeated every 4 weeks
> 8. Oral ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice daily for at least 6-8 weeks is the preferred first-line regimen
> 9. Surgery has a limited role - primarily diagnostic biopsy and debridement
> 10. Aggressive glycaemic control is as crucial as antimicrobial therapy
> 11. The incidence of SBO is increasing (67 to 421 cases over 13 years in UK data)
> 12. Negative prognostic factors include cranial neuropathies, canal granulations, bilateral symptoms, and positive Aspergillus cultures

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Always send granulation tissue from the ear canal for histological analysis to exclude malignancy, histiocytosis, and tuberculosis - the clinical presentation can mimic squamous cell carcinoma
> 2. HIV-positive patients often lack the characteristic granulation tissue in the ear canal, making clinical diagnosis more difficult
> 3. MRI shows bone marrow changes before frank cortical destruction on CT, making it superior for early central skull base disease detection
> 4. Gallium-67 normalizes with disease resolution unlike Tc-99m MDP, making it the preferred modality for monitoring treatment response
> 5. The strong relationship between laterality of SBO and handedness (self-inflicted ear canal trauma) is a useful clinical correlation
> 6. Children develop facial nerve palsies earlier than adults due to more medial fissures of Santorini and underdeveloped mastoid process
