# Intra-operative Facial Nerve Monitoring

**Question:** *Discuss the indications, anaesthesia modifications, and management of surgical trauma with loss of signal in intra-operative facial nerve monitoring.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition
- Volume 1, Chapter 45 (Electrophysiology and Monitoring)
- Volume 2, Chapters 99, 102 (Facial Paralysis, [[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular Schwannoma]] Surgery)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Intra-operative facial nerve monitoring (IOFNM) has revolutionized otological and skull base surgery since its introduction in 1979 by Delgado and colleagues. It provides real-time feedback to the surgeon, enabling identification of the facial nerve, mapping its course, and assessing changes in function during surgical procedures. Facial nerve monitoring has proven to be an invaluable aid during vestibular schwannoma surgery and other procedures where the nerve is at risk.

---

## PART A: INDICATIONS FOR INTRA-OPERATIVE FACIAL NERVE MONITORING

### Absolute Indications (Mandatory)

Facial nerve monitoring is mandatory in skull base surgery where the nerve anatomy is distorted or at high risk. This includes vestibular schwannoma surgery where the nerve is displaced by tumour, other cerebellopontine angle tumours with variable facial nerve anatomy, translabyrinthine approach where the nerve is identified early but remains at risk throughout, middle fossa approach where the nerve lies in the surgical field, retrosigmoid approach where nerve identification is critical, facial nerve tumours where the nerve may be engulfed, and [[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]] surgery.

Evidence supporting monitoring in vestibular schwannoma surgery shows significant benefit. Harner et al. demonstrated 78% facial function at 1 year with monitoring versus 65% without. Niparko et al. showed significant improvement in facial function for tumours greater than 2 cm, and Kwartler et al. confirmed significant improvement for tumours greater than 2.5 cm.

Parotid surgery also represents an absolute indication. The facial nerve traverses the parotid gland and is at risk during superficial [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]], total parotidectomy, deep lobe parotidectomy, revision parotid surgery, and surgery for parotid malignancy. Microvascular decompression for hemifacial spasm or trigeminal neuralgia when the facial nerve is at risk also requires monitoring.

### Relative Indications (Recommended)

In chronic ear surgery, monitoring is recommended when the facial nerve may be dehiscent or eroded by [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]], during revision mastoid surgery where landmarks are altered, during canal wall down mastoidectomy with greater nerve exposure, and when there is extensive disease with higher risk of nerve injury.

Monitoring is also recommended in situations with altered anatomy. Congenital ear malformations may have aberrant nerve course, previous ear surgery results in scarring and altered landmarks, trauma cases have distorted anatomy, temporal bone fractures may have damaged the nerve, and inflammatory conditions may have granulation tissue obscuring the nerve.

For [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] surgery, monitoring is recommended especially in malformed cochleas, revision cochlear implant surgery, and when the facial nerve course is uncertain.

### Arguments for Routine Monitoring

Silverstein recommends that the facial nerve should be monitored during all general anaesthetic cases where the facial nerve is at risk. Survey data by Hu et al. shows that 49% of surgeons feel facial nerve monitoring should be standard of care, increased from 32% a decade earlier, with the proportion higher in UK and Australia.

The medicolegal implications are significant. Between 2003-2013 in NHS UK, there were 15 claims for facial nerve injury during otological surgery, with 9 successful claims totalling 488,571 pounds in damages. Failure to use monitoring when recommended may be indefensible. Importantly, facial nerve injury often occurs when there is an unexpected change to normal middle ear anatomy - precisely when monitoring is most valuable.

## PART B: ANAESTHESIA MODIFICATIONS FOR FACIAL NERVE MONITORING

### Fundamental Principle

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) must be avoided after intubation because they prevent the facial muscle response that is essential for monitoring. The facial nerve monitor detects EMG activity in facial muscles, which is abolished by muscle relaxants.

### Recommended Anaesthesia Technique

Total intravenous anaesthesia (TIVA) is the preferred technique. The key agents are propofol for induction and maintenance (does not affect neuromuscular function) and remifentanil for analgesia (short-acting opioid that does not affect EMG). Alternative opioids include fentanyl or sufentanil. Choe et al. concluded that induction of TIVA with propofol and remifentanil provides reliable conditions for facial nerve monitoring during complex ear surgery.

**Induction Protocol:**
- Step 1: Propofol for induction
- Step 2: Short-acting NMBA ONLY for intubation
- Step 3: Allow NMBA to wear off completely before surgical start
- Step 4: Maintain with propofol and opioid throughout surgery

Short-acting muscle relaxants such as succinylcholine or rocuronium with sugammadex reversal may be used for intubation only, but must be allowed to wear off completely before monitoring begins.

### Agents to Avoid

Long-acting NMBAs (pancuronium, vecuronium) abolish the EMG response entirely. Intermediate NMBAs (atracurium, cisatracurium) have prolonged effects that interfere with monitoring. High-dose volatile agents (sevoflurane, isoflurane) may potentiate NMBAs. Repeated NMBA doses of any type will abolish monitoring capability.

### Verification of Adequate Neuromuscular Recovery

Before surgery begins, the train-of-four (TOF) ratio should be greater than 0.9 (ideally 1.0). This should be confirmed with a peripheral nerve stimulator, and the facial nerve monitor function should be tested. Intra-operatively, if the monitoring signal becomes weak or absent, the first step is to exclude residual paralysis. The anaesthetist should confirm no additional muscle relaxants have been given.

### Special Considerations

Depth of anaesthesia must be adequate to prevent patient movement. BIS (Bispectral Index) monitoring can be used if available, targeting BIS 40-60 for adequate depth. Blood pressure control may require controlled hypotension for skull base surgery, which does not affect facial nerve monitoring, but excessive hypotension should be avoided as it may affect nerve function. Normothermia should be maintained as hypothermia can affect nerve conduction.

## PART C: MANAGEMENT OF SURGICAL TRAUMA WITH LOSS OF SIGNAL

### Immediate Response to Loss of Signal

**Step 1: STOP surgical manipulation immediately.** Do not continue any dissection until the cause is identified.

**Step 2: Systematic Assessment.** Check equipment function by tapping skin over electrodes (should generate response). Verify electrode position ensuring electrodes are not dislodged. Check all connections and cables. Verify monitor settings including volume and sensitivity. Confirm with anaesthesia that no muscle relaxants have been given.

### Equipment Verification Technique

The tapping test is essential. Tap the skin overlying the two sets of subdermal electrodes. This should generate a recorded response on the monitor. A positive response confirms that electrodes are connected to the preamplifier pod, the preamplifier pod is connected to the monitor, and the monitor is switched on and functioning. Check that the volume is audible over background theatre noise.

### Assessing Nerve Status

If equipment is confirmed to be functioning, direct nerve stimulation should be performed. If response is present proximally but not distally, the injury is distal to the stimulation point. If response is present distally but not proximally, the injury is proximal to the stimulation point. No response anywhere indicates complete nerve disruption or severe injury. A weak response suggests partial injury or nerve in neuropraxia.

Visual inspection of the nerve should follow. If the nerve is intact and appears normal, it may be neuropraxia and observation is warranted. If the nerve is stretched or contused, decompress, irrigate, and observe. Partial transection requires consideration of repair. Complete transection requires repair.

### Types of Nerve Injury and Management

**Neuropraxia (Nerve Intact, Temporary Dysfunction):** This is caused by traction, compression, thermal injury from drilling or cautery, or ischaemia. Intra-operative management involves stopping all manipulation, irrigating with warm saline, waiting 15-20 minutes, and re-stimulating the nerve. The signal may recover. Prognosis is usually excellent.

**Axonotmesis (Axonal Damage, Epineurium Intact):** This results from more severe traction, crush injury, or significant thermal injury. Management involves decompressing the nerve if compressed, removing any bone fragments, irrigating, considering local steroid application, and documenting findings. Recovery is expected but may take months.

**Neurotmesis (Complete Nerve Disruption):** This is caused by transection (sharp or drill) or severe traction causing avulsion. Management involves identifying both nerve ends, assessing gap size, and deciding on repair technique.

### Intra-operative Nerve Repair Options

**Primary Neurorrhaphy (Direct Repair):** This is indicated for clean transection with no tension on repair and gap less than 3 mm after mobilization. The technique involves freshening nerve ends, mobilizing both ends if needed, approximating epineurium, using 9-0 or 10-0 nylon sutures, placing only 2-4 sutures without overtightening, and using fibrin glue as an adjunct if desired. This provides the best prognosis among repair techniques.

**Cable Grafting:** This is indicated when the gap is too large for primary repair (greater than 3 mm) or tension-free repair is not possible.

| Graft Source | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|--------------|-----------|--------------|
| Great auricular nerve (neck) | Same surgical field, good diameter match | Sensory loss neck/ear |
| Sural nerve (leg) | Long graft available | Separate incision, sensory loss foot |
| Medial antebrachial cutaneous (forearm) | Good match | Separate incision |

The technique involves harvesting a graft 10-20% longer than the gap, reversing graft orientation, and suturing without tension. Multiple cable grafts may be needed for a thick nerve. The typical outcome is House-Brackmann Grade III-IV.

**Hypoglossal-Facial Anastomosis:** This is indicated when the proximal nerve stump is not available, after failed primary repair, or in delayed presentation. Types include classic (complete XII-VII transfer), partial (split XII-VII), and jump graft (XII to VII with interpositional graft). This is not typically performed intra-operatively but is usually a delayed procedure.

### Predictors of Facial Nerve Outcome

Stimulation threshold testing is performed after tumour resection by assessing the minimum current needed to evoke a muscle response across the site of tumour dissection. Low stimulation threshold indicates good long-term facial function. High stimulation threshold indicates guarded prognosis. No response indicates poor prognosis. The test is most useful when immediate post-operative function is poor, as the majority with good immediate function will have good outcome regardless.

### Repetitive Responses - Warning Sign

Repetitive responses are repetitive depolarizations occurring after surgical manipulation has ended. They indicate nerve irritability and are an early sign of damage, especially thermal injury. This is a warning to modify technique. Management involves stopping manipulation, irrigating with cool saline, waiting for responses to settle, and proceeding more cautiously. Non-repetitive responses from direct mechanical stimulation are less concerning.

### Post-operative Management After Nerve Injury

**Immediate Care:**
- Eye care: lubricants, taping, moisture chamber
- Steroids: consider high-dose steroids (controversial)
- Documentation: detailed operative note
- Counselling: explain findings and prognosis to patient

**Follow-up:** House-Brackmann grade immediately, early recovery signs at 1 week, reassess grade at 1 month, electrical testing (ENoG) at 3 months, and final assessment at 6-12 months.

**Rehabilitation:** Eye exposure requires ophthalmology referral and gold weight consideration. Synkinesis is managed with botulinum toxin and physiotherapy. Asymmetry may require static or dynamic facial reanimation. Residual paralysis may warrant cross-facial nerve graft.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Monitoring is MANDATORY in vestibular schwannoma, skull base surgery, and parotid surgery
> 2. 49% of surgeons consider monitoring standard of care (Hu et al. survey)
> 3. 78% facial function at 1 year with monitoring vs 65% without (Harner et al.)
> 4. TIVA with propofol and remifentanil is the preferred anaesthetic technique
> 5. No muscle relaxants after intubation - they abolish EMG response
> 6. TOF ratio must be greater than 0.9 before starting surgery
> 7. Loss of signal: STOP immediately, check equipment (tap test), exclude paralysis
> 8. Neuropraxia may recover after 15-20 minutes of observation and irrigation
> 9. Primary repair if gap less than 3 mm; cable graft if gap greater than 3 mm
> 10. Great auricular nerve is preferred graft source (same field, good match)
> 11. Repetitive responses indicate nerve irritability - early warning sign
> 12. Eye care is the immediate priority in post-operative facial palsy


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Tapping test is the simple technique to verify equipment function - always perform first
> - TIVA is gold standard - propofol and remifentanil do not affect monitoring
> - Senior surgeon must personally verify equipment function before starting
> - Repetitive responses are early warning of thermal injury - stop and irrigate
> - Wait 15-20 minutes for neuropraxia - it may recover intra-operatively
> - Great auricular nerve is ideal graft - same surgical field, good size match
> - Harvest graft 10-20% longer than gap to account for shrinkage
> - Monitoring is an adjunct, not a substitute for good surgical technique
