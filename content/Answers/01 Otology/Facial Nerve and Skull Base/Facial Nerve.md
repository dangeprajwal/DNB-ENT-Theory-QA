## INTRODUCTION

The facial nerve (CN VII) is the most commonly paralysed cranial nerve. Its complex intracranial and intratemporal course renders it vulnerable to a variety of pathological processes. Understanding the surgical anatomy, clinical evaluation including topodiagnostic tests and electrophysiological assessment, and management protocols for different aetiologies of facial palsy forms a cornerstone of otological practice.

---

## PART A: SURGICAL ANATOMY OF THE FACIAL NERVE

The facial nerve has motor (SVE), sensory (GSA, SVA), and parasympathetic (GVE) components. The motor root and the nervus intermedius (nerve of Wrisberg) emerge separately from the pontomedullary sulcus and intermingle within the pons.

### Course and Segments

The facial nerve is conventionally divided into **intracranial (cisternal), intratemporal, and extratemporal** portions.

| Segment | Length | Key Features |
|---------|--------|-------------|
| **Cisternal** | ~24 mm | Courses anterolaterally through CPA cistern to porus of IAM; accompanied by vestibulocochlear nerve |
| **Meatal** | 5-12 mm | Runs through IAM from porus to fundus; lies in anterior superior quadrant, anterior to Bill's bar |
| **Labyrinthine** | 3-5 mm | **Shortest and narrowest** segment (avg diameter 0.68 mm); **only segment lacking anastomosing arterial cascades** — most vulnerable to ischaemia; most commonly injured in temporal bone fractures |
| **Tympanic (horizontal)** | 8-11 mm | Runs along superior edge of medial wall of tympanic cavity; lies above oval window niche, anterior/inferior to lateral SCC prominence |
| **Mastoid (vertical)** | 10-14 mm | **Longest** petrous segment; runs from second genu to stylomastoid foramen |
| **Extratemporal** | Variable | Enters parotid gland; divides into temporofacial and cervicofacial trunks; gives 5 terminal branches |

### Key Anatomical Landmarks

- **Bill's bar (vertical crest):** Named after William Fouts House; divides IAM fundus — facial nerve lies anterior to Bill's bar in anterior superior quadrant
- **Crista falciformis (transverse septum):** Divides IAM fundus horizontally
- **Geniculate ganglion (first genu):** Sensory ganglion at the sharp 'hairpin' turn between labyrinthine and tympanic segments; cradled by superior SCC posteriorly and cochlea anteroinferiorly
- **Greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN):** First branch of facial nerve; important landmark in middle fossa approach
- **Cochleariform process:** Consistent landmark for proximal tympanic segment
- **Pyramidal eminence:** Useful landmark for the second genu
- **Second genu:** Runs inferolateral to lateral SCC — extremely constant relationship
- **Digastric ridge:** Points to lateral and inferior aspect of mastoid segment; a line from anterior end of digastric ridge to tip of short process of incus marks the mastoid segment path
- **Chorda tympani:** Given off ~5 mm proximal to stylomastoid foramen; serves as lateral margin of facial recess

### Branches (Intratemporal)
1. **Greater petrosal nerve** — from geniculate ganglion (parasympathetic to lacrimal gland via PPG)
2. **Nerve to stapedius** — from mastoid segment behind pyramidal eminence
3. **Chorda tympani** — from mastoid segment (taste anterior 2/3 tongue + parasympathetic to submandibular ganglion)
4. **Sensory auricular branch** — from mastoid segment

### Blood Supply
- **Labyrinthine artery (from AICA):** Supplies cisternal, meatal, and labyrinthine segments
- **Petrosal branch of middle meningeal artery:** Supplies GSPN and geniculate ganglion region
- **Facial arch (stylomastoid artery + petrosal branch of MMA):** Supplies tympanic and mastoid segments
- The **labyrinthine segment** is supplied exclusively by meatal arteries and is therefore most susceptible to ischaemic damage

### Dehiscences
Developmental dehiscences of the Fallopian canal are **not uncommon in the tympanic segment**, especially around the oval niche, making the nerve vulnerable during middle ear surgery. A dehiscent or prolapsed facial nerve should always be anticipated, especially in patients with congenital ear deformities.

## PART B: FACIAL NERVE GRADING SYSTEMS

### House-Brackmann Grading System

The most widely used system, endorsed by the American Academy of Otolaryngology — Head and Neck Surgery. Proposed by House in 1983, modified by House and Brackmann.

| Grade | Degree of Injury | Definition |
|-------|-----------------|------------|
| **I** | Normal | Normal symmetrical function in all areas |
| **II** | Mild dysfunction | Slight weakness noticeable only on close inspection; complete eye closure with minimum effort; slight asymmetry of smile with maximal effort; synkinesis barely noticeable |
| **III** | Moderate dysfunction | Obvious weakness, but not disfiguring; may not be able to lift eyebrow; complete eye closure and strong but asymmetric mouth movement with maximal effort; obvious but not disfiguring synkinesis |
| **IV** | Moderately severe | Obvious disfiguring weakness; inability to lift eyebrow; incomplete eye closure and asymmetry of mouth with maximal effort; severe synkinesis, mass movement, spasm |
| **V** | Severe dysfunction | Motion barely perceptible; incomplete eye closure, slight movement of corner of mouth; synkinesis, contracture and spasm usually absent |
| **VI** | Total paralysis | No movement, loss of tone, no synkinesis, contracture or spasm |

### Limitations of House-Brackmann System
- Fusion of static and dynamic criteria contributes to inter-observer disagreement
- Risk of disagreement among examiners ranges from **13% to 44%**
- A single global grading number is inadequate — primarily reflects function of the eye
- Regional assessment (forehead, eye, nose, mouth separately) is recommended for more precise evaluation

### Other Grading Systems
- **Burres-Fisch system:** Multiple measurements of movement at different facial parts; considerable overlap with H-B grades III and IV
- **Nottingham system:** Subjective estimation of movement at several facial points; correlates better with H-B than Burres-Fisch; simpler to perform
- **Sunnybrook scale:** Includes secondary effects (synkinesis, spasm) in overall score
- **Facial Clinimetric Evaluation (FaCE)** and **Facial Disability Index (FDI):** Patient-reported outcome instruments addressing quality of life

## PART C: TOPODIAGNOSTIC TESTS

Topodiagnostic evaluation refers to functional testing of individual facial nerve branches to locate the anatomical level of dysfunction. These tests have some value but **limited correlation with precise site of damage** and **no prognostic value**.

| Test | Nerve Branch Assessed | Technique | Abnormal Result |
|------|----------------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Schirmer's test** | Greater petrosal nerve | Strips of paper placed in inferior conjunctival fornix for 5 minutes; compare lacrimation between eyes | >75% unilateral decrease in lacrimation, or bilateral decrease (<10 mm wetted for both sides at 5 min) |
| **Stapedial reflex** | Nerve to stapedius | Impedance audiometry (see Chapter 51) | Present or absent |
| **Electrogustometry** | Chorda tympani | Tongue stimulated electrically to produce metallic taste; compare two sides | Threshold compared between sides |
| **Salivary flow testing** | Chorda tympani | Wharton's ducts cannulated; salivary flow measured over time following gustatory stimulus (6% citric acid) | Reduction of 25% is considered abnormal |

### Localisation Logic
- **Lesion proximal to geniculate ganglion:** Abnormal Schirmer's test + absent stapedial reflex + abnormal taste + abnormal salivation
- **Lesion between geniculate and nerve to stapedius:** Normal lacrimation + absent stapedial reflex + abnormal taste
- **Lesion between stapedius nerve and chorda tympani:** Normal lacrimation + normal stapedial reflex + abnormal taste
- **Lesion distal to chorda tympani:** All branch tests normal; only motor weakness present

## PART D: ELECTRODIAGNOSTIC TESTS — ENOG & EMG

These are only used in patients with **complete established paralysis** — not in incomplete paresis (which has higher probability of full recovery).

### Electroneuronography (ENoG)

**Technique:**
- A **supramaximal stimulus** is delivered to the facial nerve trunk at the stylomastoid foramen
- The evoked biphasic **compound muscle action potential (CMAP)** is recorded using surface electrodes
- Response of the paralyzed side is compared with the normal side (control), expressed as a **percentage of normal**
- Reduction in CMAP amplitude correlates well with histological axonal loss

**Timing:**
- Not useful until the **4th day** of paralysis (Wallerian degeneration takes 3-4 days)
- Most useful between **day 3 and 3 weeks** of paralysis
- Not useful after 3 weeks due to desynchronization

**Prognostic Value:**
- In idiopathic palsy: **>90% degeneration within 14 days** of complete paralysis indicates probable poor recovery in **>50% of cases**
- However, **67% of patients** who reach 90% degeneration threshold also have excellent outcomes without intervention
- Fisch recommends surgical intervention within 3 weeks when traumatic injury shows **>90% amplitude reduction within 6 days**

**Limitations:**
- Cannot distinguish between axonotmesis and neurotmesis in remaining fibres
- Not useful in incomplete paralysis
- No useful information in first 72 hours
- Not useful in Ramsay Hunt syndrome (multiple sites of involvement)

### Electromyography (EMG)

**Records:** Active motor unit potentials of orbicularis oculi and oris during rest and voluntary contraction.

**EMG determines:**
1. If a nerve is **in continuity** (volitional activity recorded)
2. If there is **Wallerian degeneration** (fibrillation potentials — arise 2-3 weeks post-injury)
3. If there are **early signs of reinnervation** (polyphasic potentials — may precede clinical recovery by 6-12 weeks)

**EMG in Decision-Making for Long-Standing Paralysis:**

| EMG Finding | Interpretation | Action |
|-------------|---------------|--------|
| **Polyphasic motor unit potentials** | Regeneration occurring | Surgery NOT indicated; close follow-up |
| **Fibrillation potentials** | Denervation with viable motor end plates | Surgical exploration indicated (anastomosis, grafting, rerouting) |
| **Silence (no electrical output)** | Long-term denervation; muscle replaced by fibrous tissue | Static or dynamic facial reanimation indicated |

### ENoG vs EMG Summary Table

| Feature | ENoG | EMG |
|---------|------|-----|
| **Measurement** | Evoked CMAP compared to normal side | Active motor unit potentials after voluntary contraction |
| **When to measure** | Between 3 days and 3 weeks | Complementary to ENoG after 2 weeks; also in long-standing paralysis |
| **Use in acute paralysis** | >90% degeneration suggests poor prognosis | Presence of active motor potentials indicates good prognosis |
| **Use in long-standing paralysis** | Not useful (desynchronization) | Fibrillation = Wallerian degeneration; Polyphasic = reinnervation |

### Nerve Injury Classification

| Seddon | Sunderland | Pathology | Electrophysiology |
|--------|-----------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Neurapraxia** | Grade 1 | Transient compression, endoneurial oedema, focal demyelination | Conduction block; distal nerve normal |
| **Axonotmesis** | Grade 2 | Axons degenerate distally; endoneurium intact | Fibrillation; diminished to absent SNAP/CMAP |
| **Axonotmesis** | Grade 3 | Endoneurium disrupted | Fibrillation; absent SNAP/CMAP |
| **Axonotmesis** | Grade 4 | Perineurium disrupted | Fibrillation; absent SNAP/CMAP |
| **Neurotmesis** | Grade 5 | Epineurium disrupted (complete division) | Fibrillation; absent SNAP/CMAP |
| **Mixed** | Grade 6 (MacKinnon-Dellon) | Combination of Grades 2-4 | Mixed pattern |

- Recovery is typically **complete** after Grade 1 and 2 injuries
- Grade 3 injuries recover **partially**
- Grades 4 and 5 usually require **surgical intervention**
- Axonal regrowth proceeds at ~1 mm/day

## PART E: ELECTROCOCHLEOGRAPHY (ECoG)

### Definition
Electrocochleography is a near-field recording of the electrical potentials generated in the cochlea and auditory nerve in response to acoustic stimulation.

### Components Recorded
1. **Cochlear Microphonic (CM):** Generated by outer hair cells; mirrors the acoustic stimulus
2. **Summating Potential (SP):** DC shift generated by hair cells; reflects cochlear mechanics
3. **Action Potential (AP) / Compound Action Potential (CAP):** Generated by auditory nerve (wave I of ABR)

### Technique
- **Transtympanic (TT-ECoG):** Needle electrode placed on or through the tympanic membrane onto the promontory — provides **best signal quality**
- **Extratympanic (ET-ECoG):** Electrode placed in the EAC near the TM — non-invasive but smaller amplitudes

### SP/AP Ratio
- The **SP/AP ratio** is the key diagnostic measurement
- **Normal SP/AP ratio:** <0.40-0.45
- **Elevated SP/AP ratio (>0.45):** Suggests **endolymphatic hydrops** ([[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]])

### Clinical Applications in ENT

1. **Meniere's disease:** Elevated SP/AP ratio supports diagnosis of endolymphatic hydrops; used to monitor treatment response
2. **Intra-operative monitoring:** Real-time monitoring of cochlear function during procedures that place hearing at risk (e.g., [[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]] surgery, [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]])
3. **Threshold estimation:** ECoG can provide objective hearing threshold estimation, particularly useful in medico-legal cases
4. **Auditory neuropathy spectrum disorder (ANSD):** Presence of CM with absent or grossly abnormal AP/ABR helps diagnose ANSD
5. **Perilymphatic fistula:** May demonstrate electrophysiological changes during provocative testing

**Note:** For [[VEMP and Menieres Disease|VEMP]] (Q9), see the existing comprehensive answer in `VEMP_and_Menieres_Disease_Answer.md` which covers cVEMP, oVEMP, types, technique, and clinical applications.

## PART F: TEMPORAL BONE FRACTURES & TRAUMATIC FACIAL PALSY

### Classification of Temporal Bone Fractures

**Traditional Classification:**

| Type | Frequency | Description | FN Palsy Incidence |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Longitudinal** | Most common (~80%) | Fracture line parallel to long axis of petrous bone | 10-25% |
| **Transverse** | Less common (~20%) | Fracture line perpendicular to petrous bone | 38-50% |
| **Mixed** | Significant proportion | Combined features | Variable |

**Modern Classification (Otic Capsule):**
- **Otic capsule sparing:** Better prognosis
- **Otic capsule violating:** Patients are ~2x more likely to develop facial nerve paralysis, 4x more likely to have [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]], and 7x more likely to sustain profound hearing loss

### Facial Nerve Injury in Temporal Bone Fractures

- Complicates approximately **7% of temporal bone fractures**
- Penetrating trauma has higher incidence (~52%)
- **Site of injury:** 66% at geniculate ganglion, 20% at second genu, 8% tympanic segment, 6% mastoid portion
- 6% of geniculate ganglion lesions have a second site of trauma in the mastoid portion
- Longitudinal fractures: perigeniculate region most commonly involved
- Transverse fractures: labyrinthine and mastoid segments most commonly involved

### Immediate vs Delayed Facial Palsy

| Feature | Immediate Onset | Delayed Onset |
|---------|----------------|---------------|
| **Mechanism** | Direct trauma, impingement, possible transection | Neural oedema, haematoma, secondary ischaemia |
| **Implication** | Nerve may be directly traumatized or transected | Neural continuity maintained |
| **Prognosis** | Variable — 50% achieve complete/near-complete recovery without surgery | **Excellent prognosis** |
| **Investigation** | HRCT + ENoG mandatory | ENoG usually not necessary |
| **Management** | May require surgical exploration if ENoG criteria met | Conservative management; expected to recover (H-B I or II) |

### Investigation Protocol
1. **HRCT temporal bone:** Identifies fracture pattern, site of Fallopian canal involvement
2. **Contrast MRI:** May identify main location of nerve injury in complete palsy cases
3. **ENoG:** Performed at ~10-14 days post-injury; only necessary in:
   - Immediate complete paralysis
   - Complete paralysis with uncertain history that fails to improve
4. **EMG:** Indicated if ENoG shows no response

### Management Protocol

**A. Incomplete Palsy / Delayed Onset:**
- **No surgical intervention required** — all expected to make complete or near-complete recovery (H-B I or II)
- Steroids: Prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day for 1-3 weeks with taper
- Serial clinical observation

**B. Immediate Complete Paralysis:**
- Steroids initiated (if no contraindication from brain injury)
- ENoG at 10-14 days
- **Surgical exploration indicated if:** ENoG shows **>90% denervation within 6 days** of paralysis onset AND patient is neurologically stable
- **Timing of surgery:** Early exploration preferable; within 14 days (Chang and Cass); within 30 days (May) for superior results; recovery possible even if treated 27-90 days post-trauma (Quaranta)

**C. Surgical Approach:**
- **Longitudinal fractures with preserved hearing:** Middle fossa approach
- **Transverse/mixed fractures with preserved hearing:** Middle fossa + transmastoid exploration
- **Severe SNHL:** Translabyrinthine approach (easier, less morbidity)

**D. Surgical Technique:**
- Decompression is usually sufficient
- Severely crushed nerve: resect and primary anastomosis
- Transected nerve: end-to-end anastomosis if possible (tension-free)
- Otherwise: interposition nerve graft (great auricular or sural nerve)

**E. Late Management (>6-12 months):**
- EMG-guided decision making:
  - Polyphasic potentials → reinnervation occurring → observe
  - Fibrillation → viable motor end plates → surgical exploration for continuity
  - Silence → static/dynamic facial reanimation

## PART G: FACIAL NERVE IN MASTOID SURGERY — LANDMARKS & MANAGEMENT

### Landmarks for Identifying the Facial Nerve

| Landmark | Relevance |
|----------|-----------|
| **Lateral semicircular canal** | Second genu of FN runs inferolateral to LSCC — **extremely constant relationship** |
| **Short process of incus** | Line from anterior digastric ridge to tip of short process of incus marks mastoid segment |
| **Digastric ridge** | Points to lateral and inferior aspect of mastoid segment |
| **Cochleariform process** | Consistent landmark for proximal tympanic segment, especially when other landmarks destroyed by pathology |
| **Pyramidal eminence** | Useful landmark for second genu |
| **Fossa incudis** | Used with chorda tympani to identify nerve during facial recess approach |
| **Chorda tympani nerve** | Lateral margin of facial recess |
| **Posterior SCC** | Lies just posterior to second genu |
| **Bill's bar (in IAM)** | Separates facial nerve from superior vestibular nerve |

### Common Sites of Iatrogenic Injury
- Most common: **distal tympanic segment including second genu**
- Followed by: **mastoid segment**
- Incidence: 0.6-3.6% (0.8% in Royal College of Surgeons audit)
- Most commonly caused by failure to identify mastoid antrum properly or being impeded by overhanging tegmen

### Intra-operative Monitoring
- The **mastoid segment is least sensitive** to nerve stimulation — requires higher settings (**0.5 mA** in mastoid surgery vs 0.05-0.1 mA in CPA tumour surgery)
- Always avoid neuromuscular blockade after intubation

### Management of Immediate Post-operative Facial Nerve Paralysis

1. **If nerve was identified/integrity tested intra-operatively:**
   - Observe for a few hours — local anaesthetic effect may clear
   - Consider removing tight mastoid dressing/pack over exposed nerve

2. **If paralysis is incomplete:**
   - Start oral steroids
   - Observe clinically
   - If progression to full paralysis → consider exploration

3. **If complete paralysis noted immediately:**
   - HRCT imaging (despite possible iodine-compound artefact)
   - **Immediate exploration no longer advocated** — no demonstrated difference between early vs >7-day delayed exploration (Green et al.)
   - Most important: ensure experienced surgical colleague undertakes re-exploration or transfer to regional centre

4. **Delayed palsy post-surgery (incidence 0.9-1.4%):**
   - Aetiology unclear — possible HSV/VZV reactivation
   - Combined prednisone and acyclovir
   - Overall prognosis appears good

## PART H: FACIAL PALSY IN CHRONIC OTITIS MEDIA

### Pathophysiology
Facial nerve paralysis may complicate both acute otitis media and CSOM. Mechanisms include:
1. **Direct involvement** through Fallopian canal dehiscences or physiologic canaliculi
2. **Osteitis** of Fallopian canal with bone erosion and nerve involvement
3. **Inflammatory oedema** → compression → secondary thrombosis of vasa nervorum → ischaemia and infarction
4. **Demyelination** caused by bacterial toxins

### Management in Acute Otitis Media
- **Mainstay:** Antibiotic therapy
- Steroids may be used (efficacy not supported by studies)
- Myringotomy and ventilation tube insertion when spontaneous perforation is absent
- Prognosis generally good

### Management in Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (Squamosal Type)
- **Mastoid exploration and facial nerve decompression is ESSENTIAL**
- CT scan to delineate site of Fallopian canal erosion
- Surgical exploration addresses:
  - Removal of [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]]/granulation tissue compressing the nerve
  - Decompression of the nerve within the Fallopian canal
  - Assessment of nerve integrity
- If nerve is intact but compressed: decompression is sufficient
- If >50% circumference disrupted: repair with direct suture or inlay graft
- If transected: end-to-end anastomosis or interposition grafting

### Key Differences: AOM vs CSOM

| Feature | AOM-related | CSOM-related |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| Mechanism | Inflammatory oedema, toxins | Cholesteatoma erosion, osteitis |
| Urgency | Medical management first | Surgical exploration essential |
| Approach | Myringotomy + antibiotics + steroids | Mastoidectomy + FN decompression |
| Prognosis | Generally good | Depends on degree of nerve damage |

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> - The **labyrinthine segment** (3-5 mm) is the shortest, narrowest, and most vulnerable segment — sole arterial supply from meatal arteries
> - The **House-Brackmann** grading system (I-VI) is the most widely used but has limitations — regional assessment recommended
> - **Topodiagnostic tests** (Schirmer's, stapedial reflex, taste, salivary flow) help localise the lesion but have no prognostic value
> - **ENoG** is the most valuable prognostic test: >90% degeneration within 14 days in complete paralysis indicates poor prognosis in >50%
> - **EMG** complements ENoG: fibrillation potentials = viable end plates; polyphasic potentials = regeneration; silence = irreversible
> - Temporal bone fractures cause FN palsy in ~7% of cases; **66% at geniculate ganglion**
> - **Incomplete/delayed onset palsy** has excellent prognosis — no surgery needed
> - **Immediate complete palsy** with >90% ENoG degeneration within 6 days → consider surgical exploration
> - In **mastoid surgery**, the lateral SCC is the most reliable landmark for the second genu
> - In **CSOM with facial palsy**, mastoid exploration and decompression is essential
> - **ECoG** (SP/AP ratio) is primarily used for Meniere's disease diagnosis and intraoperative cochlear monitoring


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. **Progressive facial palsy over >3 weeks** or incomplete palsy not recovering after 3-6 weeks → suspect underlying **neoplasm**
> 2. **Ipsilateral recurrent facial palsy** — tumour should be suspected (6/20 cases in May's series)
> 3. In temporal bone fractures, **67% of patients reaching 90% ENoG threshold** still have excellent outcomes without surgery — the threshold is not absolute
> 4. The **cochleariform process** is the most reliable landmark when other structures are destroyed by pathology
> 5. **Immediate post-operative exploration is no longer justified** — observe and plan carefully
> 6. **Complete nerve transection** accounts for only about 14% of traumatic facial palsy cases
> 7. The positions of the tympanic anulus and mastoid segment of the facial nerve **change during childhood** — paediatric anatomy differs
> 8. In **Ramsay Hunt syndrome**, prognosis is worse than idiopathic palsy — only 10% recover completely after complete loss without treatment
