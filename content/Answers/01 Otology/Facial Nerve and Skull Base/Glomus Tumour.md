# Glomus Tumour (paraganglioma of the Temporal Bone)

**Questions Covered:**
- **Q35 (June 2021):** Enumerate the various tumors of the temporal bone. Describe the varied presentation and management of Glomus jugulare.
- **Q118 (May 2025):** Describe the staging of glomus tumour and surgical steps to excise glomus jugulare.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 99 (Paragangliomas of the Temporal Bone), Chapter 100 (Vascular Assessment and Management)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Paragangliomas (previously termed glomus tumours or chemodectomas) are the most common neoplasm of the middle ear and the most common tumour of the jugular foramen. They arise from paraganglia — clusters of neural crest-derived cells associated with the autonomic nervous system. In the temporal bone, these paraganglia are found along the course of Jacobson's nerve (tympanic branch of CN IX) on the promontory and along Arnold's nerve (auricular branch of CN X) in the adventitia of the jugular bulb. Paragangliomas are highly vascular, slow-growing, locally destructive tumours. Their rich blood supply, proximity to major neurovascular structures, and capacity for extensive skull base invasion make them among the most challenging tumours in otological and skull base surgery. Approximately 10% are functional (catecholamine-secreting), and familial forms are linked to succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) gene mutations.

## PART A: TUMOURS OF THE TEMPORAL BONE

### Classification

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Benign** | Paraganglioma (glomus tumour), osteoma, exostosis, adenoma, meningioma, facial nerve schwannoma, haemangioma, endolymphatic sac tumour |
| **Malignant Primary** | Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of temporal bone, adenoid cystic carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, rhabdomyosarcoma (children), chondrosarcoma |
| **Malignant Secondary** | Metastatic (breast, lung, renal, prostate), direct extension from parotid tumour |
| **Other** | Cholesterol granuloma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis (children) |

### Key Points

- **Paraganglioma** is the most common primary neoplasm of the middle ear and the most common tumour of the jugular foramen
- **SCC** is the most common primary malignant tumour of the temporal bone; however, it is extremely rare (incidence ~1-6 per million per year)
- **[[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]]** arises at the CPA/IAM and is covered separately (see `Vestibular_Schwannoma_Comprehensive.md`)
- **Endolymphatic sac tumour** is associated with von Hippel-Lindau disease; presents with SNHL and destructive petrous bone lesion
- **Rhabdomyosarcoma** is the most common malignant temporal bone tumour in children
- **Metastatic deposits** to the temporal bone most commonly originate from breast, lung, renal, and prostate primaries

## PART B: PARAGANGLIOMA — TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION

### Types of Temporal Bone and Head-Neck Paragangliomas

Four principal types of paraganglioma arise in and around the temporal bone and upper neck, each originating from a distinct cluster of paraganglia and presenting with characteristic clinical features.

**Glomus tympanicum** arises from paraganglia along Jacobson's nerve (tympanic branch of CN IX) on the promontory. It is the most common middle ear tumour and remains confined to the middle ear cleft in the majority of cases, producing early symptoms of pulsatile [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and conductive hearing loss.

**Glomus jugulare** originates from paraganglia along Arnold's nerve (auricular branch of CN X) or within the adventitia of the jugular bulb. It is the most common lesion of the jugular foramen and characteristically erodes the jugular fossa and surrounding bone, often involving lower cranial nerves as it expands.

**Glomus vagale** develops from paraganglia of the inferior (nodose) ganglion of the vagus nerve. It typically presents as a [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] mass, and vagal palsy is a common finding at presentation given the intimate relationship of the tumour to its nerve of origin.

**Carotid body tumour** arises from paraganglia at the carotid bifurcation. It characteristically presents as a painless, slowly enlarging neck mass that splays the internal and external carotid arteries, producing the classic "lyre sign" on angiography.

### Pathology

Paragangliomas are composed of two cell types. The **chief cells (Type I)** contain catecholamine-secreting granules and form characteristic "Zellballen" nests — the histological hallmark of paraganglioma. These cell balls are surrounded by a rich capillary network and stain positive for **chromogranin A** and **synaptophysin** on immunohistochemistry. The **sustentacular cells (Type II)** are supporting cells that surround the Zellballen nests and are positive for **S-100 protein**.

Paragangliomas are generally benign tumours. The malignancy rate is approximately 2-4%, and importantly, malignancy is defined by the presence of metastases rather than by any specific histological features — there are no reliable histological criteria to distinguish benign from malignant paragangliomas.

### Genetics

The "10% rule" provides a useful mnemonic: approximately 10% of paragangliomas are bilateral, 10% are multicentric, and 10% are familial. Familial paragangliomas are associated with mutations in the **succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)** gene complex:

- **SDHD** is the most commonly mutated gene. It follows autosomal dominant inheritance with paternal imprinting and carries a high rate of multifocal disease
- **SDHB** mutations are associated with a higher rate of malignancy and extra-adrenal phaeochromocytomas, making this the most clinically significant mutation to identify
- **SDHC** is the least common of the major SDH mutations and predominantly produces head and neck paragangliomas
- **SDHA** and **SDHAF2** represent rarer associations

Genetic screening is recommended for all patients with paragangliomas, particularly those with multicentric, bilateral, or familial tumours.

## PART C: STAGING SYSTEMS

### Fisch Classification (Most Widely Used)

The Fisch classification is the most commonly used staging system for temporal bone paragangliomas and guides surgical decision-making.

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **A** | Tumour limited to the middle ear cleft (glomus tympanicum) |
| **B** | Tumour limited to the tympanomastoid area; no infralabyrinthine compartment involvement |
| **C1** | Tumour involving the infralabyrinthine compartment of the temporal bone with erosion of the carotid canal |
| **C2** | Tumour invading the vertical portion of the carotid canal |
| **C3** | Tumour invading the horizontal portion of the carotid canal (extensive bony destruction) |
| **C4** | Tumour extending into the foramen lacerum and cavernous sinus |
| **De1** | Intradural extension less than or equal to 2 cm |
| **De2** | Intradural extension greater than 2 cm |

**Key Interpretation:**
- **Class A-B:** Limited disease; good surgical outcome with complete resection
- **Class C:** Carotid canal involvement; requires carotid artery assessment and potential management
- **Class De:** Intracranial extension; requires combined neurosurgical approach

### Glasscock-Jackson Classification

| Type | Glomus Tympanicum | Glomus Jugulare |
|------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **I** | Small mass limited to the promontory | Small tumour involving jugular bulb, middle ear, and mastoid |
| **II** | Tumour completely filling the middle ear space | Tumour extending under the internal auditory canal; may have intracranial extension |
| **III** | Tumour extending into the mastoid | Tumour extending into the petrous apex; may have intracranial extension |
| **IV** | Tumour extending through the tympanic membrane into the external auditory canal | Tumour extending beyond the petrous apex into the clivus or infratemporal fossa; may have intracranial extension |

### Comparison of Staging Systems

The **Fisch classification** is preferred for surgical planning as it specifically addresses carotid canal involvement and intracranial extension, both of which have critical implications for operative strategy. The **Glasscock-Jackson classification** is simpler and more useful for clinical communication, but provides less granular information to guide the choice of surgical approach.

## PART D: CLINICAL PRESENTATION

### Symptoms

The clinical presentation of temporal bone paragangliomas reflects their origin in the middle ear or jugular foramen and their progressive growth pattern.

- **Pulsatile tinnitus** is the characteristic presenting symptom, present in approximately 50% of patients. It is synchronous with the heartbeat and arises from the highly vascular nature of the tumour. Patients often describe a rhythmic whooshing or pounding in the affected ear
- **Hearing loss** is present in up to 90% of cases. It is initially conductive, resulting from the mass effect of the tumour within the middle ear. As the disease progresses and invades the otic capsule or cochlear nerve, the hearing loss may become sensorineural or mixed in character
- **Aural fullness** manifests as a sense of blockage or pressure in the affected ear, similar to the sensation of fluid in the middle ear
- **Otorrhagia and otorrhoea** — bleeding from the ear or persistent discharge — may occur, particularly with larger tumours. These symptoms may delay diagnosis if incorrectly attributed to chronic otitis media
- **Vertigo and imbalance** develop when the tumour involves the inner ear or vestibular nerve, indicating more advanced disease
- **Cranial nerve palsies** occur with jugulare extension and reflect the intimate relationship of the tumour to the lower cranial nerves at the jugular foramen (see below)

### Signs

The otoscopic and clinical signs of paraganglioma are distinctive when the clinician is alert to them.

- **"Rising sun" sign:** A red or blue pulsatile mass is seen rising from the hypotympanum through an intact tympanic membrane. This is the classic otoscopic appearance and reflects the tumour's origin on the promontory or within the jugular bulb
- **Brown's sign:** Blanching of the mass occurs with positive pressure on pneumatic otoscopy using a Siegle's speculum. This sign is considered pathognomonic for paraganglioma and distinguishes it from other vascular middle ear lesions
- **Pulsatile bruit:** An audible bruit may be detected on auscultation over the mastoid or periauricular area, reflecting the high-flow vascularity of the tumour
- **Polyp in the external auditory canal:** In advanced tumours that have eroded through the tympanic membrane, a fleshy, bleeding polypoid mass may be seen in the ear canal

### Cranial Nerve Involvement in Glomus Jugulare

[[Facial Nerve|Facial nerve palsy]] occurs in 11-17% of jugular paragangliomas. Lower cranial nerve palsies are characteristic of glomus jugulare and produce recognized clinical syndromes as the tumour expands within and beyond the jugular foramen.

**Vernet syndrome (jugular foramen syndrome)** results from involvement of cranial nerves IX, X, and XI as they pass through the jugular foramen. The patient presents with dysphagia and loss of the gag reflex (glossopharyngeal nerve palsy), [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal cord palsy]] causing hoarseness (vagus nerve palsy), and weakness of the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles (spinal accessory nerve palsy). This is the most common cranial nerve syndrome associated with glomus jugulare.

**Collet-Sicard syndrome** occurs when cranial nerve XII is additionally involved, adding tongue deviation to the affected side (hypoglossal nerve palsy) to all the features of Vernet syndrome. The hypoglossal nerve lies in close proximity to the jugular foramen as it passes through its own hypoglossal canal, and is vulnerable to compression by tumours expanding inferiorly.

**Villaret syndrome** represents the most extensive pattern of nerve involvement, combining the features of Collet-Sicard syndrome with involvement of the ipsilateral cervical sympathetic chain. This produces ipsilateral Horner's syndrome — miosis (constricted pupil), ptosis (drooping eyelid), and anhidrosis (absence of sweating on the affected side of the face) — in addition to the lower cranial nerve palsies.

### Functional Paragangliomas

Approximately 10% of paragangliomas are functionally active, secreting catecholamines including noradrenaline, adrenaline, and dopamine. These patients may present with episodic headaches, palpitations, hypertension, flushing, and diaphoresis — a clinical picture similar to phaeochromocytoma.

**All patients must be screened pre-operatively** with 24-hour urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), urinary metanephrines, or plasma free metanephrines. Failure to identify a functional tumour before surgery risks a life-threatening intra-operative hypertensive crisis. When a functional tumour is confirmed, the patient requires pre-operative alpha-blockade with phenoxybenzamine for a minimum of 10-14 days, followed by beta-blockade, before any surgical intervention is undertaken.

## PART E: INVESTIGATIONS

### Imaging

A multimodal imaging approach is essential for the accurate diagnosis, staging, and surgical planning of temporal bone paragangliomas.

**High-resolution CT (HRCT) of the temporal bone** is the primary imaging modality for assessing bony involvement. The characteristic finding is permeative "moth-eaten" bone destruction. Erosion of the caroticojugular spine — the thin plate of bone separating the carotid canal from the jugular fossa — is the hallmark early radiological finding for glomus jugulare and distinguishes it from glomus tympanicum. HRCT also demonstrates widening of the jugular foramen, the extent of bony erosion, and the status of the otic capsule, facial nerve canal, and carotid canal, all of which are critical for surgical planning.

**MRI with gadolinium enhancement** is superior for defining soft tissue extent. The characteristic finding is a "salt and pepper" appearance on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences. The "pepper" component represents signal voids from high-flow vessels within the tumour, while the "salt" component arises from microhaemorrhage and areas of slow flow. MRI is indispensable for assessing intracranial extension, the relationship of the tumour to the dura and brainstem, and the involvement of cranial nerves.

**Catheter angiography (digital subtraction angiography)** is mandatory for glomus jugulare. It identifies the tumour blood supply, feeding arteries, flow dynamics, and venous drainage pattern, and demonstrates the characteristic dense "tumour blush" that is pathognomonic of paraganglioma. Critically, catheter angiography enables simultaneous pre-operative embolization, which is performed in the same session.

**CT or MR angiography** is essential for assessing the Circle of Willis and collateral cerebral circulation before any procedure that may compromise the internal carotid artery. It also evaluates the patency of contralateral venous drainage (contralateral sigmoid sinus and internal jugular vein), which must be confirmed before ipsilateral sigmoid sinus or IJV ligation.

### Arterial Blood Supply of Paragangliomas

All paragangliomas of the temporal bone are primarily supplied by branches of the **ascending pharyngeal artery**. The specific feeding vessel varies by tumour type: glomus tympanicum is supplied by the inferior tympanic branch, glomus jugulare by the neuromeningeal branch, and glomus vagale by the musculospinal branch of the ascending pharyngeal artery.

Larger tumours recruit additional blood supply from the occipital artery, internal maxillary artery, posterior auricular artery, and the caroticotympanic branch of the internal carotid artery. Multicompartmental blood supply is the rule for large glomus jugulare tumours, and each feeding vessel must be individually addressed during embolization.

### Biochemical and Genetic Screening

All patients require biochemical screening to exclude a functional tumour: 24-hour urinary VMA, urinary catecholamines, and plasma free metanephrines should be measured. Genetic screening for SDH gene mutations (SDHB, SDHC, SDHD) is recommended for all patients, with particular emphasis on those with multicentric, bilateral, or familial tumours.

## PART F: MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

### Overview of Management Strategies

Management of temporal bone paragangliomas must be individualized based on tumour extent, patient age and fitness, functional status of the tumour, and the pattern of cranial nerve involvement.

**Observation (wait and scan)** is appropriate for elderly or comorbid patients, small and slow-growing tumours, asymptomatic patients, and those who decline active treatment. Paragangliomas are slow-growing tumours with an average growth rate of approximately 1 mm per year. Patients managed conservatively undergo serial MRI every 6-12 months initially, then annually if the tumour remains stable. Monitoring for new cranial nerve deficits or accelerated tumour growth is essential.

**Surgery** is undertaken with curative intent and is generally preferred for younger patients, those with progressive cranial neuropathy, and those with secretory tumours. Complete surgical excision offers the only chance of cure, but carries risks of cranial nerve injury and significant morbidity, particularly for advanced tumours.

**[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]]** is used for tumour control (growth arrest rather than cure). It is the preferred option for elderly or medically unfit patients, residual or recurrent tumour after surgery, bilateral disease (to preserve function on at least one side), and tumours with extensive intracranial extension where surgery carries unacceptable risk. The tumour remains in situ but is prevented from further growth.

**Combined surgery and radiotherapy** involves subtotal resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy for residual disease, representing a pragmatic approach when complete resection would carry unacceptable morbidity.

### Pre-operative Embolization

Pre-operative embolization is a critical step in the management of glomus jugulare tumours, significantly reducing intra-operative blood loss from approximately 2000 mL to less than 1000 mL and facilitating surgical dissection by devascularizing the tumour.

Embolization is performed **24-48 hours before surgery** — not longer, as neovascularization begins rapidly and the benefit is lost. The technique involves selective catheterization of each feeding artery; because large tumours have multicompartmental blood supply, separate injections into each feeding vessel are required.

Available embolic agents include:
- **Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles** — the traditional agent
- **Calibrated microspheres** (spherical particles) — particles must be larger than 150 micrometres to avoid passage into the vasa nervorum, which would cause cranial nerve injury
- **n-Butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA)** — a liquid embolic agent
- **Onyx (ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer)** — a liquid embolic agent

Risks of embolization include cranial nerve palsy (from inadvertent embolization of vasa nervorum), stroke, and post-embolization swelling.

### Surgical Approach by Fisch Class

The choice of surgical approach is guided by the Fisch classification and the extent of tumour involvement.

**Class A (glomus tympanicum)** tumours are managed via a transcanal or tympanotomy approach. The tumour is removed from the promontory with excellent results and low morbidity. This straightforward surgery should not be confused with the extensive approach required for glomus jugulare.

**Class B** tumours require an extended facial recess approach (posterior tympanotomy) or hypotympanotomy. A cortical mastoidectomy may be necessary to achieve adequate exposure.

**Class C1-C2 (glomus jugulare)** tumours are managed via the **infratemporal fossa approach Type A (Fisch approach)**, which is the standard and workhorse approach for glomus jugulare. This approach involves anterior rerouting of the facial nerve, removal of the external auditory canal skin with blind sac closure of the EAC, complete mastoidectomy with skeletonization of the sigmoid sinus, exposure and control of the internal carotid artery, and removal of tumour from the jugular bulb, middle ear, and infralabyrinthine compartment. The approach results in conductive hearing loss (due to EAC closure) and temporary facial paresis from nerve rerouting, which typically recovers to House-Brackmann Grade III.

**Class C3-C4** tumours additionally require carotid artery management. Balloon test occlusion (BTO) of the ICA is performed pre-operatively. If the BTO is tolerated (adequate collateral circulation), permanent ICA occlusion may be performed intra-operatively. If the BTO is not tolerated, a saphenous vein bypass graft is used to maintain cerebral perfusion.

**Class De1-De2** tumours with intracranial extension require a combined infratemporal fossa approach with neurosurgical posterior fossa or middle fossa craniotomy.

### Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy may be delivered as **stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS)** using Gamma Knife or CyberKnife (a single high-dose fraction, suitable for tumours less than 3 cm), or as **fractionated radiotherapy** using conventional external beam or intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) for larger tumours. The tumour control rate is 90-95%, representing growth arrest rather than cure.

Indications for radiotherapy include:
- Residual tumour after subtotal resection
- Recurrent tumour
- Bilateral paragangliomas (to preserve function on at least one side)
- Elderly patients or those unfit for surgery
- Extensive intracranial extension (Fisch De2) where complete resection carries high morbidity
- Patient preference

## PART G: SURGICAL STEPS FOR GLOMUS JUGULARE EXCISION — INFRATEMPORAL FOSSA APPROACH TYPE A

### Step 1: Pre-operative Workup
1. Obtain HRCT temporal bone and gadolinium-enhanced MRI to delineate tumour extent
2. Perform catheter angiography with pre-operative embolization 24-48 hours before surgery
3. Perform balloon test occlusion (BTO) of the ICA if Fisch C2 or above
4. Assess contralateral venous drainage (contralateral sigmoid sinus/IJV must be patent before ipsilateral ligation)
5. Perform biochemical screening for catecholamine secretion (24-hour urinary VMA/metanephrines)
6. If functional tumour is confirmed: institute alpha-blockade (phenoxybenzamine) followed by beta-blockade pre-operatively
7. Cross-match blood and inform anaesthesia team regarding potential for significant blood loss

### Step 2: Patient Positioning
1. Administer general anaesthesia with continuous facial nerve monitoring (EMG electrodes placed in orbicularis oculi and orbicularis oris)
2. Arrange lower cranial nerve monitoring (X, XI, XII) if available
3. Position supine with head turned to the opposite side
4. Shave and prepare the periauricular and neck regions

### Step 3: Incision
1. Make a C-shaped post-auricular incision extending inferiorly into the neck along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle
2. Elevate anterior and posterior skin flaps
3. Identify and preserve the greater auricular nerve

### Step 4: [[Neck Dissection|Neck Dissection]]
1. Identify and control the internal jugular vein (IJV) below the level of the tumour
2. Ligate and divide the IJV below the tumour (establishing proximal control)
3. Identify and preserve cranial nerves in the neck:
   - **Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI):** located deep to the posterior belly of digastric
   - **Vagus nerve (CN X):** within the carotid sheath, between the ICA/CCA and the IJV
   - **Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII):** crosses the ICA and ECA superficially
4. Identify the internal carotid artery and common carotid artery for vascular control if needed
5. Identify the posterior belly of the digastric muscle as a key surgical landmark

### Step 5: Cortical Mastoidectomy
1. Perform a complete cortical mastoidectomy
2. Identify key landmarks: tegmen tympani, sigmoid sinus, lateral semicircular canal, and short process of incus
3. Thin the posterior canal wall
4. Expose the mastoid segment of the facial nerve from the second genu to the stylomastoid foramen
5. Identify the digastric ridge as a guide to the stylomastoid foramen

### Step 6: Facial Nerve Management
1. Identify the facial nerve at the stylomastoid foramen
2. Skeletonize the facial nerve through its mastoid (vertical) segment
3. Perform **anterior rerouting of the facial nerve:**
   - Dissect the nerve from the stylomastoid foramen proximally to the geniculate ganglion
   - Transect the greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN)
   - Mobilize the nerve anteriorly out of its fallopian canal
   - Reroute the nerve to lie anteriorly, protecting it during tumour removal
4. Remove the external auditory canal skin and perform blind sac closure of the EAC (oversew the cartilaginous canal to create a closed cavity)
5. Remove the tympanic membrane and ossicles to gain access to the middle ear and hypotympanum

### Step 7: Sigmoid Sinus and Jugular Bulb Exposure
1. Skeletonize the sigmoid sinus from the sinodural angle to the jugular bulb
2. Pack and ligate the sigmoid sinus above the level of tumour involvement using extraluminal packing (Surgicel) and suture ligation
3. Confirm that the sigmoid sinus is controlled distally by the IJV ligation performed in the neck
4. Open the sigmoid sinus and jugular bulb to expose the tumour

### Step 8: Tumour Removal
1. Dissect the tumour from the jugular bulb, infralabyrinthine compartment, and middle ear
2. Remove piecemeal or en bloc depending on tumour size and adherence to surrounding structures
3. Perform careful dissection from the lower cranial nerves (IX, X, XI) at the pars nervosa of the jugular foramen
4. For Fisch C tumours: dissect tumour from the carotid artery wall, following the subadventitial plane carefully
5. For Fisch C3/C4: if the ICA is encased and BTO was tolerated, the ICA may be permanently occluded and divided; if BTO was not tolerated, perform saphenous vein bypass graft or undertake subtotal resection with planned post-operative radiotherapy
6. Ensure meticulous haemostasis throughout using bipolar cautery, Surgicel, and bone wax

### Step 9: Manage Intracranial Extension (If Present)
1. For Fisch De tumours: employ a combined approach with the neurosurgical team
2. Perform posterior fossa craniotomy or middle fossa craniotomy depending on the direction of intracranial extension
3. Excise intradural tumour after dural opening
4. Achieve watertight dural closure with a fascial graft

### Step 10: Cavity Obliteration and Closure
1. Obliterate the surgical cavity with an autologous abdominal fat graft
2. Ensure the fat graft fills the mastoid cavity, jugular bulb defect, and middle ear space
3. Confirm the blind sac closure of the EAC is watertight
4. Perform layered wound closure: periosteum, subcutaneous tissue, and skin
5. Apply a pressure dressing

### Step 11: Post-operative Care
1. Transfer to ICU for monitoring for 24-48 hours
2. Monitor for cranial nerve deficits (facial nerve and lower cranial nerves)
3. Assess swallowing function before allowing oral intake (risk of aspiration from CN X dysfunction)
4. Counsel the patient that facial nerve function typically recovers to House-Brackmann Grade III after anterior rerouting
5. Arrange serial MRI for surveillance of residual or recurrent disease

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. **Do not biopsy** a red/blue pulsatile middle ear mass — paragangliomas are highly vascular and biopsy can cause life-threatening haemorrhage. Diagnosis is radiological (CT/MRI/angiography), not histological
> 2. The differential diagnosis of a red middle ear mass includes paraganglioma, aberrant ICA, high/dehiscent jugular bulb, and middle ear cholesterol granuloma — all of which bleed profusely if biopsied inadvertently
> 3. Glomus tympanicum (Fisch A) can be completely excised via a transcanal approach with minimal morbidity; do not confuse this with the extensive surgery needed for glomus jugulare
> 4. Contralateral sigmoid sinus/IJV patency must be confirmed before ipsilateral ligation — bilateral sigmoid sinus occlusion causes fatal raised intracranial pressure
> 5. After anterior rerouting of the facial nerve, most patients recover to HB Grade III; this expected morbidity must be discussed with the patient pre-operatively
> 6. The "10% rule" is a useful mnemonic: 10% bilateral, 10% multicentric, 10% familial, 10% functional, 10% malignant (approximate figures)
> 7. SDHB mutation carriers have a higher risk of malignancy and should be followed more closely
