# Glomus Tumour (paraganglioma of the Temporal Bone)

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - - **Q35 (June 2021):** Enumerate the various tumors of the temporal bone. Describe the varied presentation and management of Glomus jugulare.
> - Q118 (May 2025):** Describe the staging of glomus tumour and surgical steps to excise glomus jugulare.
> - Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 99 (Paragangliomas of the Temporal Bone), Chapter 100 (Vascular Assessment and Management)
> - ## INTRODUCTION
> - ## PART A: TUMOURS OF THE TEMPORAL BONE *(Addresses Q35)
> - | Paraganglioma (glomus tumour), osteoma, exostosis, adenoma, meningioma, facial nerve schwannoma, haemangioma, endolymphatic sac tumour |
> - | Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) of temporal bone, adenoid cystic carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, rhabdomyosarcoma (children), chondrosarcoma |
> - | Metastatic (breast, lung, renal, prostate), direct extension from parotid tumour |
> - | Cholesterol granuloma, Langerhans cell histiocytosis (children) |
> - Paraganglioma** is the most common primary neoplasm of the middle ear and the most common tumour of the jugular foramen
> - SCC** is the most common primary malignant tumour of the temporal bone; however, it is extremely rare (incidence ~1-6 per million per year)
> - [[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]]** arises at the CPA/IAM and is covered separately (see `Vestibular_Schwannoma_Comprehensive.md`)
> - Endolymphatic sac tumour** is associated with von Hippel-Lindau disease; presents with SNHL and destructive petrous bone lesion
> - Rhabdomyosarcoma** is the most common malignant temporal bone tumour in children
> - Metastatic deposits** to the temporal bone most commonly originate from breast, lung, renal, and prostate primaries
> - ## PART B: PARAGANGLIOMA — TYPES AND CLASSIFICATION *(Addresses Q35, Q118)
> - arises from paraganglia along Jacobson's nerve (tympanic branch of CN IX) on the promontory. It is the most common middle ear tumour and remains confined to the middle ear cleft in the majority of cases, producing early symptoms of pulsatile [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and conductive hearing loss.
> - originates from paraganglia along Arnold's nerve (auricular branch of CN X) or within the adventitia of the jugular bulb. It is the most common lesion of the jugular foramen and characteristically erodes the jugular fossa and surrounding bone, often involving lower cranial nerves as it expands.
> - develops from paraganglia of the inferior (nodose) ganglion of the vagus nerve. It typically presents as a [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] mass, and vagal palsy is a common finding at presentation given the intimate relationship of the tumour to its nerve of origin.
> - arises from paraganglia at the carotid bifurcation. It characteristically presents as a painless, slowly enlarging neck mass that splays the internal and external carotid arteries, producing the classic "lyre sign" on angiography.
> - contain catecholamine-secreting granules and form characteristic "Zellballen" nests — the histological hallmark of paraganglioma. These cell balls are surrounded by a rich capillary network and stain positive for **chromogranin A** and **synaptophysin** on immunohistochemistry. The **sustentacular cells (Type II)** are supporting cells that surround the Zellballen nests and are positive for **S-100 protein**.
> - gene complex:
> - SDHD** is the most commonly mutated gene. It follows autosomal dominant inheritance with paternal imprinting and carries a high rate of multifocal disease
> - SDHB** mutations are associated with a higher rate of malignancy and extra-adrenal phaeochromocytomas, making this the most clinically significant mutation to identify
> - SDHC** is the least common of the major SDH mutations and predominantly produces head and neck paragangliomas
> - SDHA** and **SDHAF2** represent rarer associations
> - ## PART C: STAGING SYSTEMS *(Addresses Q118)
> - | Tumour limited to the middle ear cleft (glomus tympanicum) |
> - | Tumour limited to the tympanomastoid area; no infralabyrinthine compartment involvement |
> - | Tumour involving the infralabyrinthine compartment of the temporal bone with erosion of the carotid canal |
> - | Tumour invading the vertical portion of the carotid canal |
> - | Tumour invading the horizontal portion of the carotid canal (extensive bony destruction) |
> - | Tumour extending into the foramen lacerum and cavernous sinus |
> - | Intradural extension less than or equal to 2 cm |
> - | Intradural extension greater than 2 cm |
> - - **Class A-B:** Limited disease; good surgical outcome with complete resection
> - Class C:** Carotid canal involvement; requires carotid artery assessment and potential management
> - Class De:** Intracranial extension; requires combined neurosurgical approach
> - | Small mass limited to the promontory | Small tumour involving jugular bulb, middle ear, and mastoid |
> - | Tumour completely filling the middle ear space | Tumour extending under the internal auditory canal; may have intracranial extension |
> - | Tumour extending into the mastoid | Tumour extending into the petrous apex; may have intracranial extension |
> - | Tumour extending through the tympanic membrane into the external auditory canal | Tumour extending beyond the petrous apex into the clivus or infratemporal fossa; may have intracranial extension |
> - is preferred for surgical planning as it specifically addresses carotid canal involvement and intracranial extension, both of which have critical implications for operative strategy. The **Glasscock-Jackson classification** is simpler and more useful for clinical communication, but provides less granular information to guide the choice of surgical approach.
> - ## PART D: CLINICAL PRESENTATION *(Addresses Q35)
> - Pulsatile tinnitus** is the characteristic presenting symptom, present in approximately 50% of patients. It is synchronous with the heartbeat and arises from the highly vascular nature of the tumour. Patients often describe a rhythmic whooshing or pounding in the affected ear
> - Hearing loss** is present in up to 90% of cases. It is initially conductive, resulting from the mass effect of the tumour within the middle ear. As the disease progresses and invades the otic capsule or cochlear nerve, the hearing loss may become sensorineural or mixed in character
> - Aural fullness** manifests as a sense of blockage or pressure in the affected ear, similar to the sensation of fluid in the middle ear
> - Otorrhagia and otorrhoea** — bleeding from the ear or persistent discharge — may occur, particularly with larger tumours. These symptoms may delay diagnosis if incorrectly attributed to chronic otitis media
> - Vertigo and imbalance** develop when the tumour involves the inner ear or vestibular nerve, indicating more advanced disease
> - Cranial nerve palsies** occur with jugulare extension and reflect the intimate relationship of the tumour to the lower cranial nerves at the jugular foramen (see below)
> - "Rising sun" sign:** A red or blue pulsatile mass is seen rising from the hypotympanum through an intact tympanic membrane. This is the classic otoscopic appearance and reflects the tumour's origin on the promontory or within the jugular bulb
> - Brown's sign:** Blanching of the mass occurs with positive pressure on pneumatic otoscopy using a Siegle's speculum. This sign is considered pathognomonic for paraganglioma and distinguishes it from other vascular middle ear lesions
> - Pulsatile bruit:** An audible bruit may be detected on auscultation over the mastoid or periauricular area, reflecting the high-flow vascularity of the tumour
> - Polyp in the external auditory canal:** In advanced tumours that have eroded through the tympanic membrane, a fleshy, bleeding polypoid mass may be seen in the ear canal
> - results from involvement of cranial nerves IX, X, and XI as they pass through the jugular foramen. The patient presents with dysphagia and loss of the gag reflex (glossopharyngeal nerve palsy), [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal cord palsy]] causing hoarseness (vagus nerve palsy), and weakness of the trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles (spinal accessory nerve palsy). This is the most common cranial nerve syndrome associated with glomus jugulare.
> - occurs when cranial nerve XII is additionally involved, adding tongue deviation to the affected side (hypoglossal nerve palsy) to all the features of Vernet syndrome. The hypoglossal nerve lies in close proximity to the jugular foramen as it passes through its own hypoglossal canal, and is vulnerable to compression by tumours expanding inferiorly.
> - represents the most extensive pattern of nerve involvement, combining the features of Collet-Sicard syndrome with involvement of the ipsilateral cervical sympathetic chain. This produces ipsilateral Horner's syndrome — miosis (constricted pupil), ptosis (drooping eyelid), and anhidrosis (absence of sweating on the affected side of the face) — in addition to the lower cranial nerve palsies.
> - with 24-hour urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), urinary metanephrines, or plasma free metanephrines. Failure to identify a functional tumour before surgery risks a life-threatening intra-operative hypertensive crisis. When a functional tumour is confirmed, the patient requires pre-operative alpha-blockade with phenoxybenzamine for a minimum of 10-14 days, followed by beta-blockade, before any surgical intervention is undertaken.
> - ## PART E: INVESTIGATIONS *(Addresses Q35, Q118)
> - is the primary imaging modality for assessing bony involvement. The characteristic finding is permeative "moth-eaten" bone destruction. Erosion of the caroticojugular spine — the thin plate of bone separating the carotid canal from the jugular fossa — is the hallmark early radiological finding for glomus jugulare and distinguishes it from glomus tympanicum. HRCT also demonstrates widening of the jugular foramen, the extent of bony erosion, and the status of the otic capsule, facial nerve canal, and carotid canal, all of which are critical for surgical planning.
> - is superior for defining soft tissue extent. The characteristic finding is a "salt and pepper" appearance on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences. The "pepper" component represents signal voids from high-flow vessels within the tumour, while the "salt" component arises from microhaemorrhage and areas of slow flow. MRI is indispensable for assessing intracranial extension, the relationship of the tumour to the dura and brainstem, and the involvement of cranial nerves.
> - is mandatory for glomus jugulare. It identifies the tumour blood supply, feeding arteries, flow dynamics, and venous drainage pattern, and demonstrates the characteristic dense "tumour blush" that is pathognomonic of paraganglioma. Critically, catheter angiography enables simultaneous pre-operative embolization, which is performed in the same session.
> - is essential for assessing the Circle of Willis and collateral cerebral circulation before any procedure that may compromise the internal carotid artery. It also evaluates the patency of contralateral venous drainage (contralateral sigmoid sinus and internal jugular vein), which must be confirmed before ipsilateral sigmoid sinus or IJV ligation.
> - ## PART F: MANAGEMENT OPTIONS *(Addresses Q35, Q118)
> - is appropriate for elderly or comorbid patients, small and slow-growing tumours, asymptomatic patients, and those who decline active treatment. Paragangliomas are slow-growing tumours with an average growth rate of approximately 1 mm per year. Patients managed conservatively undergo serial MRI every 6-12 months initially, then annually if the tumour remains stable. Monitoring for new cranial nerve deficits or accelerated tumour growth is essential.
> - is undertaken with curative intent and is generally preferred for younger patients, those with progressive cranial neuropathy, and those with secretory tumours. Complete surgical excision offers the only chance of cure, but carries risks of cranial nerve injury and significant morbidity, particularly for advanced tumours.
> - is used for tumour control (growth arrest rather than cure). It is the preferred option for elderly or medically unfit patients, residual or recurrent tumour after surgery, bilateral disease (to preserve function on at least one side), and tumours with extensive intracranial extension where surgery carries unacceptable risk. The tumour remains in situ but is prevented from further growth.
> - involves subtotal resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy for residual disease, representing a pragmatic approach when complete resection would carry unacceptable morbidity.
> - — not longer, as neovascularization begins rapidly and the benefit is lost. The technique involves selective catheterization of each feeding artery; because large tumours have multicompartmental blood supply, separate injections into each feeding vessel are required.
> - Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles** — the traditional agent
> - Calibrated microspheres** (spherical particles) — particles must be larger than 150 micrometres to avoid passage into the vasa nervorum, which would cause cranial nerve injury
> - n-Butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA)** — a liquid embolic agent
> - Onyx (ethylene vinyl alcohol copolymer)** — a liquid embolic agent
> - tumours are managed via a transcanal or tympanotomy approach. The tumour is removed from the promontory with excellent results and low morbidity. This straightforward surgery should not be confused with the extensive approach required for glomus jugulare.
> - tumours require an extended facial recess approach (posterior tympanotomy) or hypotympanotomy. A cortical mastoidectomy may be necessary to achieve adequate exposure.
> - tumours are managed via the **infratemporal fossa approach Type A (Fisch approach)**, which is the standard and workhorse approach for glomus jugulare. This approach involves anterior rerouting of the facial nerve, removal of the external auditory canal skin with blind sac closure of the EAC, complete mastoidectomy with skeletonization of the sigmoid sinus, exposure and control of the internal carotid artery, and removal of tumour from the jugular bulb, middle ear, and infralabyrinthine compartment. The approach results in conductive hearing loss (due to EAC closure) and temporary facial paresis from nerve rerouting, which typically recovers to House-Brackmann Grade III.
> - tumours additionally require carotid artery management. Balloon test occlusion (BTO) of the ICA is performed pre-operatively. If the BTO is tolerated (adequate collateral circulation), permanent ICA occlusion may be performed intra-operatively. If the BTO is not tolerated, a saphenous vein bypass graft is used to maintain cerebral perfusion.
> - tumours with intracranial extension require a combined infratemporal fossa approach with neurosurgical posterior fossa or middle fossa craniotomy.
> - using Gamma Knife or CyberKnife (a single high-dose fraction, suitable for tumours less than 3 cm), or as **fractionated radiotherapy** using conventional external beam or intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) for larger tumours. The tumour control rate is 90-95%, representing growth arrest rather than cure.
> - Residual tumour after subtotal resection

> - Elderly patients or those unfit for surgery
> - Extensive intracranial extension (Fisch De2) where complete resection carries high morbidity

> - ## PART G: SURGICAL STEPS FOR GLOMUS JUGULARE EXCISION — INFRATEMPORAL FOSSA APPROACH TYPE A *(Addresses Q118)
> - Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI):** located deep to the posterior belly of digastric
> - Vagus nerve (CN X):** within the carotid sheath, between the ICA/CCA and the IJV
> - Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII):** crosses the ICA and ECA superficially
> - - Dissect the nerve from the stylomastoid foramen proximally to the geniculate ganglion
> - Transect the greater superficial petrosal nerve (GSPN)
> - Mobilize the nerve anteriorly out of its fallopian canal
> - Reroute the nerve to lie anteriorly, protecting it during tumour removal
