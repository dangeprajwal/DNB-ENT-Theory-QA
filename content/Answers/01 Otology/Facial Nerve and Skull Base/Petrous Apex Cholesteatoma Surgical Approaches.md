> - ## PART B: PETROUS APEX CHOLESTEATOMA — CLINICAL FEATURES *(Addresses Q128)
> - of the petrous apex that causes progressive bony erosion. It exists in two forms based on its origin.
> - is the more common type at the petrous apex. It arises from **ectodermal cell rests** (epithelial remnants of embryonal origin) that become trapped within the petrous bone during development. Although not a true cholesteatoma in the traditional otological sense, it behaves identically to acquired cholesteatoma in terms of local tissue destruction and recurrence potential.
> - of the petrous apex develops as an extension from a **middle ear cholesteatoma** that spreads through pneumatized air cell tracts or by direct bony erosion into the petrous apex. The cholesteatoma follows the pathways of pneumatization, reaching the apex via supralabyrinthine, infralabyrinthine, or peritubal routes.
> - of surrounding bone, which is distinctly different from the irregular, aggressive bony destruction seen with malignant tumours. Because of the slow growth rate and the deep, well-protected anatomical location of the petrous apex, these lesions may attain a large size before becoming symptomatic. On imaging, cholesteatoma does not enhance with gadolinium on MRI, which is an important distinguishing feature from neoplastic lesions.
> - Hearing loss** is one of the most common presenting features. **Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL)** results from cochlear compression or direct invasion by the expanding lesion. **Conductive hearing loss (CHL)** occurs when the cholesteatoma extends from or involves the middle ear, as is typical of the acquired type. **Mixed hearing loss** represents a combination of both mechanisms operating simultaneously.
> - Facial pain and trigeminal symptoms** result from compression of **CN V** at **Meckel's cave**. The presentation may take the form of trigeminal neuralgia with lancinating pain, facial numbness (hypoaesthesia) in V1, V2, or V3 distributions, or depression of the corneal reflex. These symptoms may be the initial presentation, particularly for congenital lesions that expand medially toward the apex tip.
> - Abducent nerve palsy (CN VI)** occurs when the lesion compresses CN VI at **Dorello's canal** beneath Gruber's ligament. This produces **lateral rectus weakness** with **diplopia**, manifesting as inability to abduct the eye on the affected side. CN VI palsy is often one of the earliest cranial nerve signs due to the nerve's confined passage and vulnerability at the petrous apex.
> - Gradenigo's syndrome** is the classic triad of **(1) otorrhoea** from middle ear disease, **(2) retro-orbital or deep facial pain** from CN V irritation, and **(3) CN VI palsy** with lateral rectus paralysis. Although originally described in the context of petrous apicitis, this triad is also seen with petrous apex cholesteatoma, particularly the acquired type that has a direct connection to the middle ear.
> - [[Facial Nerve|Facial nerve palsy]] (CN VII)** develops when the lesion extends to involve the labyrinthine, tympanic, or mastoid segments of the facial nerve. Unlike the acute-onset palsy of Bell's palsy, facial weakness from cholesteatoma is typically progressive, which is an important clinical clue to an underlying neoplastic or cholesteatomatous aetiology.
> - Vertigo and imbalance** result from compression or invasion of the **vestibular labyrinth** or vestibular nerve by the expanding lesion. Vestibular dysfunction may be episodic or progressive in nature.
> - CSF leak** can occur when the cholesteatoma erodes through the dura of the middle or posterior cranial fossa. This may present as **CSF otorrhoea** (through a perforated tympanic membrane or via the eustachian tube) or as **[[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]]**.
> - Headache** may result from **dural irritation** or stretching by the mass. If the lesion is very large, raised intracranial pressure may develop. The headache is typically deep-seated and persistent.
> - Lower cranial nerve palsies (CN IX, X, XI)** develop if the lesion extends posteroinferiorly toward the **jugular foramen**. These may manifest as dysphagia, hoarseness, and shoulder weakness.
> - Asymptomatic or incidental finding** — with the widespread use of MRI, petrous apex cholesteatomas are increasingly discovered incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated reasons, before any clinical symptoms have developed.
> - is the primary structural imaging modality. It reveals a **well-defined, expansile lesion** at the petrous apex with **smooth bony margins** and characteristic scalloped erosion of surrounding bone. The lesion shows no calcification and no contrast enhancement. Critically, HRCT demonstrates the spatial relationship of the lesion to the ICA, cochlea, vestibular labyrinth, and IAC, and also maps the individual pneumatization pattern — all of which are essential for surgical planning and approach selection.
> - provides essential soft tissue characterisation. On **T1-weighted images**, cholesteatoma appears as a low to intermediate signal intensity lesion. On **T2-weighted images**, it demonstrates **high signal intensity** (bright). The key feature is the **absence of gadolinium enhancement** after contrast administration, which distinguishes cholesteatoma from enhancing tumours such as meningioma and schwannoma. MRI may also reveal mass effect on adjacent neural and vascular structures.
> - is the **essential investigation** for both diagnosis and post-operative follow-up. Cholesteatoma demonstrates **restricted diffusion**, appearing as a **bright signal on DWI** (high signal on b=1000, low signal on the ADC map). This restricted diffusion pattern is the **key differentiator** from cholesterol granuloma and other petrous apex lesions, which do not show restricted diffusion. DW-MRI is also used for **post-operative surveillance** to detect recurrence without the need for revision surgery or "second look" procedures.
> - is essential to quantify the degree and type of hearing loss and to determine whether hearing is serviceable or non-serviceable, which is one of the most important factors guiding surgical approach selection. **Speech audiometry** with word recognition scoring is equally critical for making this determination.
> - may be required to delineate the precise course of the ICA, particularly if the artery appears aberrant or dehiscent on standard imaging. This is essential for preoperative planning to avoid catastrophic vascular injury.
> - including caloric testing and [[VEMP and Menieres Disease|VEMP]]s (vestibular evoked myogenic potentials) is performed to assess labyrinthine function, which is particularly relevant when a translabyrinthine approach is being considered for a patient with existing vestibular function.
> - must be performed preoperatively, with particular attention to CN V (trigeminal sensation and corneal reflex), CN VI (lateral gaze), CN VII (facial movement), CN VIII (hearing and balance), and lower cranial nerves IX, X, XI, and XII.
