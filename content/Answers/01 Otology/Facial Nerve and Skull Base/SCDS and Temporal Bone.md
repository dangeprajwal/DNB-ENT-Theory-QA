# Superior Semicircular Canal Dehiscence Syndrome

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Describe superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome — aetiopathogenesis, clinical features, diagnosis and management


## INTRODUCTION

Superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome (SCDS) is a clinical entity characterized by a bony defect in the superior semicircular canal, creating a "third window" into the inner ear. This results in distinctive auditory and vestibular symptoms. First described systematically by Minor et al., SCDS requires a combination of clinical features, imaging findings, and vestibular testing for diagnosis.

---

## AETIOPATHOGENESIS

### Epidemiology and Prevalence

The true prevalence of SCDS shows interesting discrepancies between anatomical and clinical findings. Cadaveric temporal bone studies demonstrate bony dehiscence in 0.5-0.6% of specimens, while high-resolution CT imaging shows rates of 4-8%. This discrepancy is explained by partial volume averaging in CT, which can overestimate dehiscence. Importantly, anatomical dehiscence alone is insufficient to cause the syndrome—many individuals with radiological dehiscence remain completely asymptomatic.

### The "Third Window" Mechanism

The fundamental pathophysiology involves creation of a "third window" into the inner ear. Normally, the inner ear has two mobile windows: the oval window (connected to the stapes footplate) and the round window (membrane separating middle ear from scala tympani). When bone overlying the superior semicircular canal is absent or deficient, creating a connection with the middle cranial fossa, a third mobile window is formed. This concept was first described by Cawthorne in 1949 for labyrinthine fistula and is now recognized as the mechanism underlying SCDS.

This third window creates an abnormal pathway for sound and pressure transmission, explaining both the auditory and vestibular manifestations of the syndrome.

### Aetiology: Congenital versus Acquired

The exact aetiology remains debated, with evidence supporting both congenital predisposition and acquired triggering factors.

**Congenital Factors:**

There is clear evidence for genetic predisposition, with documented cases in affected siblings. During fetal development, the membranous labyrinth protrudes into the developing middle cranial fossa, causing dural adhesion that prevents complete bone coverage of the canal. Ossification of the petrous temporal bone occurs from birth to age 3 years. Insufficient ossification during this critical period predisposes to SCDS. The presence of only the endosteal bone layer leaves the area susceptible to secondary events.

**Acquired/Triggering Factors:**

Several acquired factors can trigger or unmask SCDS in predisposed individuals. Head trauma or closed head injury can cause acute dehiscence. Shearing forces to the temporal bone can disrupt thin bone. Slow erosion of already-thin bone may occur over time. Increased dural elasticity with aging can contribute to progressive thinning. Sudden rises in intracranial pressure may precipitate symptoms.

**Most Likely Mechanism:**

The current consensus favors a **multifactorial aetiopathogenesis** combining congenital predisposition with acquired triggering events. Individuals are born with thin or incomplete bone coverage, which remains asymptomatic until trauma, aging, or other factors trigger clinical manifestations.

---

## CLINICAL FEATURES

The clinical presentation of SCDS varies considerably, ranging from predominantly auditory symptoms to predominantly vestibular symptoms, or a combination of both. Dehiscences ≥2.5mm typically present with combined auditory AND vestibular symptoms, and the size of the dehiscence correlates with the magnitude of symptoms, particularly the air-bone gap.

### Vestibular Symptoms (Third Window Effects)

Vestibular symptoms result from the abnormal transmission of sound and pressure changes into the vestibular labyrinth through the third window.

**Tullio Phenomenon** is the hallmark vestibular feature—transient vertigo triggered by loud sounds. Patients may experience vertigo when exposed to loud noises such as music, machinery, or even their own voice.

Imbalance and oscillopsia (visual illusion of environmental movement) occur due to inappropriate vestibular stimulation. Symptoms can be elicited by intracranial or middle ear pressure changes. Valsalva maneuver, coughing, straining, or changes in atmospheric pressure may trigger symptoms. Generalized imbalance results from fluctuating vestibular system function.

### Auditory Symptoms

Auditory manifestations create a paradoxical pattern of apparent conductive hearing loss with supranormal bone conduction.

**Conductive-Type Hearing Loss:** Audiometry demonstrates an air-bone gap, particularly at low frequencies. However, unlike true conductive loss, bone conduction thresholds are actually supranormal (better than 0 dB HL), indicating the third window facilitates bone-conducted sound transmission.

**Autophony** is particularly distinctive in SCDS. Patients hear their own voice abnormally loudly, but unlike patulous Eustachian tube, the quality is different and associated with other SCDS features.

**Pulsatile [[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]]** occurs from hearing one's own pulse transmitted through the abnormal pathway.

**Abnormal Bone-Conducted Sound Amplification** produces striking symptoms. Patients can hear their own eye movements (creaking sounds with eye rotation), footfall when walking, and even joint movements. In extreme cases, patients can hear a tuning fork placed on the lateral ankle malleolus—a diagnostic sign when present.

**Weber Test** lateralizes to the affected ear due to the enhanced bone conduction on that side.

The spectrum of symptoms varies enormously between patients, with some having predominantly vestibular complaints, others predominantly auditory issues, and many experiencing both.

---

## DIAGNOSIS

Diagnosis requires integration of clinical features, imaging findings, and vestibular function tests. The **"positive triad"** is essential:

1. History with typical clinical features
2. High-resolution CT confirmation of dehiscence
3. [[VEMP and Menieres Disease|VEMP]]s at lower than normal thresholds on the affected side

### High-Resolution CT (Gold Standard for Anatomy)

High-resolution CT is the imaging modality of choice for identifying bony dehiscence. Images must be reformatted in the coronal plane using specialized views:

**Pöschl View** shows the plane of the superior semicircular canal.
**Stenvers View** provides the orthogonal plane.

Optimal technique involves 0.5mm collimated scans with Pöschl reformatting to improve specificity. Cone beam CT provides higher-definition imaging with better resolution of thin bone.

**Important Limitation:** CT is prone to false positives and tends to overestimate dehiscence size due to partial volume averaging. Therefore, radiological findings must always be correlated with clinical features and vestibular testing.

### Vestibular Evoked Myogenic Potentials (VEMPs)

VEMPs have become essential diagnostic tools with excellent sensitivity and specificity.

**Ocular VEMPs (oVEMP):**
Surface electrodes placed beneath the eye detect extraocular muscle responses to sound stimulation. Sensitivity and specificity exceed 90%, making oVEMP an excellent screening tool. The advantage is that it can be performed before CT scanning, potentially reducing unnecessary radiation exposure in patients without SCDS.

**Cervical VEMPs (cVEMP):**
These record responses from the sternocleidomastoid muscle with sensitivity and specificity ranging from 80-100%.

**Key Diagnostic Finding:** In SCDS, VEMPs show responses at LOWER than normal thresholds for both air and bone conduction. This is the opposite of most pathology, where thresholds are elevated. Additionally, VEMP amplitudes are often LARGER than normal. These findings likely result from the saccule becoming hypersensitive to sound due to the third window effect.

### Audiometry

Pure tone audiometry demonstrates air-bone gap predominantly at low frequencies, with supranormal bone conduction thresholds (better than 0 dB HL). This creates the paradoxical appearance of conductive hearing loss when bone conduction is actually enhanced.

### Tuning Fork Tests

Simple bedside tests can be revealing. Weber test lateralizes to the affected ear. In extreme cases, patients can hear a vibrating tuning fork placed on the lateral ankle malleolus—a striking clinical sign when present.

### MRI (Adjunctive Role)

MRI using FIESTA sequences (Fast Imaging Employing Steady-State Acquisition) has a negative predictive value of 100% for excluding SCDS. However, it cannot consistently diagnose thin or dehiscent bone, limiting its utility for positive diagnosis.

---

## MANAGEMENT

Management depends on symptom severity, impact on quality of life, and patient preferences. Options range from conservative observation to surgical intervention.

### Conservative Management

For patients with mild, non-intrusive symptoms, conservative management is appropriate.

**Patient Education** about trigger and stimulus avoidance helps reduce symptom frequency. Understanding which activities or sounds provoke symptoms allows patients to modify behavior.

**Ventilation Tube Insertion** has been tried to reduce middle ear pressure changes. While this may provide some symptomatic relief by equalizing pressure, it does not address the underlying pathology directly and has limited efficacy.

Many patients can adapt to symptoms and maintain good quality of life without surgical intervention.

### Surgical Management

Surgery is reserved for patients with disabling symptoms significantly affecting quality of life, when symptoms are predominantly attributable to SCDS (not other conditions), and after thorough discussion of risks and alternatives. The decision must be patient-centered.

**Surgical Approaches:**

**1. Middle Fossa Craniotomy (MCF) Approach:**
This was the initial technique described in 1998. The superior semicircular canal is approached through a craniotomy, allowing direct visualization and repair. However, this approach carries higher risk of facial nerve injury and is now less commonly used due to transmastoid alternatives.

**2. Transmastoid (TM) Approach:**
The transmastoid route avoids craniotomy by approaching through mastoidectomy.

- **TM Plugging:** Adapted from the BPPV posterior canal occlusion technique. The disadvantage is lack of direct visualization of the dehiscence.

- **TM Resurfacing (Preferred):** This involves skeletonization of the labyrinth and exposure of the middle cranial fossa dura lateral to the superior semicircular canal. The dura is lifted off the middle cranial fossa floor, the dehiscence is identified, and coverage is achieved using silastic (silicone elastomer) OR bone pâté combined with fibrin sealant (Tisseel). Limitations include difficulty with extensive dural overhang or tegmen dehiscence.

**3. Round Window (RW) Reinforcement:**
This is an emerging technique using a permeatal or endaural approach. The round window is reinforced using temporalis fascia, tragal cartilage, perichondrium, fat, or gelatin sponge. This approach may obviate the need for craniotomy or mastoidectomy in the future, though it is still being evaluated.

**4. Endoscopic MCF Approach:**
Endoscopic techniques offer shorter operative and recovery times while maintaining the benefits of middle fossa visualization, though craniotomy risks remain.

### Surgical Outcomes and Complications

Overall, surgical techniques demonstrate high success rates and low complication rates when performed by experienced surgeons.

**Major Complications:**
- Permanent total sensorineural hearing loss (from labyrinth injury)
- Facial nerve injury

**Minor Complications:**
- Transient sensorineural hearing loss
- [[BPPV|Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo]] (BPPV)
- Post-operative dysequilibrium

The technique must be extremely delicate to prevent rupture of the membranous labyrinth, which would result in catastrophic hearing loss.

Success in resolving symptoms is generally high, with most patients experiencing significant improvement in both vestibular and auditory symptoms when appropriate patient selection and surgical technique are employed.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> - SCDS results from a "third window" bony defect in the superior semicircular canal
> - Multifactorial aetiology: congenital predisposition + acquired triggers
> - **Clinical features:** Tullio phenomenon, autophony, supranormal bone conduction, hearing own eye movements/footfall
> - **Diagnosis requires positive triad:** Clinical features + HRCT + VEMPs at lower thresholds
> - **VEMPs:** Sensitivity/specificity >90% (oVEMP), 80-100% (cVEMP); key finding is responses at LOWER thresholds
> - **Management:** Conservative for mild cases; surgical (transmastoid resurfacing preferred) for disabling symptoms
> - Dehiscence ≥2.5mm typically causes combined auditory and vestibular symptoms

# TUBERCULOSIS OF THE TEMPORAL BONE

## INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB) of the temporal bone is a rare manifestation of extrapulmonary tuberculosis, accounting for less than 1% of chronic suppurative otitis media cases. Despite effective anti-tuberculous therapy, it remains an important diagnosis due to severe complications including facial paralysis and profound hearing loss. High clinical suspicion is essential, particularly in TB-endemic areas, as delayed diagnosis leads to irreversible complications.

---

## AETIOPATHOGENESIS

### Routes of Infection

Mycobacterium tuberculosis can reach the temporal bone through three main routes:

**1. Through Existing Tympanic Membrane Perforation (Most Common):**
In patients with pre-existing chronic otitis media, organisms can enter through the perforation and establish infection in the middle ear cleft.

**2. Via Eustachian Tube:**
Organisms from the nasopharynx can ascend through the Eustachian tube to reach the middle ear.

**3. Haematogenous Spread:**
Bloodborne spread from a pulmonary focus or other tuberculous site can seed the temporal bone. This route is particularly relevant in disseminated tuberculosis.

### Pathological Progression

The disease evolves through distinct phases:

**Acute Phase:** Initial congestion leads to thickened tympanic membrane. Tubercles form, followed by perforations developing in the membrane.

**Chronic Phase:** The infection progresses slowly and painlessly. Multiple perforations may be present, and pale pink granulation tissue develops in the middle ear and external canal.

**Necrotic/Caseous Form:** Bone destruction occurs with sequestrum formation. Caseous necrosis is characteristic of tuberculous infection, distinguishing it from other chronic infections.

### Secondary Infection

Up to 50% of cases in children represent secondary infection from tuberculosis elsewhere in the body. Pulmonary tuberculosis predisposes to temporal bone involvement, though many patients have no active pulmonary disease at presentation. Historical studies show 14-93% prevalence of pulmonary involvement, but this may represent inactive disease.

---

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION

The clinical presentation of temporal bone tuberculosis is often subtle and nonspecific, contributing to delayed diagnosis.

### Classic Triad (Ng)

Three features should raise suspicion for tuberculous otitis:

1. **Regional lymphadenopathy** without typical systemic TB features
2. **Refractory otitis** unresponsive to conventional antimicrobials
3. **Complications** ([[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]], sensorineural hearing loss) appearing early in the disease course

### Characteristic Features

**Painless Otorrhoea** is the hallmark feature distinguishing TB from other causes of chronic otitis media. The absence of otalgia should prompt consideration of TB.

**Multiple Tympanic Membrane Perforations** are pathognomonic when present, though rare. These may coalesce into a single large perforation, making the classic finding less apparent.

**Pale or Denuded Middle Ear Mucosa** has a characteristic appearance. The mucosa appears unhealthy, often pale pink rather than inflamed, and bone may be denuded of mucosa in up to 30% of patients.

**Hearing Loss** is severe and disproportionate to otoscopic findings. Initially conductive due to middle ear involvement, it progresses to mixed hearing loss when the infection spreads to the labyrinth (labyrinthitis). The degree of hearing impairment often seems excessive compared to the relatively benign otoscopic appearance.

### Red Flag Symptoms

Several features should immediately raise suspicion for tuberculous rather than routine chronic otitis:

**Facial Nerve Palsy** occurs in 10-20% of adult patients and up to 35% in children. Critically, it appears EARLY in the disease course—unlike [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]] where facial palsy is a late complication of advanced disease. The absence of facial palsy in a patient with cholesteatoma makes TB diagnosis more likely if other features are present.

**Absence of Pain** contrasts with the often painful nature of bacterial otitis media.

**Complications Appearing Disproportionately Early** in the disease course should always trigger consideration of TB.

### Associated Systemic Symptoms

Approximately 50% of patients report history of TB exposure. Systemic symptoms may include cough, night sweats, and fever, though these are often absent. Many patients have inactive pulmonary TB or no pulmonary involvement at all.

---

## DIAGNOSIS

Diagnosis requires high clinical suspicion, particularly in TB-endemic areas. Patients with otorrhoea unresponsive to conventional treatment and disproportionate complications should be thoroughly evaluated for TB.

### Bacteriology

**Culture for Mycobacterium tuberculosis** confirms the diagnosis when positive. However, bacteriology has poor diagnostic yield with sensitivity less than 20%. The low bacterial count in ear discharge makes culture-based diagnosis unreliable.

**PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)** for Mycobacterium tuberculosis in pus or tissue specimens provides better sensitivity and faster results than culture. This molecular technique has improved diagnostic capability.

Despite these advances, negative microbiology does not exclude TB—many confirmed cases have negative cultures.

### Histopathology (Gold Standard)

**Tissue biopsy** provides the most definitive diagnosis. Samples can be obtained from granulation tissue, middle ear polyps, or tissue during mastoidectomy.

**Key Histological Findings:**
- Granulomata with caseous necrosis (hallmark feature)
- Epithelioid cells
- Langerhans giant cells

However, even histopathology is not perfectly sensitive. False-negative rates of approximately 10% are reported, with some sources citing 30-35% false-negative rates from middle ear and aural polyp biopsies. This emphasizes the need for clinical correlation.

### Imaging Studies

**Plain X-rays** are non-specific, showing haziness or erosion similar to other forms of chronic otitis media.

**High-Resolution CT** is the investigation of choice, providing detailed anatomical information:

**Key Distinguishing Features:**
- Soft tissue filling the middle ear cleft
- **Relatively well-preserved mastoid air cell system** (critical distinguishing feature from squamous chronic otitis media)
- Minimal mastoid sclerosis (unlike the marked sclerosis in typical chronic otitis)
- Mucosal thickening of the external auditory canal
- Soft tissue extension into the external canal WITHOUT scutum erosion

**Advanced Disease Findings:**
- Diffuse temporal bone destruction (contrasting with selective scutum destruction in squamous chronic suppurative otitis media)
- Assessment of facial nerve canal involvement
- Semicircular canal erosion
- Labyrinthine ossification (important for cochlear implant planning)

### Systemic Workup

**Chest X-ray** and thorough clinical examination assess for pulmonary or systemic TB. History of TB exposure should be specifically sought. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) testing may be performed though yields are typically low from ear specimens.

### Diagnostic Challenges

Several factors contribute to delayed or missed diagnosis:

The rarity of the condition, especially in developed nations, means it is not considered in differential diagnosis. The non-specific initial presentation mimics routine chronic otitis media. High false-negative rates in bacteriology mask the true diagnosis. Neomycin and gentamicin ear drops have weak anti-tuberculous effects, potentially masking features temporarily. Many patients have no prior TB history and normal chest X-ray, leading to false reassurance.

---

## COMPLICATIONS

Tuberculosis of the temporal bone causes severe complications that may be irreversible if treatment is delayed.

### Facial Nerve Paralysis

Facial palsy occurs in 16% of adults and 35% of children with temporal bone TB. The mechanism involves granulation tissue within the Fallopian canal compressing the facial nerve. The critical feature is that paralysis occurs EARLY in the disease course (10-20% of all cases), unlike cholesteatoma where it is a late complication. This early occurrence should trigger suspicion for TB.

### Profound Hearing Loss

Cochlear spread causes labyrinthitis with profound sensorineural hearing loss. Once established, this hearing loss is typically irreversible. Labyrinthine ossification can occur, making subsequent [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]] technically difficult or impossible.

### Sequestrum Formation

Bone necrosis leads to sequestrum formation in approximately 30% of cases, requiring surgical removal.

### Other Complications

Postaural fistula may develop. Temporomandibular joint involvement can occur. **Intracranial complications** include meningitis (potentially fatal), tuberculomata, and otitic hydrocephalus. Multiple cranial nerve palsies have been reported in severe cases.

**Complications are generally more common and severe in children** than adults.

---

## MANAGEMENT

### Medical Treatment (Primary/Mainstay)

Anti-tuberculous therapy is the cornerstone of treatment.

**Standard First-Line Regimen:**
- **Isoniazide + Ethambutol + Pyrazinamide + Rifampicin**
- **Minimum duration: 6 months**
- Longer treatment courses (9-12 months or more) for disseminated TB or tuberculous meningitis

**Response to Treatment:**
Otorrhoea typically begins resolving within 2-3 weeks of initiating therapy. Granulation tissue disappears over 1-5 months. Clinical improvement with anti-tuberculous therapy can serve as retrospective diagnostic confirmation in culture-negative cases.

Medical management alone is often sufficient for complete resolution, even in cases with facial nerve involvement.

### Surgical Treatment

Surgery has a limited but important role in selected cases.

**Indications for Surgery:**
1. Obtain tissue for histological diagnosis when non-invasive methods fail
2. Drain subperiosteal abscess
3. Remove bony sequestrum (occurs in ~30% of cases)
4. Facial nerve decompression if medical management fails (though this remains controversial)

**Surgical Procedures:**
- Mastoidectomy with or without facial nerve decompression
- Aural polypectomy for tissue diagnosis

**Combined Medical and Surgical Treatment:**
Some authors advocate combined approaches for faster healing, though medical therapy alone achieves similar outcomes in most cases.

**Facial Nerve Decompression:**
The role of surgery for facial palsy remains contentious. Some studies show similar results with medical management alone compared to decompression surgery. Most experts now favor initial medical management with close observation, reserving surgery for cases not improving after adequate anti-tuberculous therapy.

### Hearing Rehabilitation

For patients with bilateral profound sensorineural hearing loss, **cochlear implantation** is an option. However, labyrinthine ossification from the TB infection may complicate surgical access and electrode insertion, requiring special techniques or modified approaches.

---

## KEY POINTS

- TB of temporal bone is rare (<1% CSOM) but causes severe complications
- **Routes:** Perforation (most common), Eustachian tube, haematogenous spread
- **Classic triad:** Lymphadenopathy + refractory otitis + early complications
- **Hallmark feature:** Painless otorrhoea
- **Red flags:** Early facial palsy (10-20%), disproportionate hearing loss, multiple TM perforations
- **Diagnosis:** Histopathology (gold standard) + HRCT showing preserved mastoid air cells
- **Bacteriology:** Poor yield (<20% sensitivity); PCR better
- **Complications:** Facial palsy (16% adults, 35% children), profound SNHL, sequestrum (30%), labyrinthine ossification
- **Treatment:** Anti-TB therapy (IEPS) for minimum 6 months is mainstay
- **Surgery:** Reserved for diagnosis, abscess drainage, sequestrum removal; role in facial palsy controversial
- **Response:** Otorrhoea resolves in 2-3 weeks; granulations disappear in 1-5 months

| **Hearing Loss** | Apparent conductive (supranormal BC) | Conductive → mixed, profound |
