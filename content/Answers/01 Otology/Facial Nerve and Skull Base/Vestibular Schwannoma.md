## INTRODUCTION

Vestibular schwannoma (VS), previously termed acoustic neuroma, is a benign tumour arising from abnormally proliferative Schwann cells that envelope the lateral portion of the vestibular nerve in the internal acoustic meatus. At a consensus conference, the term "acoustic neuroma" was determined to be less accurate and "vestibular schwannoma" was recommended. VS is the most common tumour of the cerebellopontine angle, representing approximately 80-90% of CPA tumours. With improved imaging and increased clinical awareness, the incidence has risen from the previously quoted 1:100,000 per year to 1:80,000 per year, with tumours detected at smaller sizes. Management options include observation, microsurgery, and stereotactic radiosurgery.

---

## PART A: SURGICAL ANATOMY OF THE CEREBELLOPONTINE ANGLE

The cerebellopontine angle (CPA) is a triangular space in the posterior cranial fossa situated between the cerebellum, pons, and petrous temporal bone.

### Boundaries

- **Anterior:** Posterior surface of the petrous temporal bone
- **Posterior:** Anterior surface of the cerebellum
- **Medial:** Lateral aspect of the pons and brainstem
- **Superior:** Tentorium cerebelli
- **Inferior:** Lower cranial nerves and foramen magnum region

### Contents

**Cranial Nerves:**
- **V (Trigeminal):** Exits from lateral pons; compressed by large VS
- **VI (Abducens):** Runs in the subarachnoid space
- **VII (Facial):** Emerges between pons and olive; traverses the CPA to enter the porus of the internal auditory meatus (IAM), lying anterosuperiorly in the IAM
- **VIII (Vestibulocochlear):** Joins VII in the CPA; enters the IAM
- **IX, X, XI (Lower cranial nerves):** Exit the brainstem and pass through the jugular foramen

**Vascular Structures:**
- **Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery (AICA):** A branch of the basilar artery; loops into the CPA, often into the IAM; key relationship with VS
- **Superior petrosal sinus:** Runs along the posterior petrous ridge
- **Sigmoid sinus:** Posterior lateral boundary

### Internal Auditory Meatus

The IAM is divided into four quadrants by the transverse crest (horizontal) and Bill's bar (vertical crest):
- **Anterosuperior:** Facial nerve (VII)
- **Anteroinferior:** Cochlear nerve
- **Posterosuperior:** Superior vestibular nerve
- **Posteroinferior:** Inferior vestibular nerve

This anatomy is critical for surgical identification of the facial nerve during tumour removal.

## PART B: ETIOPATHOGENESIS AND EPIDEMIOLOGY

### Aetiology

The aetiology of VS is not fully known. Recent advances in molecular biology indicate that a defect of **chromosome 22q** may be responsible for development of both the unilateral sporadic VS and the bilateral VS seen in neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2). The tumour develops in the nerve sheath, compressing rather than invading the nerve on which it arose, leaving a plane between the nerve fibres and the tumour that facilitates surgical dissection.

### Growth Pattern

As the VS grows, it gradually fills the internal acoustic meatus and eventually protrudes out of the porus into the CPA. Bone resorption of the IAM is an active, slowly progressive process caused by increased vascularization and pressure from the tumour. Further growth causes displacement and stretching of the VIIth and VIIIth cranial nerves on the anterior aspect and the AICA on the inferior aspect. Continued expansion leads to compression of the cerebellum, trigeminal nerve, brainstem, and fourth ventricle, eventually causing hydrocephalus.

Many series have shown that **60-70% of VS remain stable with no growth for many years**, supporting a conservative approach with active monitoring.

### Cystic Vestibular Schwannoma

Cyst formation within VS is seen regularly on MRI. Cystic VS may have a more rapid and unpredictable growth pattern.

## PART C: CLINICAL FEATURES AND DIAGNOSIS

### Symptoms

In more than **90% of patients**, the first symptom is unilateral progressive hearing loss with or without [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]]. In 5-10% of cases, hearing loss is sudden and may be profound. Hearing was good with normal speech discrimination in 17% of patients. The caloric test was normal in 8%.

**Symptom presentation by size:**
- **Small tumours:** Asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus
- **Medium tumours:** Above plus vestibular symptoms, trigeminal symptoms (facial numbness)
- **Large tumours:** Above plus facial nerve weakness, cerebellar signs, hydrocephalus
- **17% had non-acoustic first symptoms** such as vertigo, trigeminal, or cerebellar symptoms

### Diagnostic Tests

**Audiological Tests:**
- Pure tone audiometry showing asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, more pronounced at higher frequencies
- Speech discrimination scores are often disproportionately poor
- Auditory brainstem response (ABR): Prolonged wave I-V interpeak latency or absent wave V

**Imaging:**
- **Gadolinium-enhanced MRI** is the gold standard for diagnosis, showing enhancing mass in the IAM and/or CPA
- **HRCT temporal bone:** Shows expansion of the IAM; useful for surgical planning and assessing bony anatomy

### Classification of Size

| Grade | Classification | Extrameatal Size (mm) |
|-------|---------------|----------------------|
| 0 | Intrameatal | 0 |
| 1 | Small | 1-10 |
| 2 | Medium | 11-20 |
| 3 | Moderately large | 21-30 |
| 4 | Large | 31-40 |
| 5 | Giant | >40 |

### Differential Diagnosis of CPA Tumours

- Meningioma (second most common CPA tumour)
- Epidermoid cyst
- Arachnoid cyst
- Facial nerve schwannoma
- Lower cranial nerve schwannomas (IX, X, XI)
- Metastasis

## PART D: MANAGEMENT OPTIONS

### 1. Observation (Active Monitoring)

For small and intracanalicular tumours, a conservative approach is commonly advised. Active monitoring involves serial MRI scanning to detect evidence of growth, after which intervention is offered. There is no way of predicting which tumours will grow at initial diagnosis. Some growth in small and intracanalicular tumours is tolerated without the need for urgent intervention.

### 2. Microsurgical Resection

Surgery remains the mainstay of treatment, particularly for tumours larger than 30 mm maximal intracranial diameter, beyond which the role of stereotactic radiotherapy is considered minimal.

### 3. Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)

Generally recommended for tumours less than 3 cm in extracanalicular diameter with no significant brainstem compression. Increasingly offered as an alternative to surgery for small to medium tumours.

---

## PART E: SURGICAL APPROACHES

### 1. Translabyrinthine Approach

This is the favoured approach for the majority of neurotologists.

**Advantages:**
- Avoidance of cerebellar retraction or resection
- Early identification of the facial nerve at the lateral end of the IAM
- A well-performed translabyrinthine approach allows removal of a VS of any size
- Can be extended posteriorly (posterior fossa), anteriorly (transcochlear to petrous apex/clivus), superiorly (middle fossa), or inferiorly (jugular foramen)

**Disadvantages:**
- Involves destruction of the inner ear (not suitable for hearing preservation)
- Higher incidence of CSF fistula than other approaches

**Key Surgical Steps:**
1. Skin and periosteal flaps
2. Extended cortical [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]]
3. Bony labyrinthectomy
4. Skeletonization of jugular bulb and vertical portion of facial nerve
5. Skeletonization of the IAM
6. Identification of the facial nerve at the lateral end of the internal meatus
7. Opening of the posterior fossa through the dura
8. Removal of tumour using standard neurosurgical techniques
9. Closure with obliteration of middle ear and petrosectomy defect with abdominal fat

### 2. Middle Fossa Approach

The internal meatus is approached extradurally from above through a small craniectomy.

**Advantages:**
- Allows hearing preservation surgery
- Good visualization of the lateral extent of the IAM

**Disadvantages:**
- Limited access to posterior fossa
- Maximum comfortable tumour removal size: approximately 1-1.5 cm intracranial diameter
- Small but real risk of epilepsy from temporal lobe retraction
- Facial nerve lies between the surgeon and the tumour, making it more vulnerable

**Key Surgical Steps:**
1. Skin and soft tissue incisions
2. Middle fossa craniectomy (5 cm x 5 cm bone flap)
3. Extradural elevation of temporal lobe dura
4. Identification of landmarks: greater superficial petrosal nerve, arcuate eminence, geniculate ganglion
5. Skeletonization of internal meatus
6. Identification of facial and vestibular nerves
7. Tumour removal
8. Closure with muscle plug and bone flap replacement

### 3. Retrosigmoid (Suboccipital) Approach

Evolved from the classic suboccipital operation. A retrosigmoid craniectomy of around 5 cm x 5 cm allows good access to the posterior fossa.

**Advantages:**
- Allows possibility of hearing preservation in small tumours
- Good access to the posterior fossa

**Disadvantages:**
- Considerable cerebellar retraction may be necessary for large tumours
- Post-operative headache more common than with translabyrinthine approach
- If facial nerve is lost near the fundus of the IAM, primary nerve grafting is virtually impossible

### Surgical Approach by Tumour Size

| Tumour Size | Recommended Approach |
|-------------|---------------------|
| Intrameatal with serviceable hearing | Middle fossa or retrosigmoid |
| Small (1-10 mm) with hearing preservation | Middle fossa (up to 1.5 cm) |
| Small to medium without hearing | Translabyrinthine |
| Large (>30 mm) | Translabyrinthine |
| Any size, no hearing preservation needed | Translabyrinthine |

## PART F: GAMMA KNIFE STEREOTACTIC RADIOSURGERY

### Principles

Stereotactic radiosurgery uses ionizing radiation to ablate a volume of tissue or alter its biological activity, preventing further growth. The Gamma Knife unit uses a fixed array of 192-201 radioactive cobalt sources to deliver the dose to a precisely targeted location using a rigid stereotactic headframe. SRS is generally restricted to targets less than 3 cm in diameter.

### Dose and Technique

Currently, most centres prescribe **12-13 Gy to the margin** of the tumour. Over time, the recommended dose has reduced from 16 Gy, which has lowered the incidence of cranial nerve complications without adversely affecting local control rates.

### Outcomes

Published results demonstrate high local control rates with minimal toxicity:

- Local control rates of **91-98%** across multiple series
- **Trigeminal nerve preservation:** 95-100%
- **Facial nerve preservation:** 92-100%
- Transient tumour enlargement (pseudoprogression) occurs in 23% of cases, most commonly regressing at 24 months post-treatment
- Only tumours that continue to enlarge after 24 months should be considered treatment failures

### Hearing Preservation

For patients with serviceable hearing (Gardner-Robertson Grades I-II), some centres advocate early radiosurgery. One prospective non-randomized series reported improved hearing preservation rates with early Gamma Knife SRS (77%, 70%, 64% at 3, 4, 5 years) compared to observation (75%, 52%, 41%).

### Indications

- Tumour less than 3 cm extracanalicular diameter with no significant brainstem compression
- Patient preference against surgery
- Elderly or medically unfit patients
- Residual or recurrent tumour post-surgery

## PART G: CASE SCENARIO - 1 cm VS IN A 35-YEAR-OLD FEMALE

### Management Considerations

A 1 cm (Grade 1, small) vestibular schwannoma in a 35-year-old female presents several management considerations:

**Initial Assessment:**
- Full audiological evaluation including PTA, speech discrimination, and ABR
- Gadolinium-enhanced MRI to confirm size and location
- Assessment of hearing status (serviceable vs non-serviceable)
- Screening for NF2 (bilateral VS, other features) given young age

**Management Options:**

**1. Active Monitoring (Watch-and-Wait):** Given that 60-70% of VS remain stable without growth, serial MRI scanning (typically 6-monthly initially, then annually) is a reasonable first-line approach. Intervention is offered if growth is demonstrated.

**2. Middle Fossa Approach:** If the patient has serviceable hearing and desires hearing preservation, the middle fossa approach allows removal of a tumour up to 1-1.5 cm with the possibility of preserving hearing. This is suitable given the tumour's small size.

**3. Retrosigmoid Approach:** Also allows hearing preservation for small tumours. Provides good posterior fossa access.

**4. Gamma Knife SRS:** 12-13 Gy marginal dose. High local control (>95%) with excellent cranial nerve preservation. May offer better hearing preservation than observation alone. Suitable for this tumour size.

**Recommended Approach:** In a young patient with serviceable hearing, the options should be discussed in a multidisciplinary setting. Initial observation with serial MRI is commonly advised, with intervention (surgery or SRS) offered if growth is demonstrated. If early intervention is chosen, either middle fossa surgery (for hearing preservation) or Gamma Knife SRS are appropriate options.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. VS is a benign tumour arising from Schwann cells of the vestibular nerve; defect of chromosome 22q is implicated
> 2. The tumour compresses rather than invades the nerve, leaving a dissection plane between tumour and nerve fibres
> 3. In more than 90% of patients, the first symptom is unilateral progressive SNHL; 5-10% present with sudden hearing loss
> 4. Gadolinium-enhanced MRI is the gold standard for diagnosis
> 5. 60-70% of VS remain stable without growth, supporting active monitoring as initial management
> 6. Translabyrinthine approach is the preferred approach for most neurotologists; allows VS removal of any size but sacrifices hearing
> 7. Middle fossa approach is limited to tumours of approximately 1-1.5 cm and allows hearing preservation
> 8. Retrosigmoid approach allows hearing preservation but involves cerebellar retraction
> 9. Gamma Knife SRS at 12-13 Gy marginal dose achieves 91-98% local control with minimal cranial nerve morbidity
> 10. Pseudoprogression occurs in 23% post-SRS; tumours should not be considered treatment failures until 24 months post-treatment
> 11. For large tumours (>30 mm), surgery is preferred as the role of SRS is considered minimal at this size
> 12. The facial nerve runs in the anterosuperior quadrant of the IAM; Bill's bar and the transverse crest are key surgical landmarks


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. The "best" surgical approach is the one that gives the best results in the hands of the individual surgeon - no approach is universally superior
> 2. The facial nerve is most at risk at or just medial to the porus during translabyrinthine surgery, where the nerve becomes very thin and difficult to differentiate from surrounding arachnoid
> 3. In cystic VS, growth may be more rapid and unpredictable, and facial nerve outcomes may be worse
> 4. CSF fistula remains one of the most common post-operative complications of translabyrinthine surgery; careful obliteration of the middle ear and Eustachian tube with muscle, fascia, and bone wax is essential
> 5. Post-operative headache is more common with the retrosigmoid approach than translabyrinthine
> 6. The geniculate ganglion may be dehiscent in up to 5% of individuals, placing the facial nerve at risk during middle fossa approach
