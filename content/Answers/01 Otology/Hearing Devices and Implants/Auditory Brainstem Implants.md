# Auditory Brainstem Implants and Recent Developments

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the auditory brainstem implant (ABI), its indications, surgical considerations, outcomes and recent developments in its application.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Volume 2 – Chapter 96: Auditory Brainstem Implantation (Shakeel R. Saeed and Harry R.F. Powell)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The auditory brainstem implant (ABI) is indicated in severe to profound hearing loss when congenital or acquired abnormalities of the cochlea or auditory nerve (or sometimes both) preclude [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]] (CI). The acquired group predominantly comprises neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) patients, and occasionally those with acquired cochlear diseases such as [[Otosclerosis|otosclerosis]] or post-meningitic cochlear ossification. The congenital group includes infants and children with dysplastic or aplastic cochleas and/or auditory nerves.

The aim of ABI is to rehabilitate patients with no hearing as a result of absence or damage to the auditory nerves and thereby no cochlea-to-brainstem connections. Unlike CI which stimulates the spiral ganglion neurons in the cochlea, the ABI directly stimulates the cochlear nucleus complex on the surface of the brainstem.

---

## PART A: HISTORY AND DEVICE DESIGN

A single-channel ABI was first used to successfully stimulate the cochlear nucleus in 1979 by the House Institute for patients with NF2 who had no remaining hearing after bilateral [[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]] (VS) surgery. Prior attempts to elicit auditory sensations by stimulating the inferior colliculus had failed. Two ball electrodes were implanted near the surface of the cochlear nucleus.

The first multichannel device was implanted in 1991 and subsequently modified. Two commercially available ABI devices exist:

- **Cochlear Limited (Nucleus) ABI:** Has 21 electrode contacts, based on the Nucleus cochlear implant. First introduced in the UK by Richard Ramsden in Manchester as part of a European multicentre clinical trial.
- **MED-EL ABI:** First implanted in 1997. Has a paddle array of 12 electrode contacts on a 5.5 mm x 3.0 mm soft silicone matrix. Works with an external audio processor in the same manner as a CI. The most recent MED-EL ABI is MRI conditional, enabling patients to be scanned in a 1.5 Tesla machine without the need for magnet removal due to an advance in magnet polarity technology where the magnet is self-aligning and rotates freely within its housing.

## PART B: INDICATIONS

### ABI in Acquired Hearing Loss (NF2 and Non-NF2)

ABIs were initially designed for patients with NF2. Bilateral VSs are the pathological hallmark of NF2, occurring in 90-95% of patients. Hearing loss occurs as a result of tumour growth causing auditory nerve compression or cochlear damage from ischaemia, infarction or tumour involvement. The rate or extent of hearing loss does not correlate with tumour size or growth.

The English consensus protocol for auditory rehabilitation in NF2 recommends annual audiological assessment including:

- Pure-tone audiometry (PTA)
- Speech discrimination testing with Arthur Boothroyd (AB) word test
- Hearing aid review
- If speech discrimination scores (SDS) fall below 50%, Bamford-Kowal-Bench (BKB) sentence testing is recommended
- Assessment for auditory implantation if BKB scores are below 50% in best-aided conditions

**Table: Indications for CI vs ABI in NF2**

| Scenario | CI Indicated | ABI Indicated |
|----------|-------------|---------------|
| VS excision planned | CN preservation possible | CN preservation not possible |
| Post-excision | CN preserved, good ECAP/EABR | CN preserved but poor ECAP/EABR |
| Contralateral VS excised | Hearing present contralaterally | No contralateral hearing or poor CI/ABI |
| Bilateral profound HL, no surgery | Small-medium stable tumours | Previous CN loss contralaterally |

### ABI in Congenital Hearing Loss

A small but important group includes infants and children with bilateral congenital profound hearing loss who have inner ear or cochlear nerve (CN) abnormalities that preclude CI. These include children with absent cochleas, absent CNs, or a combination. For every 100 children referred for CI, approximately one or two will have abnormalities making them unsuitable for CI.

## PART C: PRE-OPERATIVE ASSESSMENT AND DECISION-MAKING

### Audiological Assessment

Auditory implant assessment involves:

- Free-field speech discrimination testing with and without lip-reading
- Testing with hearing aids
- Environmental noise discrimination tests
- Promontory electrical stimulation in an apparent dead ear (transtympanic needle on the promontory to elicit auditory percept) - auditory perception suggests a functioning CN but does not predict CI outcome
- Transtympanic electrical auditory brainstem response (TTEABR) testing - correlates well with CI-evoked EABR and aids decision-making in CN aplasia/hypoplasia

### Intra-operative Monitoring

Monitoring of the auditory nerve to aid hearing preservation includes:

- **Cochlear nerve action potential (CNAP) monitoring:** A multi-strand silver wire attached to a cotton pledget placed proximal to the tumour adjacent to the CN acts as the positive electrode
- **Fast auditory brainstem responses (ABR):** Elicited by a 104 dB SPL click stimulus from an ear mould placed in the ear canal pre-operatively. The surgeon is alerted if there is a change in latency of wave V of >0.5 milliseconds

The presence of post-tumour resection ABR, CNAP, EABR or CI-evoked EABR provides prognostic information and is an indicator of residual auditory nerve transmission. If these are negative and/or the auditory nerve has been sacrificed, ABI is the auditory rehabilitation choice.

### Multidisciplinary Decision-Making

Surgery is usually only offered once hearing has deteriorated below 50% on BKB sentence testing. CN preservation surgery may be attempted for growing tumours up to 3 cm intracranial tumour diameter (ICTD), but the likelihood of success diminishes progressively over 1 cm ICTD. Hearing preservation is rarely possible for tumours >1.5 cm ICTD.

Key counselling points include:

- Pictorial explanation of the site/positioning of the implants
- The possibility of the implant being a "sleeper" device while serviceable hearing is present contralaterally
- The range of possible outcomes and difficulties with predicting prognosis
- Managing patient expectations is crucial
- The immense workload and commitment required for rehabilitation

## PART D: SURGICAL CONSIDERATIONS

### Surgical Approaches

- **Middle fossa, retrosigmoid and retrosigmoid-retrolabyrinthine approaches:** Options for hearing preservation in small tumours; middle fossa favoured when more tumour is in the fundus of the internal auditory canal
- **Translabyrinthine and retrosigmoid approaches:** Commonest for larger tumours
- **Modified translabyrinthine approach:** Described to enhance access for large tumours

Extent of tumour resection is classified as:

- **Total:** Complete excision
- **Near-total:** <1% of tumour volume remaining
- **Subtotal:** 1-5% of tumour remaining
- **Partial:** >5% of tumour remaining

Total tumour excision is achievable in 78-92% of patients.

### ABI Implantation Technique

Auditory implantation usually takes place after tumour resection at the same operation. Key surgical points include:

- Meticulous surgical technique to minimize brainstem trauma from direct physical damage or collateral effects of electrocautery
- Early division of the vestibulocochlear nerve at the brainstem may be considered with large tumours or when CN preservation is unlikely
- A well or anterior lip is drilled on the skull to recess the ABI in a tight periosteal pocket to prevent migration
- A channel for the array can be drilled from the well to the edge of the craniotomy/[[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]]
- **Intra-operative EABR testing** is critical to optimize paddle position - four corners of the array are activated in succession, followed by other electrodes
- Simultaneous facial and glossopharyngeal nerve monitoring takes place
- The silicone mesh on the ABI paddle aids adherence to the brainstem but may be a disadvantage when repositioning
- Fibrin and dural sealants can be used to help prevent displacement

### Challenges in NF2

- Distortion of brainstem anatomy makes placement more difficult than in non-tumour cases
- Subsequent remodelling of the brainstem to fill the tumour void may lead to movement or displacement of the array
- NF2 VSs are more aggressive than sporadic VS - NF2 schwannomas may encapsulate the fibres of the CN rather than stretching and spreading

## PART E: POST-OPERATIVE CARE AND PROGRAMMING

Post-operative care is similar to that for non-implantation VS surgery with antibiotic prophylaxis and pre-operative vaccinations 2 weeks or more before surgery (as per CI).

### ABI Activation

ABI activation differs significantly from CI activation:

- Done with the patient **nil by mouth** in a setting with cardiac monitoring and an anaesthetist present
- This is due to the risk of **non-auditory stimulation**, particularly vagal stimulation causing bradycardia, tachyarrhythmia or the need for a secure airway
- Each electrode is stimulated individually, then in groups across the array
- Threshold and comfort levels are varied
- Auditory perception with feedback on loudness and pitch enables programming
- Numerous appointments over the first year enable adjustment of current levels

### Tumour Surveillance

Ongoing interval MRI is essential for tumour surveillance. Previously, temporary removal of the magnet from the Cochlear device was required, with resultant problems including infection and magnet degradation. The most recent MED-EL ABI is MRI conditional at 1.5 Tesla without magnet removal due to self-aligning magnet technology. However, artefact remains a problem for monitoring, especially for ipsilateral residual VS.

## PART F: OUTCOMES

Outcomes of ABI are far more variable than outcomes of CI. This variability is due to:

- Uncertain positioning of the paddle in the lateral recess of the fourth ventricle adjacent to the cochlear nucleus, as compared to CI which naturally guides the array around cochlear turns
- Patient factors (NF2/tumour-related or concurrent medical problems)
- Intra-operative factors (mechanical or ischaemic damage to the cochlear nucleus or brainstem)
- Habilitation and performance take longer with ABI and may not plateau for several years compared to several months for most CI users

### Outcomes in NF2

- Initial hearing benefits ranged from sound awareness, identification of some environmental sounds and improved performance over lip-reading alone
- Open-set speech recognition (understanding without visual cues) has been reported in NF2
- Better results achieved with non-NF2 patients
- Short duration of deafness (<1 year) was associated with a mean increase in word recognition score of 20%
- Good results have been reported with deafness durations of 15 years or more

### Factors Associated with Better Outcomes

- Short duration of deafness (<1 year)
- Semi-sitting surgical position (lower intracranial venous pressure, less bleeding, less electrocautery use) - though this carries increased risk of air embolism and tension pneumocephalus
- Lower thresholds with higher stimulation rates (proximity of paddle to stimulable tissue)
- Increasing number of distinct pitch electrodes with good spectrum of pitch perceptions
- Maximum comfortable level (MCL) as an indicator of distance/proximity to the cochlear nucleus

### Outcomes in Children

In one of the largest series (Sennaroglu et al.), long-term outcomes in 35 children undergoing ABI showed:

- The majority achieved grade 5 on the Categories of Auditory Performance (CAP) scale, implying understanding common phrases without lip-reading
- Outcomes were not as good as a comparable age group that had undergone CI
- Children with additional disabilities fared less well than those with no additional physical or cognitive problems

## PART G: RECENT DEVELOPMENTS - ABI IN CHILDREN

### Expansion of Indications

The experience of ABI in NF2 provided impetus for exploration in non-NF2 patients. Colletti and his team pioneered the extension of ABI indications to include congenitally deaf children who are not suitable for CI. This catalyzed centres worldwide including Manchester (UK, first three children in 2005), USA, Turkey, Korea, and subsequently London and Cambridge.

### International Consensus Statements

Sennaroglu convened international meetings of key stakeholders resulting in two consensus statements:

**First Consensus Statement (2011):** Standardization of assessment and indications for ABI in children internationally.

**Second Consensus Statement (2016):** A collaborative exercise involving 20 centres from 11 countries explored clinical and radiological indications, timing of surgery, habilitation and evaluation of outcomes. Key recommendations include:

- Comprehensive MDT drawing on extensive CI and lateral skullbase experience is essential
- **Optimal age for ABI in congenitally deaf children is 18-24 months**, balancing neural plasticity against metabolic stress of intracranial surgery and anaesthesia
- Children with cochlear and/or CN **aplasia** should be considered for ABI
- Children with **hypoplastic CN** should be offered CI first, with fast-tracking to ABI if CI does not confer benefit
- The majority of ABI recipients derive auditory perception but not necessarily speech acquisition, in contrast to children with CI
- Children with additional physical or cognitive disabilities fare less well than non-syndromic otherwise healthy children

### MRI-Compatible Devices

The most recent MED-EL ABI incorporates self-aligning magnet polarity technology enabling 1.5 Tesla MRI scanning without magnet removal, reducing infection risk and improving the feasibility of ongoing tumour surveillance.

---

## FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

- ABI design to optimize electrode array paddle placement with reference to the tonotopicity of the cochlear nuclei
- Electrophysiological evaluation of the cochlear nerve post-NF2 surgery or the hypoplastic cochlear nerve
- Auditory habilitation and rehabilitation of ABI recipients to improve outcomes

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The ABI directly stimulates the cochlear nucleus and is indicated when cochlear or auditory nerve abnormalities preclude cochlear implantation.
> 2. Primary indications include NF2 with bilateral VS where CN preservation is not possible, and congenital cochlear/CN aplasia in children.
> 3. Two commercially available devices exist: Cochlear (21 electrodes) and MED-EL (12 electrodes on a 5.5 x 3.0 mm paddle).
> 4. Surgery is usually offered once hearing deteriorates below 50% on BKB sentence testing in NF2 patients.
> 5. ABI is typically implanted at the same operation as tumour resection.
> 6. Intra-operative EABR testing is critical for optimizing paddle position on the brainstem.
> 7. ABI activation requires cardiac monitoring and anaesthetist presence due to risk of vagal stimulation causing bradycardia.
> 8. Outcomes are more variable than CI and may not plateau for several years compared to months for CI.
> 9. Short duration of deafness (<1 year) is associated with a 20% mean increase in word recognition scores.
> 10. The 2016 international consensus recommends ABI at 18-24 months of age in congenitally deaf children with cochlear/CN aplasia.
> 11. Children with CN hypoplasia should receive CI first, with fast-tracking to ABI if CI fails.
> 12. The latest MED-EL ABI is MRI conditional at 1.5 Tesla without magnet removal due to self-aligning magnet technology.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. ABI outcomes in non-NF2 patients are better than in NF2 patients, likely due to the absence of tumour-related brainstem damage.
> 2. For every 100 children referred for CI, approximately 1-2 will have abnormalities requiring ABI instead.
> 3. The semi-sitting surgical position has been associated with the best ABI outcomes, though it carries risk of air embolism.
> 4. NF2 schwannomas may encapsulate CN fibres (unlike sporadic VS which stretches them), making nerve preservation more challenging.
> 5. Transtympanic EABR testing correlates well with CI-evoked EABR and aids pre-operative decision-making between CI and ABI.
> 6. Total tumour excision is achievable in 78-92% of NF2 patients, but centres with lower total excision rates generally have better functional outcomes.
