# Electroacoustic Stimulation and Hearing Preservation in [[Cochlear Implant|Cochlear Implantation]]

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - **Q12, December 2019:** Describe concepts and its application of Electroacoustic stimulation for management of deaf child. Surgical factors for preserving hearing in cochlear implantation.


**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 11: Paediatric Implantation Otology; Chapter 96: Cochlear Implantation in Adults

---

## INTRODUCTION

Electroacoustic stimulation (EAS) represents a paradigm shift in cochlear implantation, bridging the gap between conventional hearing aids and cochlear implants. It combines electrical stimulation for high-frequency hearing loss with acoustic amplification for preserved low-frequency residual hearing within a single device. This hybrid approach has expanded the candidacy criteria for cochlear implantation and has made hearing preservation during surgery a critical surgical objective. Understanding the concepts of EAS and the surgical factors that influence hearing preservation is essential for optimal outcomes in modern cochlear implant practice.

---

## PART A: ELECTROACOUSTIC STIMULATION (EAS)

### Definition and Concept

Electroacoustic stimulation (EAS) is a dual-modality approach in which:
- **Electrical stimulation** (via the cochlear implant electrode) delivers sound information for the high frequencies
- **Acoustic amplification** (via a hearing aid component) boosts the residual low-frequency hearing
- Both are combined in a **single, unified external processor**

This allows the patient to benefit from the advantages of both natural acoustic hearing in the low frequencies and electrical stimulation in the high frequencies.

### Rationale for EAS

An increasing number of studies have confirmed that partial deafness — most commonly high-frequency hearing loss with residual low-frequency natural hearing — is effectively treated with cochlear implantation. These patients, particularly children, are difficult to aid with conventional hearing aids because of the "ski-slope" nature of the hearing loss. They may have:
- Limited ability to hear consonants and fricative sounds
- Good low-tone hearing, which is important for appreciation of music
- Poor speech discrimination despite adequate amplification

### Mechanism of Action

| Component | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| **Cochlear implant electrode** | Provides electrical stimulation for high-frequency hearing (typically above 1000-1500 Hz) |
| **Acoustic hearing aid** | Amplifies residual low-frequency hearing (typically below 500-1000 Hz) |
| **Combined processor** | Integrates both signals into a single wearable speech processor |

### Advantages of EAS Over Conventional CI

1. **Binaural benefits:** Preserving low-frequency hearing allows access to interaural level and timing differences, which facilitates:
   - Sound localization
   - Binaural effects that increase speech understanding in background noise
2. **Music appreciation:** Low-frequency acoustic hearing preserves the perception of musical pitch and melody
3. **Natural sound quality:** Combined acoustic and electrical stimulation provides a more natural listening experience
4. **Better speech perception in noise:** Compared to a conventional CI without low-frequency residual hearing

### Candidacy Criteria for EAS

| Parameter | Criteria |
|-----------|---------|
| **Audiometric profile** | Ski-slope hearing loss with preserved low-frequency hearing |
| **Pre-operative low-frequency thresholds** | Should be better than 60 dB HL (hybrid strategy usually not attempted if ≥60 dB HL pre-operatively) |
| **Upper limit for effectiveness** | Once residual hearing is below 80 dB HL, the approach is ineffective as residual hearing is no longer aidable |
| **Ideal range** | Low-frequency PTA between 40-60 dB HL with high-frequency PTA >90 dB HL |

### Electrode Options for EAS

1. **Short electrodes (Hybrid electrodes):**
   - Cochlear Hybrid electrode — shorter than standard, enters only the basal turn
   - Covers only the higher frequencies
   - Minimizes trauma to apical cochlear structures
   - Risk: If residual hearing is lost, full frequency range is not covered

2. **Medium/Standard-length atraumatic electrodes (preferred by many surgeons):**
   - Allow hearing preservation while providing "complete cochlea coverage"
   - If residual hearing is eventually lost, the full range of frequencies is covered by the electrode
   - Ensure optimal performance regardless of hearing preservation outcome

3. **Flexible lateral wall electrodes:**
   - MED-EL FLEX electrode — designed to minimize intracochlear trauma
   - Lateral wall positioning reduces risk of basilar membrane disruption

### Application of EAS in Children

**Success rates:** It is possible to implant with an approximately 90% chance of preserving useful hearing using a variety of electrodes

**Expected hearing shift:** There is an incidence of late loss of hearing a few months after surgery, and it is usual to lose around 10-20 dB of hearing

**Long-term concern in children:** Children may lose residual hearing eventually during the natural process of ageing in the cochlea, a process which often occurs early in individuals with hearing loss. However, modern hearing preservation electrodes are full or near-full length and will function well even if the residual hearing is lost.

**Wider application:** A wider range of severely deaf children can now be offered cochlear implants, especially as residual hearing can usually be preserved, allowing hybrid electroacoustic stimulation in both ears.

## PART B: SURGICAL FACTORS FOR PRESERVING HEARING IN COCHLEAR IMPLANTATION

### 1. Electrode Position Within Scala Tympani

The ideal position of the electrode is within the **scala tympani** where it sits close to the neural elements.

**Critical principle:** Disruption of the basilar membrane by traumatic insertion or displacement into the scala vestibuli causes:
- Complete loss of residual hearing
- Potentially worse overall outcomes

**Types of electrode positioning:**

| Position | Example | Characteristics |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| **Modiolar hugging** | Cochlear Contour | Close to modiolus; may cause more trauma during insertion |
| **Mid-scala** | Advanced Bionics Mid-Scala | Intermediate position; balanced approach |
| **Lateral wall** | MED-EL FLEX | Less trauma to modiolus; preferred for hearing preservation |

### 2. Electrode Design and Size

- The cross-sectional size of electrodes is progressively reducing
- Purpose: To improve ease of insertion and avoid trauma to inner ear structures
- Current electrodes allow preservation of residual hearing in many cases and may improve long-term cochlear health
- Electrodes with plugs (MED-EL) help seal the cochleostomy and prevent CSF escape
- Electrodes with wings (Cochlear Hybrid and EAS electrodes) help positioning

### 3. Cochleostomy Approach: Round Window vs Bony Cochleostomy

**Round Window Approach:**
- A small perforation is made by a needle in the round window membrane
- **Advantages:**
  - Helps seal the entry site of the electrode into the cochlea
  - No need for intracochlear drilling
  - Minimizes bone dust and blood entering the cochlea
  - Reduced acoustic trauma

**Bony Cochleostomy:**
- Placed inferior to the round window
- Involves meticulous drilling with exposure of the endosteum
- Endosteum is removed with a small hook
- Minimizes trauma to the cochlea
- Prevents entry of bone dust or blood into the cochlea

**Both techniques** have been shown to preserve low-tone hearing and minimize cochlear trauma. "Soft surgery" may also minimize scar formation within the cochlea if the child needs revision surgery in the future.

### 4. Soft Surgery / Atraumatic Insertion Technique

The concept of "soft surgery" encompasses the following principles:

| Principle | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Slow, gentle electrode insertion** | Reduces mechanical trauma to cochlear structures |
| **Avoidance of bone dust entry** | Prevents inflammatory response within cochlea |
| **Avoidance of blood entry** | Blood products can cause fibrosis and new bone formation |
| **Avoidance of suctioning perilymph** | Maintains cochlear fluid homeostasis |
| **Minimal drilling** | Reduces vibration-induced acoustic trauma |
| **Use of soft, flexible electrodes** | Conforms to cochlear anatomy, reducing intracochlear damage |

### 5. Insertion Forces

Insertion forces appear to be **crucial** to maintain a healthy cochlea for:
- Long-term health of the inner ear
- Hearing preservation

**Robotic insertion** to reduce these forces further is an active area of research interest with promising early studies. Robots ensure a smooth and atraumatic electrode insertion, and can also perform minimally invasive and atraumatic drilling of the temporal bone and cochlea.

### 6. Sealing the Cochleostomy

Once the electrode has been inserted:
- The electrode entry site is packed with **fascia or periosteum** to prevent leak of perilymph
- **Important:** In cases of residual hearing, it is crucial NOT to overpack the middle ear, as this may cause a degree of conductive hearing loss of the residual hearing frequencies

### 7. Choice of Electrode Length

| Strategy | Electrode | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|----------|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| **Short electrode (Hybrid)** | Cochlear Hybrid | Minimal apical trauma; best for hearing preservation | If hearing is lost, limited frequency coverage |
| **Medium/Standard atraumatic** | Various | "Complete cochlea coverage"; works even if residual hearing is lost | Slightly higher risk of apical trauma |

Many surgeons prefer medium or standard-length atraumatic electrodes because:
- Residual hearing can often still be preserved
- If hearing is lost, the full range of frequencies is covered
- Ensures optimal performance regardless of outcome

### 8. Intra-operative Monitoring

- Intra-operative testing to confirm electrode integrity and measure impedance is standard practice
- Neural response testing (NRT/ECAP) confirms electrode-neural interface function
- Post-operative X-ray (Stenver's view) confirms electrode position and identifies potential tip fold-over

### 9. Additional Surgical Considerations

- **Bilateral facial nerve monitoring** is required for bilateral surgery
- **Cortical [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]]** with posterior tympanotomy approach
- Key landmarks: incus short process, facial nerve, chorda tympani nerve, incudostapedial joint, round window membrane
- **Surgery should be atraumatic** to preserve cochlear function — this is a consensus principle

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. EAS combines electrical stimulation (high frequencies) and acoustic amplification (low frequencies) in a single processor
> 2. EAS is indicated for ski-slope hearing loss with pre-operative low-frequency thresholds better than 60 dB HL
> 3. Once residual hearing drops below 80 dB HL, the EAS approach becomes ineffective as hearing is no longer aidable
> 4. Approximately 90% chance of preserving useful hearing with appropriate electrodes and careful surgery
> 5. Typical post-operative hearing loss is 10-20 dB in the preserved frequencies
> 6. Disruption of the basilar membrane or displacement into scala vestibuli causes complete loss of residual hearing
> 7. Round window approach and soft cochleostomy are both effective for hearing preservation; round window avoids intracochlear drilling
> 8. Electrode entry site must be sealed with fascia/periosteum but NOT overpacked (risk of conductive loss)
> 9. Insertion forces are a crucial factor — robotic insertion is an emerging technique to minimize trauma
> 10. Modern hearing preservation electrodes are full or near-full length, ensuring function even if residual hearing is eventually lost
> 11. Cross-sectional size of electrodes is progressively reducing to improve insertion ease and minimize trauma
> 12. Lateral wall electrodes (e.g., MED-EL FLEX) are preferred for hearing preservation over modiolar hugging electrodes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. For EAS candidates, many surgeons prefer standard-length atraumatic electrodes over short hybrid electrodes because they provide "complete cochlea coverage" as a safety net if residual hearing is lost
> 2. In children, there is concern about eventual loss of residual hearing due to natural cochlear ageing — a process that often occurs early in those with pre-existing hearing loss
> 3. The hybrid strategy should not be attempted if pre-operative low-frequency hearing is worse than 60 dB HL
> 4. Avoiding entry of bone dust and blood into the cochlea during cochleostomy is as important as the insertion technique itself
> 5. Scar formation within the cochlea is minimized by soft surgery, which is important if the child needs revision surgery
> 6. Drug-eluting electrodes that deliver anti-inflammatory or neurotrophic agents directly into the inner ear to prevent post-implantation neural degeneration represent a promising future development
