## Part A: Surgical Techniques for Otosclerosis (Q3, Q113)

### Indications for Surgery
- Conductive hearing loss due to stapes fixation with air–bone gap ≥20 dB (traditional); some expert surgeons operate on air–bone gaps as small as 10 dB
- Normal tympanic membrane with confirmed/presumed otosclerosis
- Absence of active middle ear infection or cholesteatoma (absolute contraindication)
- General fitness for anaesthesia

### Contraindications and Controversies
| Factor | Consideration |
|---|---|
| Only hearing ear | Relative contraindication — risk of dead ear ~1% |
| Active infection | Absolute contraindication |
| Pregnancy | Absolute contraindication for timing of surgery |
| Ménière's disease | Relative contraindication; surgery may proceed if BC levels ≤35 dB at 500 Hz with no high-frequency impairment |
| Eustachian tube dysfunction | Relative contraindication |
| Children | Caution: higher obliterative otosclerosis rate (26–41%); risk of congenital inner ear abnormalities; CT advised for mixed HL |
| Age >70 years | Results similar to younger adults; no upper age limit on technical grounds |

### Anaesthesia
- **General anaesthesia:** Preferred in UK (>90%); hypotensive technique reduces bleeding; provides motionless field
- **Local anaesthesia:** Popular in Europe; 1% lidocaine with 1:30,000–1:100,000 adrenaline; patient can report vertigo during surgery; hearing restoration confirmed intraoperatively; advantageous in revision surgery (patient reports vertiginous symptoms if adhesions contact membranous labyrinth)

### Surgical Exposure
- Permeatal approach (most common); some surgeons use endaural incision
- Speculum holder stabilizes speculum and gives platform for surgeon's hands
- Tympanomeatal flap elevated from 7 o'clock to 12 o'clock (right ear), hinging forwards just posterior to malleus handle
- Chorda tympani nerve gently freed from mucosal folds
- Posterosuperior bony annulus reduced with curettes, drill, or hammer and gouge to expose stapes, oval window, facial nerve, and base of pyramid

### Establishing the Diagnosis at Surgery
- Identify anatomical anomalies: persistent stapedial artery, overhanging facial nerve
- Exclude alternative causes: tympanosclerosis, congenital abnormalities
- Palpate stapes superstructure to check footplate fixation
- Palpate malleus to test for malleus head fixation, anterior ligament and malleoincudal joint fixation
- Assessment is clearer after incudostapedial joint disarticulation

### Stapedotomy Technique (Step-by-Step)
1. **Disarticulate incudostapedial joint** using joint knife (insert from posterior, move anteriorly)
2. **Reassess mobility** of all ossicles (confirm stapes fixation; exclude fixed malleus)
3. **Divide stapedius tendon** with laser, scissors, or microhook
4. **Divide posterior crus** with laser or crurotomy scissors (reduces risk of footplate mobilization)
5. **Divide anterior crus** if possible (may be hidden; use handheld laser angled away from facial nerve)
6. **Remove stapes superstructure** by down-fracturing crura away from facial nerve
7. **Measure distance** from undersurface of incus to footplate surface (remeasure after footplate thinning if thickened)
8. **Create stapedotomy** at junction of middle and inferior thirds of central footplate (where distance to membranous labyrinth is greatest)
9. **Fashion fenestration** slightly wider than prosthesis (0.5–0.6 mm for 0.4 mm prosthesis)
10. **Place interposition graft** if used (vein, fat, or connective tissue)
11. **Position prosthesis** — loop onto incus long process, shaft into fenestration
12. **Crimp loop** (wire: manual crimping; alloy: laser/diathermy for self-crimping prostheses)
13. **Assess** adequate tightness at incus and length at footplate
14. **Seal fenestration** with blood, fat, or connective tissue around prosthesis
15. **Replace tympanomeatal flap** and pack external ear canal

### Stapedectomy Technique
- Approach identical to stapedotomy up to footplate stage
- Control hole made in footplate; posterior third (or entire footplate) removed with picks
- Defect covered with connective tissue graft (most commonly vein from forearm)
- Prosthesis placed on graft and suspended from long process of incus
- Still technique of choice for 19% of UK otolaryngologists (2002 survey)

### Footplate Mobilization
- Laser Stapedotomy Minus Prosthesis (STAMP) procedure
- Laser vaporizes anterior crus + linear stapedotomy across anterior third of footplate
- Depends on ankylosis being confined to anterior third
- Achievable in approximately one-third of operative cases

## Part B: Fenestra Options and Prostheses (Q3)

### Fenestra Options

| Technique | Description | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Stapedotomy (small fenestra)** | 0.4–0.8 mm hole in footplate | Most widely used; less labyrinthine trauma; better high-frequency outcomes |
| **Partial stapedectomy** | Posterior third of footplate removed | Intermediate approach |
| **Total stapedectomy (large fenestra)** | Entire footplate removed | Better low-frequency gain; higher complication rate; falling in popularity |

### Stapedotomy vs Stapedectomy — Comparison

| Parameter | Stapedotomy | Stapedectomy |
|---|---|---|
| Low-frequency gain | Similar (some studies show slightly less) | Similar (some studies show slightly more) |
| High-frequency gain (≥4 kHz) | Superior (consistent finding) | Inferior |
| Perilymphatic fistula rate | 0.6% | 2.0% |
| Sensorineural damage >20 dB | 0% | 1.5% |
| Revision rate | 3% | 7% |
| Post-operative vertigo | Less | More |
| Stability of hearing gain | More stable | Less stable |

### Prosthesis Types

**Design variables:**
- Material: Teflon, stainless steel, gold, platinum, titanium, shape-memory alloys
- Diameter: 0.4 mm, 0.6 mm, 0.8 mm
- Weight: Teflon (~3 mg), gold (~10 mg), steel (~12.5 mg)
- Attachment: wire loop (manual crimping), Teflon loop (self-closing), self-crimping alloy (heat-triggered)

**Common Prostheses:**

| Prosthesis | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Teflon-wire piston (McGee/Robinson)** | Teflon shaft + wire loop; lightweight; 0.5 mm Teflon loop thickness must be factored into length calculation |
| **Titanium piston** | High rigidity, low weight; various designs including clip prostheses |
| **Platinum ribbon prosthesis** | Self-crimping with heat (laser/diathermy); consistent crimping |
| **Bucket-handle prosthesis** | Sits under lenticular process; useful when facial nerve overhangs oval window |
| **Shape-memory alloy (NiTi)** | Self-crimping when heated; easier to use; consistent results; mid-term results stable |

**Prosthesis Diameter:**
- Larger pistons (0.6–0.8 mm) may produce better low-frequency results (4–10 dB advantage)
- Lighter pistons (Teflon) may be better at high frequencies
- Heavier pistons (steel/gold) may be better at low frequencies
- Recent systematic review: no definitive evidence supporting superiority of larger over smaller diameter pistons

**Prosthesis Length:**
- Measured from undersurface of incus to footplate surface
- Selected 0.25 mm longer than measured distance for adequate vestibular penetration
- Typical lengths: 4.25 mm, 4.5 mm, or 4.75 mm

### Connective Tissue Seals
- **Vein graft:** Thinnest option; reduces lateralization risk; most suitable for stapedectomy
- **Perichondrium, fat, fascia:** Increasingly thicker; greater risk of prosthesis lateralization through contracture
- **Tight-fitting laser stapedotomy:** May require no seal — blood or fat placed around prosthesis
- Seal increases sound-transmitting surface area; prevents perilymph leakage; restores annular ligament stiffness

### Endoscopic Stapedotomy
- Safe with similar audiological outcomes to microscopic stapes procedures
- Advantages: possibly reduced bony annulus resection
- Limitations: one-handed surgery; bleeding management

## Part C: Lasers in Otological Surgery (Q71)

### Types of Laser Used in Otology

| Laser | Wavelength | Delivery | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CO₂** | 10,600 nm | Mirror/scanner (non-contact) | Excellent tissue absorption; precise; no deep penetration; flagship laser for stapedotomy |
| **KTP (potassium titanyl phosphate)** | 532 nm | Fibre-optic (handheld) | Green light; absorbed by haemoglobin; useful in vascular tissues; fibre delivery allows handheld probe for angled access |
| **Argon** | 488–514 nm | Fibre-optic | Similar to KTP; blue-green; absorbed by pigmented tissue; can be delivered via operating microscope |
| **Erbium:YAG** | 2,940 nm | Non-contact | High absorption by water; precise; some reports of threshold changes — one group discontinued use |
| **Diode** | 810–980 nm | Fibre-optic | Compact; versatile; increasingly available |

### Laser Applications in Otology

**1. Stapes Surgery (Primary):**
- Division of stapedius tendon
- Division of posterior and anterior crura
- Creation of stapedotomy (rosette pattern or single shot)
- Revision surgery: vaporizing adhesions, freeing ankylosed prosthesis, fashioning new fenestration

**2. Other Otological Applications:**
- Cholesteatoma removal (KTP laser helps precisely remove disease from hidden areas)
- Division of adhesions in middle ear
- Myringotomy
- Lysis of granulation tissue

### Advantages of Laser in Stapes Surgery
1. **Precision:** Controlled tissue removal without mechanical force on footplate
2. **Reduced floating footplate risk:** Fivefold less footplate fracture than conventional fenestration methods (Rauch and Bartley study)
3. **No-touch technique:** Minimal transmission of mechanical force to membranous labyrinth
4. **Haemostasis:** Simultaneous coagulation (particularly KTP/argon)
5. **Reduced complications in revision surgery:** Better assessment of footplate pathology; adhesion clearance
6. **Lower complication rates:** Lower incidence of perilymph fistula and vertigo compared to traditional techniques
7. **Access:** Fibre-optic delivery (KTP, argon) allows access to areas hidden from direct line of sight

### Laser Safety Considerations in Stapes Surgery
- Risk of photothermal, photochemical, and photoacoustic damage to membranous labyrinth
- Temporary threshold changes reported with CO₂, KTP, and erbium:YAG
- Laser should be angled away from facial nerve when dividing anterior crus
- Systematic review inconclusive on which laser is optimum for stapes surgery

### Comparison: Laser vs Traditional Methods
- House Ear Clinic (550 cases): Similar results for laser vs microdrill
- Marquet series (1681 cases): Similar results for picks, drills, and lasers
- Argon laser vs conventional: Fivefold less footplate fracture with laser
- CO₂ vs argon: No outcome differences found
- Overall: Laser may have lower complication rate despite similar hearing outcomes

## Part D: Complications of Stapes Surgery (Q46, Q94)

### Early Complications

**1. Sensorineural Hearing Loss:**
- Most recent series: no dead ears in primary stapedotomy
- Large series: 13/1911 (0.7%) cases of SNHL combining early and late failures (stapedotomy)
- Stapedectomy: generally higher rates (0–11%)
- Revision surgery: 4.4% severe SNHL rate (113/2585 revision cases)
- Dead ear rate in revisions: 0–14%
- ~50% of SNHL episodes are delayed (months to years post-operatively)
- Early mild SNHL common: 62% at day 5 post-op, reducing to 32% at 6 months

**Causes of peri-operative SNHL:**
- Excessive drilling (obliterative otosclerosis)
- Floating footplate (<1% of cases)
- Perilymph aspiration
- Congenital footplate fixation with CSF gusher
- Blood entering oval window

**2. Vertigo:**
- Transient dysequilibrium: common, managed with vestibular sedatives
- Serous labyrinthitis: vertigo + SNHL + [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] in first week; rapid recovery expected
- [[BPPV|BPPV]]: reported in 4/63 post-stapedectomy patients; treated with Epley manoeuvre
- Overlong prosthesis: positive fistula test on pneumatic otoscopy
- Other causes: depressed footplate, bony fragments on saccule, suppurative labyrinthitis, endolymphatic hydrops

**3. Facial Nerve Injury:**
- Immediate temporary palsy: from local anaesthetic infiltration
- Delayed temporary palsy: days 4–10 post-op; 0.1–0.5% rate; full recovery always reported
- Permanent injury: very rare (0.07% in largest series)
- Associated with: aberrant nerve crossing footplate, laser/burr injury with dehiscent canal, clearing fibrous tissue at revision
- Facial nerve dehiscent above oval window in ~10% of ears

**4. Tympanic Membrane Perforation:**
- ~2% of cases
- Usually small tears; easily repaired with underlay [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|myringoplasty]] (spare vein graft or perichondrium)
- Long-term results unchanged

**5. CSF Gusher:**
- Rare (0.03%); rapid fluid flow on entering vestibule
- Most common in congenital mixed hearing impairment
- From abnormally wide cochlear aqueduct or IAC defect
- Management: position head up → tissue graft over oval window + prosthesis to anchor → lumbar drain if persistent → rarely cochlear aqueduct ablation or IAC packing

### Late Complications

**6. Conductive Hearing Loss (Post-operative):**
Three main causes at revision surgery:
- **Prosthesis displacement** from fenestration: 36% of revisions (range 18–82%)
  - Causes: short prosthesis, Valsalva/sneezing, connective tissue contracture, adhesion traction
- **Incus erosion:** 21% of revisions (range 5–32%)
  - May be from foreign body reaction or reduced blood supply
  - Fluctuating hearing; coexists with displacement
- **Bony regrowth** over fenestration: 1–24% (more common after obliterative otosclerosis)
- Fibrous adhesions: 8–24% of revisions
- Footplate refixation: after mobilization procedures (rarely performed now)

**7. Perilymphatic Fistula:**
- 0.25–2.5% after stapedectomy; significantly lower after stapedotomy
- Found at 1.5–12% of revisions
- Symptoms: fluctuating hearing loss (71–87%), vertigo (~33%), tinnitus (28–45%), fullness
- Greatest risk factor: Gelfoam used to seal (now largely abandoned)
- Management: re-exploration → graft defect + new prosthesis; or pack footplate with fat if prosthesis in vestibule with adhesions

**8. Reparative Granuloma:**
- 0.1% post-stapedotomy; 0.07% historical post-stapedectomy (current estimate higher)
- Usually presents 1–2 weeks post-surgery (rarely up to 2 years)
- 70–100%: progressive or sudden SNHL after initial improvement
- 20–35%: vertigo; may have otalgia
- Examination: dull red TM in posterosuperior quadrant
- Associated with graft material: most commonly Gelfoam or fat
- Management: controversial — many advise early surgical intervention within 2 weeks; others favour conservative steroids/antibiotics initially; outcomes split between improvement, stabilization, and profound impairment

**9. Alteration in Taste:**
- Chorda tympani damage: up to 30% of primary cases; only 20% intact at revision
- Symptoms: metallic taste, impaired taste, dry mouth, sore tongue — typically 3–4 months
- Cutting nerve → greater and longer-lasting symptoms than preserving it
- Bilateral damage causes severe symptoms

**10. Discomfort to Loud Noise:**
- Reported in 35–41% following stapes surgery
- May reflect improved hearing rather than true hyperacusis
- Loudness discomfort levels seem little changed

**11. Tinnitus:**
- Pre-operative: 56–79% of patients
- Post-operative improvement: 44% no tinnitus, 34% less, 16% equal, 5% more
- Low-pitched tinnitus: good prognosis; high-pitched: poor prognosis
- New tinnitus post-operatively: 0–16% (most papers: no new cases)

**12. Cholesteatoma:**
- Rare; skin elements implanted during fat graft harvesting in oval window

**13. Meningitis:**
- Reported (especially older literature) following stapedectomy with fistula formation
- Some advocate exploratory tympanotomy in meningitis patients with stapes prosthesis

## Part E: Management of Dislocated Incus (Q46)

### Causes of Incus Dislocation During Stapes Surgery
- Excessive curettage of bony annulus
- Instrumentation of oval window
- Excessive manipulation of incudostapedial joint
- May also be encountered as pre-existing congenital or inflammatory fixation (1% of stapedectomies for incudomalleolar fixation)

### Management Options
1. **If incus maintains anatomical position:** Continue with standard incus-to-footplate prosthesis placement
2. **If incus subluxed but retrievable:** Attempt repositioning; if successful, use standard prosthesis with careful crimping
3. **If incus displacement prevents standard prosthesis use:** Perform **malleostapedotomy** — prosthesis attached to malleus handle rather than incus
   - Wire loop firmly attached to malleus handle (biomechanical evidence emphasizes firm attachment to prevent tilting)
   - Shaft enters stapedotomy fenestration
   - Angulated prosthesis may cause tilting movements at footplate
4. **Incus erosion at revision:** Similar management — malleostapedotomy is recommended in most cases of severe incus erosion (Fisch reports malleus fixation in 46% of revision stapedectomies)

### Revision Surgery Considerations
- More revisions → poorer expected results: 76% closure to 10 dB for 1st revision, 66% for 2nd, 57% for 3rd, 38% for 4th
- Local anaesthesia recommended by many centres (patient reports vertigo if adhesions contact membranous labyrinth)
- Laser recommended: vaporizing adhesions, freeing prosthesis, fashioning new fenestration
- Higher SNHL risk: 4.4% vs <1% primary
