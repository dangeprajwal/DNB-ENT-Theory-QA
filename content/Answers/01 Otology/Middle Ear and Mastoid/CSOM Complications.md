# Complications of Otitis Media & Lateral Sinus Thrombosis

## Questions Covered
- **Q11 (2019):** Enumerate complications of otitis media. Discuss etiopathogenesis, symptomatology & treatment of Sigmoid sinus thrombosis.
- **Q96 (2024):** Briefly outline the pathways of spread of complications of otitis media. Discuss the management of lateral sinus thrombosis.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology 8th Edition, Volume 2 — Chapter 83 (Chronic Otitis Media) and Chapter 84 (Intracranial Complications of Otitis Media)

## Part A: Classification of Complications of Otitis Media (Q11, Q96)

Complications of otitis media may be broadly classified into intratemporal and intracranial categories. These complications arise when infection extends beyond the confines of the middle ear cleft. Multiple intracranial complications may coexist, and lateral sinus thrombosis is frequently complicated by associated intracranial septic complications such as meningitis or brain abscess.

| Category | Complications |
|---|---|
| **Intratemporal (Extracranial)** | Acute mastoiditis with subperiosteal abscess, Petrositis (Gradenigo's syndrome), Facial nerve paralysis, Labyrinthitis (serous, suppurative, fistula) |
| **Intracranial** | Meningitis, Extradural abscess, Subdural abscess/empyema, Brain abscess (temporal lobe, cerebellar), Lateral (sigmoid) sinus thrombosis, Otitic hydrocephalus |

### Intratemporal Complications

#### Acute Mastoiditis with Subperiosteal Abscess

Acute mastoiditis represents extension of infection from the middle ear cleft to the mastoid cortex with periosteal breach. When the infection erodes through the cortical bone, a subperiosteal abscess forms beneath the periosteum overlying the mastoid process. The presentation is characteristic, with a swollen, tender, erythematous mastoid region that pushes the auricle forward and downward.

Several named variants exist depending on the route of bony erosion. **Bezold's abscess** occurs when infection ruptures through the mastoid tip into the sternocleidomastoid and digastric region, tracking inferiorly into the neck along fascial planes. **Citelli's abscess** develops when infection extends through the digastric groove. **Luc's abscess** forms when infection penetrates through the zygomatic root into the temporal fossa, presenting as a temporal swelling above the ear.

#### Petrositis (Gradenigo's Syndrome)

Petrositis develops when infection spreads from the mastoid air cell system to the petrous apex air cells. The petrous apex is an anatomically deep structure lying medial to the middle ear, and infection in this region has close anatomical relationships to the abducens nerve at Dorello's canal and the trigeminal ganglion at Meckel's cave.

The clinical presentation is the classic **Gradenigo's triad**: VI cranial nerve palsy (producing lateral rectus paralysis and diplopia), retro-orbital or deep facial pain (from trigeminal nerve irritation at the petrous apex), and ipsilateral otorrhea (from the underlying chronic otitis media). Diagnosis is confirmed by high-resolution CT demonstrating opacification and erosion of petrous apex air cells. Management involves prolonged intravenous antibiotics and mastoidectomy to address the primary ear disease; surgical drainage of the petrous apex may be required in refractory cases.

#### Facial Nerve Paralysis

Facial nerve paralysis in the context of otitis media occurs from inflammation, oedema, or direct erosion of the bony fallopian canal. The tympanic segment and the second genu are the most vulnerable portions of the facial nerve because the bony canal is thinnest (and sometimes dehiscent) in these locations. Facial nerve paralysis may complicate either acute or chronic otitis media.

In acute otitis media, the mechanism is usually inflammatory oedema around a dehiscent nerve canal, and the paralysis often responds to medical treatment with antibiotics and possible myringotomy. In chronic otitis media, particularly with [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]], the mechanism is direct erosion of the bony canal by the disease process, and mastoid surgery is required to decompress and protect the nerve. The prognosis depends on the severity and duration of the nerve injury, with early intervention producing the best outcomes.

#### Labyrinthitis

Labyrinthitis progresses through three recognized stages, each representing increasing severity of labyrinthine involvement.

- **Serous labyrinthitis** is the earliest stage, in which bacterial toxins (but not bacteria themselves) penetrate the round window or oval window membranes and irritate the membranous labyrinth. The patient experiences irritative symptoms: vertigo with nystagmus directed toward the affected ear, and the hearing loss at this stage may be potentially reversible. Prompt treatment of the underlying infection may allow complete recovery.

- **Suppurative labyrinthitis** represents frank bacterial invasion of the labyrinth. This causes destructive rather than irritative changes, with the nystagmus now directed away from the affected ear (due to loss of vestibular function on that side). Sensorineural hearing loss at this stage is typically profound and irreversible. The patient is severely vertiginous and systemically unwell.

- **Labyrinthine fistula** most commonly results from cholesteatoma eroding the bony labyrinth, with the lateral semicircular canal being the most frequently affected site. The patient demonstrates a positive fistula test (vertigo and nystagmus induced by pressure changes in the external auditory canal). Management requires mastoid surgery with careful handling of the cholesteatoma matrix overlying the fistula — many surgeons leave a small remnant of matrix over the fistula to avoid entering the labyrinth.

### Intracranial Complications

#### Meningitis

Meningitis is the most common intracranial complication of otitis media. It presents as an acute illness with severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, and progressive drowsiness. Positive Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are typically elicitable on examination. Lumbar puncture confirms the diagnosis by demonstrating raised white cell count and protein in the cerebrospinal fluid. Management consists of high-dose intravenous antibiotic therapy and close observation. If there is no improvement after 48 hours or if clinical deterioration occurs, immediate ear exploration is indicated to eradicate the primary source of infection.

#### Extradural Abscess

An extradural abscess is a collection of pus between the dura mater and the inner table of the temporal bone. It is often discovered incidentally during mastoid surgery when granulation tissue or cholesteatoma is found to have eroded the tegmen or sinus plate. The clinical presentation may be asymptomatic or limited to a persistent, deep-seated headache that does not respond to simple analgesics. Because it may be clinically silent, a high index of suspicion during mastoid surgery is important. Management involves drainage of the collection at the time of mastoidectomy with thorough removal of the overlying diseased bone.

#### Subdural Abscess (Empyema)

Subdural empyema is a particularly dangerous complication in which pus collects in the subdural space between the dura and the arachnoid membrane. It tends to present more acutely than brain abscess and carries a high mortality rate. The clinical features include fits or seizures, rapid neurological deterioration, focal neurological signs, and signs of systemic sepsis. The pus can spread rapidly over the surface of the brain within the subdural space. Diagnosis is made by contrast-enhanced CT or MRI, and management requires urgent neurosurgical drainage combined with prolonged intravenous antibiotics and treatment of the underlying ear disease.

#### Brain Abscess

Otogenic brain abscess most commonly affects the temporal lobe (due to its proximity to the tegmen tympani) and the cerebellum (due to its proximity to the posterior fossa plate). The presentation is notoriously insidious, with headache, vomiting, low-grade fever, and progressive drowsiness developing over days to weeks. Temporal lobe abscesses may produce subtle personality changes, nominal dysphasia, or upper quadrantanopia. Cerebellar abscesses present with ataxia, intention tremor, nystagmus, and dysdiadochokinesis.

CT scanning is essential for diagnosis, and MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides superior sensitivity for early abscess detection. Traditional management involves neurosurgical aspiration through a burrhole or formal craniotomy for drainage. More recently, transmastoid drainage combined with mastoidectomy has been described when the abscess is contiguous with the mastoid disease, potentially avoiding a separate neurosurgical procedure. Despite modern treatment, mortality from otogenic brain abscess remains 6-14% in recent series.

#### Lateral (Sigmoid) Sinus Thrombosis

Lateral sinus thrombosis occurs when infection spreads from the mastoid to the lateral venous sinus, inducing mural thrombus formation with potential septic embolization. It is discussed in detail in Parts C through F below.

#### Otitic Hydrocephalus (Benign Intracranial Hypertension)

Otitic hydrocephalus is the rarest intracranial complication of otitis media. It results from lateral sinus thrombosis with obstruction of intracranial venous channels, which impairs CSF absorption at the arachnoid granulations, leading to raised intracranial pressure.

The key clinical finding is papilloedema, which is highly suggestive of this diagnosis. However, early CT is essential to rule out more serious complications such as brain abscess or subdural empyema before attributing papilloedema to otitic hydrocephalus. Management involves reduction of raised intracranial pressure using steroids (dexamethasone), diuretics (acetazolamide, furosemide), and hyperosmolar dehydrating agents (mannitol). Treatment may need to continue for many weeks. In refractory cases, a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt may be required for persistent raised intracranial pressure. Concurrently, mastoidectomy and antibiotics must address the underlying lateral sinus thrombosis.

## Part B: Pathways of Spread of Complications (Q96)

Sepsis spreads from the infected ear to the cranial cavity through several well-defined routes. Understanding these pathways is essential for anticipating complications and guiding surgical management.

### 1. Direct Erosion of Osteitic Bone

This is the most common pathway by which complications develop. In chronic osteitis or cholesteatoma, progressive destruction of the bony walls of the middle ear and mastoid erodes critical barriers. The tegmen tympani and tegmen mastoideum separate the middle ear and mastoid from the middle cranial fossa, and their erosion allows direct contact between infected tissue and the dura or brain. Similarly, erosion of the sinus plate exposes the lateral (sigmoid) venous sinus to infection. Cholesteatoma, with its enzymatic bone-eroding capacity, is particularly prone to causing this form of direct spread.

### 2. Infected Thrombophlebitis of Emissary Veins

The mastoid emissary veins are small venous channels that connect the mastoid venous plexus with the intracranial venous sinuses. Infection can propagate along these veins as an infected thrombophlebitis, extending from the mastoid periosteum into the lumen of the lateral sinus without requiring actual bony erosion. This represents a major route for lateral sinus involvement and explains how thrombosis can develop even when the sinus plate appears intact at surgery.

### 3. Venous Channels Draining the Middle Ear and Mastoid

The diploic and mucosal veins of the middle ear and mastoid are valveless, which allows bidirectional flow of blood and infected material. This means that infection within the middle ear mucosa can spread retrograde through these venous channels into the dural venous sinuses. The absence of valves means there is no mechanism to prevent this reverse flow, making these channels an important conduit for intracranial extension of infection.

### 4. Pre-existing Anatomical Weak Points

Several natural anatomical defects provide potential routes for the spread of infection:

- **Oval window and round window:** These membranes separate the middle ear from the labyrinth. Toxins and bacteria can penetrate these membranes to cause serous or suppurative labyrinthitis. Once the labyrinth is involved, infection may extend further to the intracranial cavity through the internal auditory meatus.
- **Internal auditory meatus:** Infection can travel along the sheaths of the VII and VIII cranial nerves within the internal auditory canal to reach the posterior cranial fossa.
- **Cochlear aqueduct:** This channel provides a direct communication between the perilymph of the cochlea and the cerebrospinal fluid in the subarachnoid space, representing a potential pathway for meningitis.
- **Fracture lines and surgical defects:** Prior temporal bone fractures or previous mastoid surgery may create additional defects in the bony barriers, facilitating spread of infection.

### Clinical Relevance

Antibiotic use may modify the natural progression of these pathways, resulting in "masked mastoiditis" where the classical external signs of mastoid infection are suppressed while intracranial complications develop silently. This phenomenon makes it essential to maintain a high index of suspicion for intracranial complications in any patient with chronic otitis media who develops persistent headache, fever, or neurological symptoms, even in the absence of overt mastoid signs.

## Part C: Lateral (Sigmoid) Sinus Thrombosis — Etiopathogenesis (Q11, Q96)

### Pathogenesis

Lateral sinus thrombosis (LST), also termed sigmoid sinus thrombosis, occurs when infection from the middle ear or mastoid reaches the lateral (sigmoid) venous sinus and induces thrombosis within it. Infection reaches the venous sinus either by direct erosion from the mastoid through the sinus plate or via infected thrombophlebitis of the emissary veins draining the mastoid. Prior to infected thrombus formation, a perisinus abscess (extradural abscess adjacent to the sinus wall) may develop between the sinus and the surrounding bone, serving as a nidus for the subsequent thrombotic process.

### Pathological Sequence

The development of lateral sinus thrombosis follows a characteristic stepwise progression:

1. **Perisinus inflammation and abscess formation** develops adjacent to the sinus wall as infected granulation tissue or cholesteatoma erodes the sinus plate. This perisinus collection irritates the outer wall of the sinus.
2. **Mural thrombus formation** occurs on the inner wall of the sinus at the site of perisinus inflammation. The inflammatory process damages the sinus endothelium and activates the clotting cascade, leading to the deposition of a thrombus on the sinus wall.
3. **Thrombus propagation** occurs as the mural thrombus gradually extends along the sinus lumen. The thrombus may propagate proximally toward the torcula (confluence of sinuses) and transverse sinus, or distally toward the jugular bulb and internal jugular vein.
4. **Organization and sinus occlusion** develop as the thrombus matures and may completely occlude the sinus lumen. Complete occlusion obstructs venous drainage from the ipsilateral cerebral hemisphere, contributing to raised intracranial pressure.
5. **Septic embolization** occurs when fragments of the infected thrombus break off and travel through the venous system to distant sites. The most common destination is the pulmonary vasculature, leading to septic pulmonary emboli.
6. **Metastatic abscess formation** results from septic emboli lodging in distant organs, particularly the lungs, where they can produce pulmonary abscesses and infiltrates.
7. **Sinus occlusion and venous hypertension** — complete obstruction of the lateral sinus impairs intracranial venous drainage and may lead to otitic hydrocephalus (benign intracranial hypertension) if the contralateral sinus cannot compensate adequately.
8. **Distal propagation to the jugular vein** — the thrombus may extend from the sigmoid sinus into the jugular bulb and down the internal jugular vein, producing a palpable, tender cord-like swelling in the neck.

### Predisposing Factors

The most important predisposing factor is **cholesteatoma**, which causes direct bony erosion of the sinus plate by the cholesteatoma matrix and associated osteitis, bringing infected material into immediate contact with the sinus wall. An adjacent **perisinus abscess** (extradural collection) causes an inflammatory reaction of the sinus wall, promoting mural thrombus deposition. **Septic thrombophlebitis of the emissary veins** provides an alternative route whereby infected clot within the emissary veins extends directly into the main sinus lumen. Finally, **acute coalescent mastoiditis** with rapid bony destruction may expose the sinus plate even without cholesteatoma.

## Part D: Lateral Sinus Thrombosis — Clinical Features (Q11, Q96)

Clinical presentation is often modified by prior antibiotic therapy ("masked mastoiditis"), making diagnosis challenging in the modern era. The clinician must maintain a high index of suspicion in any patient with chronic otitis media who develops systemic signs of sepsis or neurological symptoms.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Persistent pyrexia with swinging/picket-fence pattern** is the hallmark feature of lateral sinus thrombosis. The temperature chart characteristically shows dramatic spiking fevers interspersed with periods of near-normal temperature, creating a "picket-fence" appearance. Each spike is accompanied by rigors (shaking chills) and drenching sweats, reflecting repeated showering of septic emboli from the infected thrombus into the bloodstream. This classic pattern may be significantly blunted or abolished by concurrent antibiotic therapy.

- **Otalgia** is typically deep-seated and persistent, reflecting the underlying ear disease and the adjacent perisinus inflammatory process.

- **Headache** tends to be severe, often ipsilateral initially but may become generalized as the illness progresses. It reflects raised intracranial venous pressure from sinus obstruction and associated dural irritation.

- **Mastoid and neck tenderness** develops along the course of the internal jugular vein in the neck, particularly if the thrombus has propagated distally into the jugular bulb and internal jugular vein.

- **Griesinger's sign** is a classic clinical sign consisting of tenderness and/or oedema over the mastoid emissary vein, located posteroinferior to the mastoid process. This sign indicates that the thrombophlebitis has extended through the emissary vein from the lateral sinus to the subcutaneous tissues overlying the mastoid. Although classic and diagnostically important, it is not always present.

- **Systemically unwell appearance** — the patient appears toxic, with malaise, anorexia, and general ill health reflecting the ongoing septicaemia.

- **Drowsiness and lethargy** develop progressively as the illness worsens, indicating increasing intracranial compromise from venous hypertension and systemic sepsis.

- **Papilloedema** is frequently present and is highly suggestive of lateral sinus thrombosis. It results from impaired intracranial venous drainage secondary to sinus occlusion, leading to raised intracranial pressure and swelling of the optic disc. Its presence should prompt urgent imaging.

- **VI nerve palsy** may occur as a false localizing sign due to raised intracranial pressure rather than direct involvement of the abducens nerve.

### Complications of Propagating Thrombus

As the thrombus extends along the venous system, several secondary complications may develop:

- **Positive Queckenstedt test:** This test is performed during lumbar puncture. Compression of the ipsilateral internal jugular vein fails to produce the expected rise in CSF pressure, indicating that the lateral sinus on that side is occluded by thrombus. Compression of the contralateral internal jugular vein produces a normal CSF pressure rise, confirming that the obstruction is unilateral. This test provides functional confirmation of sinus occlusion.

- **Septic pulmonary emboli** result from fragments of infected thrombus breaking off and travelling through the venous system to the lungs. The patient may develop chest symptoms including cough, pleuritic pain, and haemoptysis. Chest imaging reveals pulmonary infiltrates, and frank lung abscesses may develop.

- **Internal jugular vein thrombosis** occurs when the thrombus propagates distally from the sigmoid sinus through the jugular bulb into the internal jugular vein. Clinically, this manifests as a tender, cord-like swelling palpable in the neck along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle.

- **Associated intracranial complications** — meningitis and brain abscess (temporal lobe or cerebellar) may coexist with lateral sinus thrombosis. This underlines the importance of comprehensive imaging when one intracranial complication is identified.

- **Otitic hydrocephalus** develops when sinus occlusion impairs overall intracranial venous drainage beyond the capacity of the contralateral sinus to compensate. This causes benign intracranial hypertension with prominent papilloedema and severe headache.

## Part E: Lateral Sinus Thrombosis — Investigations (Q11, Q96)

A combination of imaging studies and laboratory investigations is required to confirm the diagnosis, assess the extent of disease, and guide management.

### Imaging

**CT scan with contrast** is usually the first imaging study obtained and provides important diagnostic information. The characteristic finding is the **delta sign** (also called the empty triangle sign), which represents a filling defect within the sinus where the non-enhancing thrombus is surrounded by the enhancing sinus wall and collateral veins. Perisinus enhancement indicates adjacent inflammatory reaction. CT also demonstrates the extent of mastoid disease, which is essential for surgical planning.

**High-resolution CT (HRCT) of the temporal bone** provides detailed assessment of the mastoid pathology, including cholesteatoma, coalescent mastoiditis, and bone erosion near the sinus plate. This study is essential for surgical planning as it defines the extent of temporal bone disease and identifies the anatomical landmarks for mastoidectomy.

**MRI with MR venography (MRV)** is considered the gold standard investigation for confirming lateral sinus thrombosis. It directly visualizes the thrombus within the sinus and accurately assesses the extent of propagation — whether the thrombus extends into the transverse sinus, the torcula, or distally into the jugular bulb and internal jugular vein. MRV demonstrates the presence or absence of flow within the sinus, providing functional information about the degree of obstruction. Additionally, MRI evaluates for associated intracranial complications such as brain abscess, meningitis, or subdural empyema, making it the most comprehensive single investigation.

### Laboratory and Other Investigations

**Blood cultures** are essential and should be obtained before commencing antibiotic therapy whenever possible. In septic cases, cultures may grow mixed organisms including both aerobes and anaerobes. Positive blood cultures confirm the diagnosis of septicaemia and guide targeted antibiotic therapy.

**Lumbar puncture** is performed to assess the cerebrospinal fluid and to perform the Queckenstedt test. CSF analysis typically shows raised protein and pleocytosis (elevated white cell count). The **positive Queckenstedt test** — absence of CSF pressure rise on compression of the ipsilateral internal jugular vein while normal rise occurs with contralateral compression — confirms sinus occlusion. Lumbar puncture also helps exclude concurrent meningitis.

**Full blood count** typically demonstrates leucocytosis with neutrophilia, reflecting the acute infectious process. Chronic cases may show anaemia. **Inflammatory markers** including C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are characteristically elevated and are useful for monitoring treatment response.

**Ear swab culture** guides initial antibiotic selection but it is important to recognize that the organisms cultured from ear discharge do not necessarily correlate with the organisms present in the intracranial abscess or thrombus. Broad-spectrum cover is therefore essential from the outset.

## Part F: Management of Lateral Sinus Thrombosis (Q11, Q96)

### General Principles of Management of Intracranial Complications

All intracranial complications of otitis media share four fundamental management principles:

1. **Antibiotics** — Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics must be started immediately in effective dosage without awaiting culture results. Ear swabs do not necessarily correlate with abscess swab organisms, so empirical broad-spectrum cover is essential from the outset. Antibiotic choice depends on the likely organisms and the suspected location of infection.

2. **Full neurological observations** — The patient must be regularly monitored with neurological observations including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupil reactions, and vital signs. Any deterioration mandates urgent re-evaluation and repeat imaging.

3. **Neurosurgical advice** — A neurosurgeon should be involved early in the management of any intracranial complication. The neurosurgical team usually directs the management of any associated intracranial collections (brain abscess, subdural empyema).

4. **Management of the ear** — Most clinicians advocate early surgical management of the ear disease to eradicate the primary source of infection. Mastoidectomy removes the nidus from which intracranial complications have arisen.

### Specific Management of Lateral Sinus Thrombosis

#### 1. Antibiotic Therapy

Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are commenced immediately upon diagnosis. A typical regimen includes **ceftriaxone** (or cefotaxime) combined with **metronidazole** for anaerobic cover. **Vancomycin** is added if MRSA is suspected or in severely septic patients. The duration of intravenous therapy is typically 4-6 weeks, adjusted according to clinical response and blood culture results. Ear swab cultures guide initial therapy but may not reflect the intracranial organisms, so broad-spectrum cover must be maintained until culture-directed narrowing is possible.

#### 2. Surgical Management

The surgical management of lateral sinus thrombosis centres on **mastoidectomy** to eradicate the primary source of infection in the middle ear and mastoid.

1. Perform mastoidectomy — canal wall up (CWU) is preferred when cholesteatoma can be completely removed; canal wall down (CWD) is performed when more extensive disease is present or when complete visualization of the sinus plate is required.
2. Remove all bone overlying the lateral sinus to fully expose the sinus plate.
3. Drain any perisinus abscess (extradural collection adjacent to the sinus wall).
4. Inspect the sinus wall for signs of thrombophlebitis.

**Historical approach:** In the pre-antibiotic era, the standard practice was to remove all bone covering the lateral sinus, incise the sinus wall, and remove the infected clot from within the sinus lumen. Internal jugular vein ligation in the neck was sometimes performed to prevent further distal embolization.

**Modern approach:** More recent case series recommend mastoidectomy and prolonged intravenous antibiotics without routinely opening the sinus. The rationale is that modern imaging (MRI/MRV) can reliably monitor thrombus resolution over time, and prolonged intravenous antibiotics are effective at sterilizing the thrombus. Opening the sinus carries risks of significant haemorrhage and incomplete clot removal. The sinus is only opened if there is a clear perisinus abscess requiring drainage or if the patient fails to respond to conservative management.

#### 3. Anticoagulation

Anticoagulation remains a controversial aspect of management. Some centres use **heparin** initially, followed by **warfarin** or direct oral anticoagulants, particularly in the following situations:

- The thrombus is actively propagating despite antibiotics and surgery
- There is evidence of otitic hydrocephalus from sinus occlusion
- The thrombus is extending into the internal jugular vein

The goal of anticoagulation is to prevent further thrombus propagation and promote recanalization of the occluded sinus. However, anticoagulation must be weighed against the risk of haemorrhagic complications, especially if a concurrent brain abscess is present (a relative contraindication). The decision is therefore made on a case-by-case basis in conjunction with neurosurgical and haematological advice.

#### 4. Management of Complications

- **Septic pulmonary emboli** are managed with continued intravenous antibiotics and supportive care; most resolve with treatment of the underlying sinus thrombosis.
- **Otitic hydrocephalus** requires reduction of raised intracranial pressure (steroids, diuretics, mannitol; VP shunt in refractory cases — see Part G).
- **Associated brain abscess or meningitis** must be managed concurrently with appropriate neurosurgical intervention and prolonged antibiotics (see Part G).

## Part G: Other Intracranial Complications — Brief Management (Q11)

### Meningitis

Otogenic meningitis is the most common intracranial complication of otitis media and presents as an acute illness with severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, and progressive drowsiness. On examination, positive Kernig's sign (resistance to knee extension with hip flexed) and Brudzinski's sign (involuntary hip flexion when the neck is passively flexed) are typically present.

Management centres on high-dose intravenous antibiotic therapy with close clinical observation. The timing of ear surgery is an important clinical decision: surgery is generally deferred for a few days until the patient shows improvement on antibiotics. However, if there is no clinical improvement after 48 hours, or if clinical deterioration occurs despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, immediate ear exploration (mastoidectomy) is indicated to eradicate the source of ongoing sepsis.

### Intracranial Abscess (Brain Abscess)

**Location and presentation:** Otogenic brain abscesses arise most commonly in the temporal lobe and cerebellum due to their proximity to the temporal bone. Subdural empyema has a more acute presentation than intracerebral abscess and may present with fits or seizures, rapid neurological deterioration, and focal neurological signs; it carries a high mortality rate. Intracerebral abscess is notoriously insidious, with headache, vomiting, low-grade fever, and progressive drowsiness developing gradually. Temporal lobe abscesses may produce subtle personality changes, nominal dysphasia, or visual field defects. Cerebellar abscesses present with ataxia, intention tremor, nystagmus, and dysdiadochokinesis.

**Diagnosis:** CT scanning is essential for diagnosis, and MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides superior sensitivity for detecting early abscess formation and distinguishing abscess from tumour.

**Management:** The traditional approach involves neurosurgical aspiration through a burrhole or formal craniotomy for drainage of the abscess cavity, combined with prolonged intravenous antibiotics. A more recent approach is transmastoid drainage combined with mastoidectomy (CWU or CWD), which may be performed when the abscess is contiguous with the mastoid disease through an eroded tegmen or posterior fossa plate; this approach avoids a separate neurosurgical procedure. Despite modern treatment, mortality from otogenic brain abscess remains 6-14% in recent series.

### Otitic Hydrocephalus (Benign Intracranial Hypertension)

Otitic hydrocephalus is the rarest intracranial complication of otitis media. Its pathophysiology is linked to lateral sinus thrombosis: occlusion of the lateral sinus obstructs intracranial venous drainage, which impairs CSF absorption at the arachnoid granulations, resulting in raised intracranial pressure.

The key clinical finding is papilloedema, which is highly suggestive of this diagnosis. Early CT is essential to rule out more serious space-occupying complications (brain abscess, subdural empyema) before attributing papilloedema to otitic hydrocephalus alone.

Management involves reduction of raised intracranial pressure through a combination of:

- **Steroids** (dexamethasone) to reduce cerebral oedema
- **Diuretics** (acetazolamide, furosemide) to reduce CSF production
- **Hyperosmolar dehydrating agents** (mannitol) for acute ICP reduction

Treatment may need to continue for many weeks. In refractory cases where raised intracranial pressure persists despite medical therapy, a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt may be required. Concurrently, the underlying lateral sinus thrombosis must be treated with mastoidectomy and prolonged intravenous antibiotics.
