# Eustachian Tube Dysfunction and Balloon Dilatation Eustachian Tuboplasty

## DNB ENT Exam Comprehensive Answer

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 86 (Eustachian Tube) and Chapter 83 (Chronic Otitis Media)

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - - **Q105 (2024):** Discuss the aetiopathogenesis of Eustachian tube dysfunction. Describe the various methods to assess the Eustachian tube function. Discuss the indications and complications of Balloon dilatation Eustachian tuboplasty.
> - ## Part A: Anatomy of the Eustachian Tube (Pharyngotympanic Tube / Auditory Tube)
> - lies within the petrous temporal bone and constitutes the posterolateral one-third of the tube. Its rigid walls ensure it remains always patent, and it opens into the anterior wall of the middle ear at the protympanum. An important anatomical relationship is the carotid canal, with the internal carotid artery lying inferomedially to the bony ET — a relationship of critical surgical significance.
> - forms the anteromedial two-thirds and is composed of a medial cartilaginous lamina with a superior hook-shaped configuration, and a lateral membranous wall formed by soft tissue. The lateral wall is deficient in cartilage and is the portion that collapses and opens during function. At rest, the cartilaginous ET is normally collapsed; it opens actively during swallowing, yawning, and the Valsalva manoeuvre. The Ostmann fat pad lies lateral to the cartilaginous ET and contributes to passive closure of the tube by exerting gentle medial pressure on the lateral wall.
> - lies at the junction between the bony and cartilaginous portions. As the narrowest segment of the entire tube (approximately 1-2 mm in diameter), it represents a critical point where even minor mucosal oedema or pathology can significantly impair ventilatory function.
> - is the cartilaginous elevation formed by the medial end of the ET cartilage, lying posterior and superior to the ET orifice. Posterior to the torus tubarius lies the **fossa of Rosenmuller (pharyngeal recess)**, an important site for [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]]. The **salpingopharyngeal fold** is a mucosal fold extending inferiorly from the torus tubarius containing the salpingopharyngeus muscle, while the **salpingopalatine fold** extends anteroinferiorly from the torus tubarius.
> - ## Part B: Aetiopathogenesis of Eustachian Tube Dysfunction (Q105)
> - | Failure of the ET to open adequately; produces negative ME pressure, effusion, retraction | Most common type; ear fullness, muffled hearing, otalgia, conductive hearing loss, retracted TM |
> - | Failure of the ET to close; ET remains persistently open | Autophony (hearing own voice/breathing), fullness; TM moves with respiration; improves with head-down position or nasal congestion |
> - | ET opens normally at ambient pressure but fails to equalize under rapid pressure changes | Ear pain/barotrauma during flying, diving, or rapid altitude changes; normal ET function at sea level |
> - are particularly important in children. The immature ET in paediatric patients is shorter, more horizontal, and has more compliant cartilage with reduced efficiency of TVP contraction, all of which predispose to dysfunction. In both adults and children, poor tensor veli palatini contraction from neuromuscular disorders or hypotonia, and high compliance of the cartilaginous ET (floppy cartilage with reduced elastic recoil), represent functional causes of obstruction.
> - involve pathology within the ET lumen itself. Mucosal oedema from upper respiratory tract infections, [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|allergic rhinitis]], or rhinosinusitis narrows or occludes the lumen. Bacterial biofilm formation within the ET leads to chronic inflammation. Polyps at the ET orifice can obstruct the nasopharyngeal opening, while mucosal scarring and fibrosis from prior inflammation or surgery cause cicatrization. Viscid secretions may also cause mucous plugging of the lumen.
> - produce compression or obstruction from outside the ET. Adenoid hypertrophy in children physically obstructs the nasopharyngeal ET orifice and serves as a reservoir of infection. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma or other masses can directly compress or invade the ET orifice — this must be excluded in any adult with unilateral [[OME Pathophysiology|otitis media with effusion]]. Post-[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] fibrosis from treatment of nasopharyngeal or head and neck malignancy is another important extrinsic cause, as is peritubal lymphoid hypertrophy (Gerlach tonsil hypertrophy) around the ET orifice.
> - represent a significant category. Cleft palate disrupts the normal decussation and insertion of the TVP and produces abnormal cartilage morphology, resulting in a very high incidence of OME. Down syndrome (Trisomy 21) causes midface hypoplasia, a narrow nasopharynx, and muscular hypotonia. Other craniofacial syndromes associated with ETD include Treacher Collins, Pierre Robin, Apert, and Crouzon syndromes.
> - include allergic rhinitis with IgE-mediated mucosal oedema extending to the ET mucosa, gastroesophageal reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux where pepsin and acid cause peritubal mucosal inflammation, and granulomatous diseases such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and sarcoidosis causing granulomatous inflammation at the ET orifice.
> - include post-adenoidectomy scarring at the nasopharyngeal orifice, post-radiotherapy fibrosis and mucosal atrophy, and post-nasopharyngeal surgery trauma to the ET orifice region.
> - is the most recognized cause, leading to loss of the Ostmann fat pad and peritubal tissue volume. **Atrophy of peritubal tissue** occurs with aging and connective tissue disorders. **Pregnancy** affects mucosal turgor through hormonal changes. **Dehydration** reduces mucosal and submucosal tissue volume. **Post-radiotherapy** changes cause tissue atrophy and fibrosis with loss of ET compliance. **Neuromuscular disease** alters muscle tone affecting passive closure. Certain **medications** including diuretics and oral contraceptives have historical associations with patulous ET.
> - - **Politzer's hydrops ex vacuo theory:** Negative ME pressure leads to transudation of fluid
> - Middle ear gas exchange:** Normally maintained by ET opening (~1-2 times/minute during swallowing); ME gas composition depends on mucosal gas exchange and ET ventilation
> - Mucosal gas exchange:** Even with intact ET function, the ME mucosa constantly absorbs gas; ET opening replenishes this
> - Critical closing pressure:** The pressure at which the cartilaginous ET collapses; increased in obstructive ETD
> - ## Part C: Assessment of Eustachian Tube Function (Q105)
> - involves the patient forcefully exhaling against a pinched nose and closed mouth; a positive result is TM bulging outward seen on otoscopy or tympanometry, with the patient feeling an ear "pop." This demonstrates ET patency under positive nasopharyngeal pressure but is non-physiological, testing only forced opening, and can be negative even with a partially functional ET.
> - requires the patient to swallow with the nose pinched. The TM initially retracts due to negative pressure generated by swallowing and then returns to neutral, demonstrating physiological ET opening during swallowing. An intact TM is required for observation, and assessment is somewhat subjective.
> - involves insufflating air into one nostril using a Politzer bag while the patient swallows or says "kick." Air entering the middle ear is heard via an auscultation tube (Toynbee tube), or TM movement is observed, confirming ET opening during swallowing. This test is operator-dependent and requires patient cooperation.
> - uses tongue thrust against a closed palate with nose pinched to produce outward TM movement. This is an alternative forced opening test commonly used by divers but is highly technique-dependent.
> - provides indirect evidence of ETD through direct visualization of the TM, revealing retraction, air-fluid levels, bubbles behind the TM, effusion, or retraction pockets. **Pneumatic otoscopy (Siegle speculum)** applies positive and negative pressure to the TM; reduced TM mobility suggests effusion or negative ME pressure. Both are static assessments and do not directly test ET function.
> - | Normal (-50 to +50 daPa) | Normal | Normal ET function |
> - | Negative (< -100 daPa) | Normal or reduced | Negative ME pressure suggesting obstructive ETD |
> - | Cannot be determined | Absent peak | ME effusion (severe ETD) or TM perforation |
> - | Normal | Reduced | Ossicular fixation (not primarily ETD) |
> - is performed by applying +200 daPa through the EAC via tympanometer, then having the patient swallow 3-5 times, followed by repeat tympanometry to measure pressure change. Pressure should equalize (return toward 0) with each swallow if the ET is functional, producing a step-wise reduction in ME pressure. Quantitatively, a good ET equalizes more than 50% of the pressure within 5 swallows, while a poor ET equalizes less than 25%.
> - involves inflating the middle ear via Valsalva, performing tympanometry to confirm positive ME pressure, having the patient swallow, then repeating tympanometry. A shift of the tympanometric peak toward 0 daPa indicates ET opening and pressure equalization. The more comprehensive **Nine-Step Inflation-Deflation Test (Bluestone)** involves a sequence of: (1) baseline tympanogram, (2) Valsalva, (3) tympanogram, (4) swallow, (5) tympanogram, (6) Toynbee, (7) tympanogram, (8) swallow, (9) tympanogram. Serial peak pressure shifts indicate ET opening ability with both inflation (Valsalva) and deflation (Toynbee) phases assessed.
> - ## Part D: Balloon Dilatation Eustachian Tuboplasty (BDET) — Indications (Q105)
> - is an important indication, but only after mandatory exclusion of nasopharyngeal pathology, in patients with persistent or recurrent effusion despite medical management. BDET may also be performed as an **adjunctive procedure alongside [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]] or mastoid surgery** to improve middle ear ventilation and optimize surgical outcomes in patients with ETD-related chronic ear disease.
> - — those with recurrent OME after grommet extrusion who would otherwise require repeated grommet insertions — benefit from BDET as it addresses the underlying ET pathology. **Baro-challenge-induced ETD** in divers, frequent flyers, or aircrew with recurrent barotrauma refractory to medical treatment and auto-inflation techniques is another recognized indication. Finally, **early retraction pockets with ETD** may be considered, as improving ET function may prevent progression to cholesteatoma.
> - ## Part E: BDET — Surgical Technique (Q105)
> - General anaesthesia (preferred) or sedation with topical anaesthesia (4% lignocaine with oxymetazoline pledgets)
> - Patient supine with head slightly elevated; nasal cavity and nasopharynx decongested with topical vasoconstrictors
> - Transnasal endoscopic approach using a 0-degree or 30-degree rigid nasal endoscope
> - Visualize the nasopharyngeal orifice of the ET; identify the torus tubarius posteriorly and the ET opening anteroinferior to it
> - Insert the balloon catheter system (e.g., Bielefeld balloon / AERA balloon catheter / Acclarent system) through the nostril under endoscopic guidance
> - Advance the catheter tip into the ET orifice and into the cartilaginous portion of the ET
> - Advance until the balloon segment lies within the cartilaginous portion of the ET; the balloon does NOT enter the bony portion (critical for safety, as the ICA lies near the bony portion)
> - Inflate balloon with saline to **10-12 atmospheres** pressure
> - Hold inflation for **2 minutes** (120 seconds) — this is the standard dilation time
> - Deflate the balloon completely; withdraw the catheter under endoscopic vision
> - Endoscopically inspect the ET orifice and surrounding mucosa for mucosal integrity, tears, or bleeding
> - May be combined with adenoidectomy, inferior turbinate reduction, septoplasty, FESS, or tympanoplasty as indicated
> - of the ET — the membranous lateral wall is stretched and remodelled. The **bony portion is NOT dilated**, which is critical for safety as the internal carotid artery is intimately related to the bony ET. The mechanism of action involves microtrauma to the cartilaginous framework and submucosal tissue, leading to remodelling, fibrosis, and improved luminal patency. Balloon dimensions are typically 16-20 mm in length and 3-5 mm in diameter when inflated. The procedure is typically performed as day-case surgery.
> - ## Part F: BDET — Complications (Q105)
> - is rare and occurs when air tracks through tissue planes from mucosal disruption; it is typically cervicofacial, self-resolving, and patients should avoid Valsalva post-operatively.
> - from over-dilation is rare but can cause persistent ET patency with autophony and aural fullness; this may resolve spontaneously or require further intervention. **Tympanic membrane perforation** from pressure transmission to the TM during balloon inflation is rare and usually heals spontaneously, though it may require myringoplasty. **Temporary taste disturbance** may occur occasionally from chorda tympani irritation related to pressure transmission through the middle ear; it is typically transient.
> - is an extremely rare, largely theoretical risk that is minimized because the balloon is confined to the cartilaginous portion; pre-operative CT should identify aberrant ICA or carotid dehiscence. **Negative pressure pulmonary oedema** is extremely rare and may occur if forced respiratory efforts against obstruction happen during the procedure under general anaesthesia. **Infection** (otitis media or nasopharyngeal infection) is rare, and prophylactic antibiotics may be used. **Middle ear barotrauma** with haemotympanum from pressure transmission during balloon inflation is also rare.
> - occurs in 15-30% of patients, with incomplete symptom resolution or recurrence of ETD. Such patients may require repeat BDET or alternative management.
> - for treatment of persistent ETD in adults, and NICE has issued Interventional Procedures Guidance (IPG) considering BDET safe with adequate short-term efficacy evidence. Emerging data suggest sustained benefit at 1-2 years, though long-term data beyond 5 years are still accumulating. Repeat BDET may be performed in cases of recurrence, with potential for improved outcomes on second dilation. Preliminary evidence also suggests improved tympanoplasty outcomes when BDET is combined with tympanoplasty in patients with ETD.
