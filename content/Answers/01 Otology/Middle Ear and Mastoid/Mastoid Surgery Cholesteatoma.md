## Part A: Types of Mastoid Surgery (Q104)

### Classification of Mastoid Procedures

Mastoid surgery encompasses a spectrum of procedures that vary in the extent of bone removal, the fate of the posterior canal wall, and the degree of middle ear exteriorization. The choice of procedure depends on the nature and extent of disease, the status of the canal wall, and the goals of reconstruction.

**Cortical (simple) mastoidectomy**, also known as the Schwartze procedure, involves removal of cortical bone and mastoid air cells while preserving the posterior canal wall. It is used primarily for acute mastoiditis unresponsive to antibiotics, drainage of subperiosteal abscess, and as the initial step to gain access to the antrum for more complex procedures such as posterior tympanotomy and [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]].

**Canal Wall Up (CWU) mastoidectomy**, also termed intact canal wall (ICW) mastoidectomy or Combined Approach [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|Tympanoplasty]] (CAT), employs both transmastoid and transcanal approaches while preserving the posterior canal wall. The tympanic membrane is reconstructed and normal ear canal anatomy is maintained. This is the preferred approach for cholesteatoma when complete clearance is achievable, as it avoids the morbidity associated with an open cavity. A second-look operation is traditionally planned at 9 to 12 months.

**Canal Wall Down (CWD) mastoidectomy**, or modified radical mastoidectomy, removes the posterior canal wall to exteriorize the mastoid cavity. The tympanic membrane remnant is preserved where possible. This approach is selected for extensive cholesteatoma, particularly when the canal wall is already eroded, after failed CWU procedures, in an only hearing ear where safer visualization is paramount, or when the patient is unlikely to attend regular follow-up.

**Radical mastoidectomy** goes further by removing the canal wall and exteriorizing the entire middle ear and mastoid without tympanic membrane reconstruction. The Eustachian tube may be obliterated. It is reserved for extensive squamous disease, complications of cholesteatoma, or tumour.

**Atticotomy** is the most conservative surgical option, involving removal of the lateral attic wall (scutum) only to access the epitympanum. It may be combined with reconstruction of the scutum using cartilage.

**Subtotal petrosectomy** is the most extensive procedure and is reserved for very extensive disease. It is often combined with obliteration of the mastoid and blind sac closure of the external auditory canal.

### Surgical Principles — CWD Mastoidectomy

The aim of CWD surgery is to create a “small cavity” as described by Sadé, essentially an atticoantrostomy. Key technical principles include:

1. Remove all air cell tracts and disease completely
2. Lower the facial ridge to the level of the facial nerve — a high residual ridge is the most common cause of persistent discharge
3. Create an adequate meatoplasty to ensure ventilation and self-cleaning of the cavity
4. Saucerize the cavity walls — smooth, rounded contours without ridges or overhangs that would trap debris

### Surgical Principles — CWU Mastoidectomy

CWU surgery preserves the posterior canal wall and accesses disease through both transmastoid (posterior tympanotomy) and transcanal routes. The tympanic membrane is reconstructed with a graft, most commonly temporalis fascia or cartilage. [[Ossiculoplasty CSF Otorrhea Cochleostomy|Ossiculoplasty]] is performed if ossicular erosion is present, either at primary surgery or as a staged procedure. A second-look operation has traditionally been planned at 9 to 12 months to inspect for residual disease, although diffusion-weighted MRI is increasingly replacing this practice.

### Long-Term Hearing Outcomes

Despite the theoretical hearing advantage of CWU surgery in maintaining normal ear canal anatomy, long-term studies demonstrate no significant difference in hearing outcomes between CWD and CWU approaches. The UK National Audit of mastoid surgery reported a [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]] rate of 1% and a dead ear rate of 2%.

## Part B: Paediatric Cholesteatoma Surgery (Q104)

### Principles of Paediatric Cholesteatoma Management

Surgery is the primary treatment for cholesteatoma in children, with goals prioritized in a clear hierarchy. The primary aims are to remove all cholesteatoma matrix, prevent erosion and complications, achieve a dry watertight ear, create a self-cleaning ear, and prevent recurrence. Hearing improvement is a secondary goal pursued only after the primary aims are secured. Parents must understand that cholesteatoma management is not a single operation — multiple procedures are likely, prolonged follow-up is essential, and hearing will never be truly normal.

### Congenital vs Acquired Cholesteatoma in Children

Congenital cholesteatoma accounts for approximately 10 to 28% of paediatric cholesteatoma. It arises from epidermoid cell rests in the anterior epitympanum, first described by Michaels. Diagnostic criteria include an intact tympanic membrane, no history of otorrhoea or perforation, and no prior ear surgery. Children with congenital cholesteatoma present earlier, with a mean age of 5.6 years. The Potsic classification system is used to stage congenital cholesteatoma.

Acquired cholesteatoma may be primary, arising from retraction of the pars flaccida or pars tensa (the retraction theory being most widely accepted), or secondary, developing through ingrowth of squamous epithelium via a perforation or traumatic defect. Children with acquired cholesteatoma present later, with a mean age of 9.7 years. Bilateral cholesteatoma is found in 7 to 10% of children at presentation.

### Potsic Staging for Congenital Cholesteatoma

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Single quadrant, no ossicular or mastoid involvement |
| II | Multiple quadrants, no ossicular or mastoid involvement |
| III | Ossicular erosion present, no mastoid extension |
| IV | Mastoid extension |

### Paediatric vs Adult Cholesteatoma

Cholesteatoma in children behaves more aggressively than in adults for several reasons. The well-pneumatized paediatric temporal bone provides multiple pathways for disease spread, and the greater inflammatory response — driven by metalloproteinases and antibodies — leads to more extensive ossicular erosion. Paradoxically, children have less labyrinthine fistula and less facial nerve exposure than adults, likely because cholesteatoma in children tends to spread through air cell tracts rather than eroding into dense otic capsule bone. Ongoing Eustachian tube dysfunction due to the immature paediatric Eustachian tube contributes to higher recurrence rates. Practically, young children may not tolerate outpatient microsuction or aural toilet, which influences the choice between CWU and CWD approaches.

### CWU vs CWD in Children

| Feature | CWU (Preferred) | CWD |
|---|---|---|
| Ear canal anatomy | Normal canal preserved with reconstructed TM | Canal wall removed; open cavity |
| Water precautions | None needed | Often must keep ear dry (swimming restrictions) |
| Healing | Rapid | Slower; risk of “mastoid misery” |
| Hearing aid use | Compatible with standard hearing aids | Difficult to fit hearing aids |
| Follow-up burden | Second-look operation may be needed | Cavity easily inspected but needs regular microsuction |
| Recurrence mechanism | Risk of recurrent cholesteatoma from reconstructed TM | Reduced mastoid volume means ET has less to ventilate |
| Technical demand | More technically demanding | One major operation |
| Paediatric tolerance | No ongoing microsuction needed | Multiple GAs may be needed for uncooperative child; caloric vertigo from cold water |

CWU surgery is the preferred initial approach in children, used in approximately 89% of cases. The trend over the last decade has been away from CWD in children because of the significant problems associated with managing mastoid cavities in the paediatric population. CWD is performed in approximately 10% of children initially, rising to 14% of the cohort when conversions are included. The main reasons for converting from CWU to CWD are extensive or aggressive disease with canal wall erosion (56% of conversions) and unfavourable anatomy such as poor pneumatization, low tegmen, or anterior sigmoid sinus (43% of conversions).

### Down Syndrome Considerations

Children with Down syndrome have a higher prevalence of cholesteatoma and present particular management challenges. Their characteristically narrow ear canals make post-operative care difficult, and recurrence rates are higher. CWD may be necessary in these patients despite its disadvantages, because the narrow canals preclude adequate post-operative surveillance and microsuction in a CWU cavity.

## Part C: Hidden Areas and Residual Cholesteatoma (Q124)

### Hidden Areas for Residual Cholesteatoma

The term “hidden areas” refers to regions of the middle ear and epitympanum that are poorly visualized by the operating microscope during CWU mastoidectomy. These anatomical recesses are the most common sites for residual cholesteatoma because small fragments of matrix can remain undetected during surgery. Understanding their three-dimensional relationships is essential for complete disease clearance.

The **sinus tympani** is the single most important hidden area. It is a posterior extension of the mesotympanum that lies medial to the pyramidal eminence, stapedius muscle, and the vertical segment of the facial nerve, and lateral to the posterior semicircular canal and vestibule. Its superior limit is the ponticulus (a bony bridge between the promontory and the pyramidal process), and its inferior limit is the subiculum (a ridge running from the styloid eminence to the posterior rim of the round window niche). The sinus tympani is significant because even posterior tympanotomy fails to provide adequate microscopic visualization of its depths — cholesteatoma can extend medial to and posterior to the facial nerve, completely out of the microscope’s line of sight.

The **anterior epitympanum** is a deep recess located anterior to the malleus head, above the tensor tympani muscle. Disease within this space can extend anteriorly towards the Eustachian tube orifice. Immediately related to it is the **supratubal recess**, which lies superior to the bony Eustachian tube orifice and communicates directly with the anterior epitympanum. Together, these two spaces form a continuous anterior compartment where cholesteatoma can hide.

The **facial recess** is the triangular space bounded by the chorda tympani nerve laterally and the facial nerve medially. Although it is opened surgically during posterior tympanotomy, residual disease can persist in its recesses, particularly at the margins of the tympanotomy.

The **protympanum**, located anterior to the mesotympanum around the Eustachian tube orifice, is difficult to access with the microscope because of its anterior position and narrow confines. The **hypotympanum**, the inferior extension of the mesotympanum below the level of the tympanic membrane annulus, can also harbour residual pearls that lie below the surgeon’s line of sight.

Finally, the **epitympanic diaphragm folds** — the complex mucosal folds and ligaments surrounding the ossicular chain in the epitympanum — can trap small cholesteatoma pearls within their recesses. These folds create compartments that are difficult to inspect and clean even under high magnification.

### Residual Cholesteatoma Rates

Residual cholesteatoma rates after CWU surgery in children range from 6 to 43%, while rates after CWD surgery in children range from 6 to 38%. These rates can be significantly reduced with modern adjuncts including intraoperative otoendoscopy and laser assistance. The wide variation in reported rates reflects the extent of initial disease, the degree of temporal bone pneumatization (a well-pneumatized bone in children provides more pathways for disease spread), and the thoroughness of intraoperative inspection.

## Part D: CWD vs CWU — Detailed Comparison (Q124)

### Comparing Approaches for Hidden Area Disease

The choice between CWD and CWU surgery is particularly relevant when cholesteatoma involves the hidden areas of the middle ear. Each approach has distinct advantages and limitations in addressing these challenging recesses.

CWD surgery provides superior intraoperative access to all areas of the middle ear and mastoid. The resulting cavity can be easily inspected post-operatively in the outpatient setting, and the reduced total mastoid volume means the Eustachian tube has less space to ventilate, which may lower recurrence rates. Residual disease is easier to identify and manage when it occurs in an open cavity.

However, CWD carries significant long-term morbidity. Cavity problems include chronic discharge, caloric vertigo from temperature changes, cosmetic concerns, and debris accumulation requiring regular microsuction. The so-called “mastoid misery” described by Males and Gray refers to this constellation of problems, with reported wet cavity rates ranging from 5 to over 30%. Patients typically face swimming restrictions, difficulty wearing hearing aids, and the need for lifelong follow-up with aural toilet.

CWU surgery is increasingly preferred because it preserves normal ear anatomy and quality of life. Patients can swim without precautions, wear hearing aids normally, and avoid the burden of regular microsuction. Modern adjuncts have substantially reduced the historical disadvantage of higher residual disease rates: the otoendoscope allows inspection of hidden areas during surgery, laser technology enables precise removal of disease from deep recesses, diffusion-weighted MRI provides non-invasive surveillance, and cartilage reinforcement of the reconstructed tympanic membrane may reduce recurrence from retraction. Obliteration techniques further reduce cavity problems when CWD is ultimately required.

## Part E: Sinus Tympani Cholesteatoma Management (Q124)

### Anatomy of the Sinus Tympani

The sinus tympani is the most clinically significant hidden area in cholesteatoma surgery. It is a posterior extension of the mesotympanum that occupies a critical position relative to vital structures. The sinus lies medial to the pyramidal eminence, the stapedius muscle, and the vertical segment of the facial nerve. Laterally, it is bounded by the posterior semicircular canal and the vestibule. Its superior boundary is the ponticulus, a bony bridge connecting the promontory to the pyramidal process, while its inferior boundary is the subiculum, a bony ridge extending from the styloid eminence to the posterior rim of the round window niche.

### Marchioni Classification (Endoscopic)

Marchioni developed an endoscopic classification system that describes the morphological variants of the sinus tympani and their surgical implications.

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Type A** | Limited (shallow) sinus tympani |
| **Type B** | Deep sinus tympani with medial extension beyond the facial nerve |
| **Type C** | Deep sinus tympani with posterior extension beyond the facial nerve |

Types B and C are particularly problematic because disease can extend into spaces that are completely inaccessible to the operating microscope, even when viewed through a well-performed posterior tympanotomy. The Bowdler and Walsh study demonstrated that the microscope fundamentally cannot visualize the depths of the sinus tympani through the posterior tympanotomy approach.

### Management Strategies for Sinus Tympani Cholesteatoma

**Endoscopic approach (transcanal):** The otoendoscope has transformed the management of sinus tympani cholesteatoma. A 0-degree endoscope provides a good initial view of the sinus tympani, while 30-degree, 45-degree, and 70-degree angled endoscopes provide excellent visualization deep into the recess. From the endoscopic perspective, the facial recess ceases to be a true “recess” and becomes a small depression that can be reached within minutes through a transcanal route. This allows direct removal of disease from hidden areas without the need for cortical mastoidectomy. Special curved instruments have been designed specifically for endoscopic access to these deep recesses.

**Microscopic approaches:** Through a standard posterior tympanotomy, the microscope provides limited keyhole access to the sinus tympani but fundamentally fails to visualize its deeper portions. A retrofacial approach, accessing the sinus from the mastoid posterior to the facial nerve, can reach some areas but adds surgical complexity. Angled mirrors or probes offer indirect visualization but with significant limitations.

**Combined approach:** An increasingly common practice is to perform a microscopic mastoidectomy for the bulk of disease clearance, then use the otoendoscope as an adjunct to inspect hidden areas and confirm complete clearance. This combines the bimanual dissection advantages of the microscope with the panoramic visualization of the endoscope.

**Laser assistance:** The KTP laser can precisely vaporize or remove disease from the sinus tympani with minimal trauma to surrounding structures. When combined with endoscopic visualization, laser assistance further reduces residual cholesteatoma rates in this challenging location.

## Part F: Residivism After Mastoid Surgery (Q94)

### Definitions

Understanding the terminology of disease recurrence after mastoid surgery is essential. **Residual cholesteatoma** refers to cholesteatoma matrix inadvertently left behind at the time of initial surgery, which subsequently grows. It is fundamentally a problem of surgical technique — incomplete clearance, particularly from hidden areas. **Recurrent cholesteatoma** refers to new cholesteatoma forming at a different site after surgery, typically from retraction of the reconstructed tympanic membrane. It is related to the underlying pathological process, particularly persistent Eustachian tube dysfunction. **Recidivism** (or recidivistic cholesteatoma) is the collective term encompassing both residual and recurrent disease.

### Rates of Recidivism

| Measure | CWU | CWD |
|---|---|---|
| Residual cholesteatoma | 10–36% (children 6–43%) | 5–10% (children 6–38%) |
| Recurrent cholesteatoma | 17–25% at 5 years (children) | 7–25% at 5 years (children) |
| Overall recidivism | Higher | Lower |

Recurrence can occur as late as 13 to 25 years post-operatively, underscoring the need for prolonged surveillance. Rates are consistently higher in children than adults, reflecting ongoing Eustachian tube dysfunction and the multiple pathways for disease spread through the well-pneumatized paediatric temporal bone.

### Risk Factors for Recidivism

Risk factors for **residual** cholesteatoma include extensive initial disease, a well-pneumatized temporal bone providing multiple pathways for spread, a deep sinus tympani (Marchioni Type B or C), and inadequate surgical clearance of hidden areas. Risk factors for **recurrent** cholesteatoma include persistent Eustachian tube dysfunction (especially in children), poor reconstruction technique, and a thin or atrophic reconstructed tympanic membrane that is prone to retraction. Patient-level risk factors that increase both residual and recurrent disease include young age, cleft palate, and Down syndrome.

### Strategies for Reducing Recidivism

1. **Otoendoscope during primary surgery:** Inspecting hidden areas at the time of initial clearance significantly reduces residual rates by identifying matrix in the sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, and other recesses
2. **KTP laser:** Enables precise removal of disease from hidden areas with minimal collateral damage
3. **Cartilage reinforcement of reconstructed tympanic membrane:** Cartilage is more rigid than fascia and resists retraction, thereby reducing the risk of recurrent cholesteatoma from retraction pockets
4. **Mastoid obliteration in CWU surgery:** Reduces dead space within the mastoid, lowering recurrence rates to approximately 3% in paediatric series
5. **Diffusion-weighted MRI surveillance:** Enables non-invasive detection of residual or recurrent disease, reducing the need for routine second-look surgery
6. **Long-term follow-up:** Essential in all patients, particularly children, given the potential for very late recurrence

## Part G: Second-Look Operations and Innovations (Q8)

### Thomassin Minimally Invasive Technique

Thomassin pioneered the use of endoscopic middle ear surgery for cholesteatoma and fundamentally changed the approach to second-look procedures. His technique employed otoendoscopes — both 0-degree and angled — introduced through a posterior tympanotomy or transcanal route to systematically search for and remove residual disease in the most problematic hidden areas: the anterior epitympanic recess, the tubal orifice, and the sinus tympani. Special microinstruments were designed specifically for use alongside the endoscope in these confined spaces.

Thomassin coupled the endoscope to a camera system, creating the otovideoendoscopic technique that allowed the entire surgical team to view the operative field on a monitor. The result was a distinct reduction in residual cholesteatoma rates. He advocated the endoscopic second-look as a minimally invasive alternative to formal surgical re-exploration — this could be performed under local anaesthetic through an existing posterior tympanotomy, dramatically reducing patient morbidity compared with a full re-exploration under general anaesthesia.

### Diffusion-Weighted MRI (DW-MRI)

Non-echo-planar diffusion-weighted MRI sequences can detect cholesteatoma by identifying the restricted diffusion of keratin within cholesteatoma matrix. Meta-analyses report a pooled sensitivity of approximately 90% or greater for non-echo-planar DW-MRI, with high specificity — the technique reliably differentiates cholesteatoma from post-operative fluid, granulation tissue, and inflammation.

The primary application of DW-MRI is post-operative surveillance for residual or recurrent cholesteatoma after CWU mastoidectomy and blind sac closure procedures. Its impact has been transformative: by enabling radiological surveillance, DW-MRI has substantially reduced the need for routine second-look surgery. This in turn allows primary ossiculoplasty at the first operation, since the surgeon no longer needs to plan a staged procedure for hearing reconstruction. DW-MRI is used similarly for both paediatric and adult cholesteatoma surveillance. The main limitation is that very small cholesteatoma pearls less than 3 mm in diameter may be missed.

### Combined Modern Approach

Modern cholesteatoma management increasingly combines multiple innovations into an integrated strategy:

1. **Endoscopic-assisted primary clearance** — otoendoscope used to inspect and clear hidden areas during the initial operation
2. **Cartilage tympanoplasty** — rigid cartilage graft resists retraction, reducing recurrent cholesteatoma
3. **DW-MRI surveillance** — replaces routine second-look surgery with non-invasive imaging
4. **Targeted second surgery** — performed only when DW-MRI is positive or clinical suspicion warrants re-exploration

## Part H: Mastoid Cavity Problems and Obliteration (Q48, Q34, Q113)

### Post-operative Discharging Mastoid Cavity — Aetiology (Q48)

A persistently discharging mastoid cavity after CWD surgery is one of the most common long-term complications and represents a significant source of patient morbidity. The causes are multiple and often coexist.

**Inadequate meatoplasty** is one of the most frequent and correctable causes. A small meatus restricts ventilation and self-cleaning of the cavity, allowing debris and secretions to accumulate and become secondarily infected. Similarly, a **high facial ridge** — residual bone between the facial nerve and the floor of the external auditory canal — creates a dependent sump where moisture pools rather than draining. This is considered the single most important surgical factor in cavity failure.

**Residual mastoid tip cells** lined with unhealthy mucosa continue to produce secretions that drain into the cavity. An excessively **large cavity** presents a greater surface area that may fail to epithelialize completely, leading to persistent granulation tissue and discharge. The **dependent sump** problem is compounded when the cavity anatomy is unfavourable for gravity-assisted drainage.

**Residual or recurrent cholesteatoma** produces ongoing discharge from active disease, and must always be excluded in a persistently wet cavity. **Eustachian tube dysfunction** creates negative middle ear pressure that draws moisture into the cavity. **Exposed bone and granulation tissue** from failed epithelialization produce chronic discharge. **Secondary infection** — bacterial or fungal — can colonize an unhealthy cavity, and **foreign body reaction** to graft material or residual bone dust perpetuates inflammation.

### Management of Discharging Mastoid Cavity (Q48)

**Conservative and medical management** is always attempted first and is successful in the majority of cases:

1. Regular aural toilet and microsuction — the most important single intervention for cavity care
2. Topical antibiotic and steroid drops — quinolone-based preparations are preferred to aminoglycosides to avoid ototoxicity
3. Aural toilet under the operating microscope in the outpatient setting
4. Drying agents such as boric acid powder or acetic acid drops
5. Treatment of any underlying fungal infection with appropriate topical antifungals
6. Patient education regarding keeping the cavity dry and avoiding water entry

**Surgical management** is pursued when conservative measures fail:

1. **Revision meatoplasty** — enlarging the meatus to improve access, ventilation, and self-cleaning
2. **Lowering the facial ridge** — removing residual bone to eliminate the dependent sump
3. **Removal of residual mastoid tip cells** — eliminating sources of mucoid secretion
4. **Canalplasty** — widening the bony canal to improve access and aeration
5. **Mastoid obliteration** — reducing cavity volume to promote epithelialization (see below)
6. **Subtotal petrosectomy with blind sac closure** — reserved for intractable cases; the external auditory canal is closed as a blind sac, the middle ear and mastoid are obliterated with abdominal fat, and the Eustachian tube is obliterated

### Mastoid Obliteration Techniques (Q113 obliteration part)

The rationale for mastoid obliteration is straightforward: reducing cavity volume promotes epithelialization and reduces the surface area that must heal, resulting in fewer cavity problems. Obliteration achieves dry ears in 80 to 95% of cases and has become a cornerstone of modern mastoid surgery.

**Bone pate** is autologous bone dust collected during drilling. It is simple to harvest, requires no additional donor site, and produces excellent results. It is packed into the mastoid cavity where it provides a scaffold for new bone formation and reduces dead space. Results consistently show 80 to 95% dry ear rates after obliteration with bone pate.

**Bone chips and cortical bone** harvested from the mastoid cortex provide more structural support than bone pate and can be used to reconstruct contours within the cavity.

**Temporalis muscle flap** is a pedicled muscle flap rotated into the mastoid cavity. Its vascularized nature ensures reliable healing and it conforms well to irregular cavity shapes. It remains one of the most widely used and reliable obliteration materials.

**Abdominal fat** is a free fat graft harvested from the periumbilical region. It is the material of choice for obliterating the mastoid and middle ear in subtotal petrosectomy with blind sac closure procedures, where a large volume of obliteration material is needed.

**Cartilage**, harvested from the concha or tragus, provides structural support with very low resorption rates. It is particularly useful for reconstructing the posterior canal wall.

**Hydroxyapatite cement and granules** are biocompatible ceramics that fill dead space and promote new bone formation. They offer the advantage of integration with native bone over time.

**Bioactive glass (S53P4)** is a newer material with inherent antibacterial properties in addition to promoting bone formation. It represents an advance in synthetic obliteration materials.

**Fascioperiosteal flap** is a vascularized local flap that can be laid over bone pate or other obliteration materials to provide a reliable surface for epithelialization.

### Posterior Canal Wall Reconstruction

In revision surgery for a problematic CWD cavity, the posterior canal wall can be reconstructed using cartilage or bone combined with mastoid obliteration. This effectively converts an open cavity back to a closed cavity. Conchal or tragal cartilage is layered to reconstruct the canal wall contour, restoring near-normal ear canal anatomy. Results of obliteration tympanoplasty show 80 to 95% dry ear rates.

### Bony Obliteration Tympanoplasty

This technique applies obliteration principles to CWU surgery. The mastoid cavity is obliterated with bone pate at the time of primary surgery, reducing dead space and lowering recurrence rates. In paediatric series, this approach has achieved recurrence rates as low as 3%, making it an increasingly popular strategy in paediatric cholesteatoma surgery.

## Part I: Posterior Tympanotomy — Anatomy and Indications (Q34, Q28)

### Anatomy of the Facial Recess

The posterior tympanotomy is performed through the facial recess, a triangular space in the posterior wall of the middle ear defined by three boundaries. The medial boundary is the vertical (mastoid) segment of the facial nerve. The lateral boundary is the chorda tympani nerve, which diverges from the facial nerve at a variable angle. The superior boundary is the fossa incudis, where the short process of the incus sits. The size of the posterior tympanotomy opening depends on the angle between the chorda tympani and the facial nerve — a wider angle permits a larger tympanotomy. The lateral semicircular canal serves as the major landmark of the posterior mastoid wall and is the key structure guiding the surgeon to the correct depth.

### Surgical Technique

1. Perform a cortical mastoidectomy as the initial step
2. Identify the three key landmarks: the lateral semicircular canal, the short process of the incus in the fossa incudis, and the vertical segment of the facial nerve
3. Use the triangular relationship of these three structures to locate the facial recess
4. Open the facial recess using a diamond burr, drilling between the chorda tympani nerve laterally and the facial nerve medially
5. Enlarge the tympanotomy to a size appropriate for the intended procedure (for example, a 3 mm diamond burr width for Vibrant Soundbridge FMT placement)

### Structures Visible Through Posterior Tympanotomy

Through a well-performed posterior tympanotomy, the surgeon gains direct visualization of the round window niche and membrane, the promontory, the stapes and incudostapedial joint, the oval window region, and a partial view of the hypotympanum. This access is essential for cochlear implant electrode insertion, cholesteatoma clearance, and ossicular assessment.

### Indications for Posterior Tympanotomy

The posterior tympanotomy is most commonly performed for **cochlear implantation**, where it provides access to the round window or promontory for cochleostomy and electrode insertion. In **cholesteatoma surgery (CWU)**, it allows access to the posterior mesotympanum, inspection for residual disease, and limited visualization of the sinus tympani. For **Vibrant Soundbridge** implantation, the FMT is placed on the incus long process or other coupling sites through this approach. It also serves as a route for **ossicular assessment** from behind, **second-look surgery** to inspect the middle ear for residual cholesteatoma without raising a tympanomeatal flap, and **facial nerve decompression** of the tympanic and mastoid segments.

### Complications of Posterior Tympanotomy

**Facial nerve injury** is the most feared complication. The facial nerve runs immediately medial to the tympanotomy, and injury risk is increased when the nerve is dehiscent or when anatomy is aberrant. Continuous facial nerve monitoring is standard practice to mitigate this risk.

**Chorda tympani injury** is a common complication, as this nerve forms the lateral boundary of the tympanotomy and is often stretched or divided during the procedure. Patients may experience taste disturbance (typically a metallic taste) and dry mouth, though symptoms are usually temporary.

**Sensorineural hearing loss** can occur from inadvertent drill contact with the ossicular chain, which transmits vibratory energy to the inner ear, or from direct trauma to inner ear structures.

**Incomplete access** is an inherent limitation when the facial recess is narrow, restricting visualization and instrument passage. The otoendoscope may supplement microscopic visualization in these cases.

**Dural injury** can occur if the tegmen is very low, and **sigmoid sinus injury** can occur if the sinus is very anteriorly positioned. Both are related to anatomical variation and can usually be anticipated on pre-operative imaging.
