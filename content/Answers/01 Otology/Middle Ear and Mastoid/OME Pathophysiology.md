# Otitis Media with Effusion (OME): Definition, Etiology, Pathophysiology, and Sequelae

## DNB ENT Comprehensive Exam Answer

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery, 8th Edition, Volume 2 — Chapter 13 (Otitis Media with Effusion), Chapter 83 (Chronic Otitis Media)

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - - **Q69 (DNB 2023):** Define Otitis media with effusion and etiology. Describe the pathophysiology of OME and its sequelae.
> - ## Part A: Definition of Otitis Media with Effusion (OME)
> - — that is, without acute otalgia, fever, or a bulging tympanic membrane. The condition is known by several synonyms: **glue ear** (the most common lay term, referring especially to thick mucoid effusion), **secretory otitis media**, **serous otitis media**, and **non-suppurative otitis media**. When the effusion persists for longer than three months, it is classified as **chronic OME**.
> - experience at least one episode by the age of three years, and at any given time roughly 20% of two-year-olds have an effusion. The age distribution is bimodal, with peak incidence at approximately **2 years** and again at **5 years** (school entry). The condition is more common in males than females, and **90% of episodes resolve spontaneously** within three months. Grommet insertion for persistent OME remains the single most common reason for elective surgery in childhood.

> - ## Part B: Etiology and Risk Factors
> - arising from the interplay of Eustachian tube dysfunction, infection, inflammation, host factors, and environmental exposures. Understanding the individual contributions of each factor is essential, as current consensus favours a unified theory in which all mechanisms operate in varying proportions.
> - — enlarged adenoids physically block the nasopharyngeal opening of the Eustachian tube. Second, **reservoir of infection** — adenoid tissue harbours pathogenic bacteria, especially biofilm-forming organisms, that can ascend to the middle ear via the ET. Third, **immunological dysfunction** — chronic adenoiditis produces inflammatory mediators that cause ET mucosal oedema and dysfunction. These three mechanisms are frequently tested in examinations.

Genetic factors are highly significant — twin studies demonstrate heritability exceeding 60%, and polymorphisms in immune genes (TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-10), mucin gene variants (MUC5B), and HLA associations have all been identified.

> - ## Part C: Pathophysiology of OME
> - — most commonly a viral upper respiratory tract infection, an allergic episode, or mechanical ET obstruction by adenoid hypertrophy. This trigger causes mucosal inflammation and oedema within the Eustachian tube, impairing its three core functions: **ventilation**, **drainage**, and **protection** of the middle ear. In children, the anatomically immature ET (shorter, more horizontal, with floppy cartilage) and the less efficient tensor veli palatini muscle compound this dysfunction. The result is functional or mechanical obstruction of the ET.
> - within the middle ear, reaching values of -200 to -400 daPa. The negative pressure has two immediate consequences: it causes **transudation of fluid** from mucosal capillaries into the middle ear space, and it draws the **tympanic membrane medially** (retraction) due to the pressure differential between the atmosphere and the middle ear cavity.
> - — essentially respiratory-type mucosa. This transformation is most marked in the anterior mesotympanum and around the ET orifice, and is driven by inflammatory cytokines and growth factors. The metaplastic epithelium contains a greatly **increased number of goblet cells** (mucus-secreting cells), and submucosal gland hypertrophy and hyperplasia follow. The result is a massive increase in mucus production within the middle ear. The mucin composition changes, with **MUC5B** becoming the predominant mucin — this is the molecule responsible for the thick, tenacious "glue" consistency of chronic effusion. MUC5AC is also increased. Because the ET remains obstructed, this mucus cannot drain and accumulates within the middle ear.
> - — are cultured from **30-50%** of OME effusions that were previously considered sterile. PCR-based studies detect bacterial DNA in up to **80%** of effusions, indicating that infection plays a far larger role than culture-based methods alone would suggest. These bacteria stimulate an **intense inflammatory cascade**, with release of prostaglandins (PGE2, causing vasodilation and increased vascular permeability), leukotrienes (LTB4 and LTC4, causing chemotaxis and mucus hypersecretion), cytokines (IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha), histamine (from mast cell degranulation, especially in allergic OME), and reactive oxygen species (which cause tissue damage and perpetuate inflammation). These mediators increase vascular permeability, recruit neutrophils and macrophages, and stimulate yet further mucus production.
> - of OME middle ear mucosa compared to only **10%** of controls. These biofilms are approximately **1000 times more resistant** to antibiotics than planktonic (free-floating) bacteria and are also resistant to host immune defences. Critically, biofilms are capable of intermittent shedding of planktonic bacteria, which explains acute exacerbations of OME. Biofilm formation is the key mechanism explaining both the **chronicity** of OME and the well-documented **failure of antibiotic therapy**.
> - — the effusion of OME. Over time, this creates a **self-perpetuating vicious cycle**: negative pressure promotes transudation and mucosal oedema, which worsens ET dysfunction; infection drives inflammation, which causes further mucosal oedema and ET obstruction, impairing drainage and allowing bacterial persistence; and biofilms confer antibiotic resistance, maintaining chronic infection and chronic inflammation. This cycle maintains the disease state and is the mechanism by which acute OME transitions to **chronic OME**.
> - | Thin, watery, straw-coloured; low viscosity; predominantly transudate | Early/mild OME; acute phase |
> - | Thick, tenacious, viscous; high mucin content (MUC5B); yellow-brown | Chronic OME; goblet cell metaplasia established |
> - | Contains pus; high neutrophil count; opaque | Active bacterial infection; overlap with AOM |
> - | Combination of above types | Transitional phases |
> - — MUC5B (responsible for the viscosity of glue ear) and MUC5AC, both increased in chronic OME. The effusion is rich in inflammatory mediators including prostaglandins (PGE2), leukotrienes (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4), histamine, and platelet-activating factor, alongside cytokines such as IL-1beta, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, and interferon-gamma. Immunoglobulins are present in significant concentrations, with IgG predominating; IgA, secretory IgA, IgM, and IgE (elevated in allergic OME) are also found. Complement activation products (C3, C4) and cellular elements — neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, and eosinophils (in allergic OME) — are routinely identified. Bacteria or their products (endotoxin, peptidoglycan) are detected in the majority of effusions. Pepsinogen, a marker of gastroesophageal reflux, is present in approximately **84%** of samples, and *H. pylori* antigens in roughly **16.3%**. Innate immune defence proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin) and destructive enzymes (collagenase, elastase, other proteases) are also present, with the latter contributing to tissue damage and structural sequelae.
> - theory (Politzer) holds that negative middle ear pressure from gas absorption causes transudation. The **inflammatory/infective theory** — the most widely accepted current model — proposes that viral and bacterial infection drives inflammation and effusion. The **Eustachian tube theory** places primary ET dysfunction at the centre of all downstream effects. The **adenoid theory** emphasises the adenoids as both a source of infection and a mechanical obstruction. The **allergy theory** highlights IgE-mediated inflammation of the middle ear and ET mucosa. The **reflux theory** implicates gastroesophageal reflux as a cause of chemical inflammation of the nasopharynx and ET. The **biofilm theory** focuses on chronic biofilm colonisation as the mechanism maintaining low-grade infection. Current consensus favours the **unified (multifactorial) theory**, which recognises that OME arises from a combination of all these factors operating in varying proportions in different patients.
> - ## Part D: Sequelae of OME
> - — delayed phonological development and reduced vocabulary acquisition are well documented. The educational consequences include poor attention and concentration, behavioural problems (the child may be perceived as inattentive or disruptive), and difficulty in noisy classroom environments, all contributing to **educational underperformance**. Long-term follow-up studies have also reported **high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss** (above 8 kHz), thought to result from passage of inflammatory mediators through the round window membrane causing cochlear hair cell damage; this component may be subclinical.
> - medially toward the promontory. This retraction can be graded using the **Sade classification** or the Tos classification.
> - — when a retraction pocket traps desquamated keratin, squamous epithelium invaginates into the middle ear to form an acquired cholesteatoma. This occurs in 0.1-0.5% of OME patients and requires surgical management.
> - results from hyaline degeneration and calcium/phosphate deposition in the lamina propria of the tympanic membrane and/or middle ear mucosa. It appears as white chalky plaques on otoscopy. Notably, its incidence is significantly increased after grommet insertion (up to 40-60%). The term myringosclerosis refers to involvement limited to the TM, while tympanosclerosis indicates involvement of both TM and middle ear structures.
> - occurs through chronic inflammation, enzymatic degradation, and the effects of negative pressure, which together cause resorption of ossicular bone. The **long process of the incus** is the most commonly affected structure, owing to its thin calibre and tenuous blood supply. This erosion can cause persistent conductive hearing loss even after effusion resolution.
> - represents the end stage of chronic retraction, in which dense fibrosis and granulation tissue bind the tympanic membrane to the promontory, ossicles, and middle ear walls. The result is severe, fixed conductive hearing loss that is extremely difficult to treat surgically. The **atelectatic ear** — in which the TM becomes thin, atrophic, and drapes over the ossicles and promontory like tissue paper, losing its structural rigidity — represents advanced retraction (Sade Grade III-IV). **Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM)** may develop from a persistent TM perforation related to OME changes or after grommet extrusion with ongoing middle ear infection. **Granular myringitis**, characterised by granulation tissue on the TM surface, may also occur after grommet insertion or chronic OME.
> - Recurrent acute otitis media (rAOM)** — OME predisposes to repeated episodes of AOM because the persistent middle ear fluid serves as a culture medium for bacterial growth
> - Balance disturbance** — Mild vestibular dysfunction from the effusion altering labyrinthine pressure dynamics; may manifest as clumsiness in children
> - Psychological impact** — Frustration, social withdrawal, and behavioural changes in the child; parental anxiety and stress; impact on family dynamics
> - Quality of life** — Reduced overall quality of life as measured by OM-specific instruments (OM-6 questionnaire)
> - Sleep disturbance** — Associated with snoring and obstructive sleep-disordered breathing, reflecting the shared adenoid aetiology
> - ## Part E: Management Overview
> - within three months. During this period, serial audiometry and otoscopy are performed at regular intervals to monitor for persistent or worsening disease. Parents should be educated about the natural history of OME and reassured appropriately. Practical strategies to mitigate hearing difficulties during the observation period include preferential seating in the classroom and face-to-face communication techniques.
> - may provide short-term benefit and can be tried in children with allergic rhinitis, but no long-term efficacy has been proven. **Autoinflation** (using the Otovent balloon) has positive short-term evidence — it improves middle ear pressure and promotes ET opening in a non-invasive manner, and can be used in children older than three years. **Oral or systemic antibiotics** produce only short-term benefit and are not recommended for long-term use due to the risk of resistance and minimal lasting improvement. **Oral steroids** similarly offer transient benefit but carry significant side effects and are not routinely recommended. **Antihistamines and decongestants** have **no proven benefit** in OME and are not recommended by any guideline. **Mucolytics** lack sufficient evidence and are not routinely used.
> - is the gold standard surgical intervention for OME. The ventilation tube bypasses the dysfunctional Eustachian tube, equalises middle ear pressure, allows drainage of the effusion, and permits aeration to reverse mucosal metaplasia. Indications include bilateral OME with hearing loss greater than 25-30 dB HL persisting for more than three months, unilateral OME with hearing loss persisting for more than three months with significant functional impact, OME in "at-risk" children (those with speech delay, developmental delay, craniofacial anomalies, or cleft palate), and recurrent AOM superimposed on OME.
> - is recommended when the child is older than three years (below this age, there is insufficient adenoid tissue for a meaningful effect). It reduces OME recurrence by removing the nasopharyngeal bacterial reservoir and relieving mechanical ET obstruction. The benefit is independent of adenoid size, reflecting the importance of the immunological reservoir function. Adenoidectomy is particularly beneficial at the time of second grommet insertion. UK NICE guidelines recommend bilateral OME with documented hearing loss greater than 25-30 dB HL persisting for more than three months as the threshold for grommets with or without adenoidectomy. Hearing aids are considered as an alternative to surgery in selected cases.
