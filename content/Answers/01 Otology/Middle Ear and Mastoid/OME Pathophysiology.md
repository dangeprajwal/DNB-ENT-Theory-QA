# Otitis Media with Effusion (OME): Definition, Etiology, Pathophysiology, and Sequelae

## DNB ENT Comprehensive Exam Answer

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery, 8th Edition, Volume 2 — Chapter 13 (Otitis Media with Effusion), Chapter 83 (Chronic Otitis Media)

> [!question] Questions
> - **Q69 (DNB 2023):** Define Otitis media with effusion and etiology. Describe the pathophysiology of OME and its sequelae.

---

## Part A: Definition of Otitis Media with Effusion (OME)

Otitis media with effusion (OME) is defined as the **presence of fluid in the middle ear cleft WITHOUT signs or symptoms of acute infection** — that is, without acute otalgia, fever, or a bulging tympanic membrane. The condition is known by several synonyms: **glue ear** (the most common lay term, referring especially to thick mucoid effusion), **secretory otitis media**, **serous otitis media**, and **non-suppurative otitis media**. When the effusion persists for longer than three months, it is classified as **chronic OME**.

OME is the **most common cause of hearing loss in children**. Approximately **80% of children** experience at least one episode by the age of three years, and at any given time roughly 20% of two-year-olds have an effusion. The age distribution is bimodal, with peak incidence at approximately **2 years** and again at **5 years** (school entry). The condition is more common in males than females, and **90% of episodes resolve spontaneously** within three months. Grommet insertion for persistent OME remains the single most common reason for elective surgery in childhood.

### Distinction from Acute Otitis Media (AOM)

| Feature | OME | AOM |
|---------|-----|-----|
| **Acute symptoms** | Absent | Present (otalgia, fever, irritability) |
| **TM appearance** | Dull, retracted, amber/blue, air-fluid level | Bulging, erythematous, may be perforated |
| **Infection** | No acute infection (may have chronic low-grade) | Acute bacterial/viral infection |
| **Pain** | Absent or minimal | Significant |
| **Hearing loss** | Usually the presenting complaint | Secondary to pain |

## Part B: Etiology and Risk Factors

OME is a **multifactorial disease** arising from the interplay of Eustachian tube dysfunction, infection, inflammation, host factors, and environmental exposures. Understanding the individual contributions of each factor is essential, as current consensus favours a unified theory in which all mechanisms operate in varying proportions.

### Eustachian Tube Dysfunction

The Eustachian tube (ET) is the single most important structure in OME pathogenesis. In children, the ET is anatomically immature — shorter (18 mm versus 36 mm in adults), more horizontal (10 degrees versus 45 degrees), and composed of floppier cartilage with reduced elasticity. These features predispose to both functional and mechanical obstruction. In addition, mucociliary clearance within the ET lumen may be impaired by ciliary dyskinesia or deficient surfactant production, both of which reduce the efficiency of the mucociliary escalator that normally clears secretions from the middle ear.

### Adenoid Hypertrophy

The adenoids contribute to OME through three distinct mechanisms. First, **mechanical obstruction** — enlarged adenoids physically block the nasopharyngeal opening of the Eustachian tube. Second, **reservoir of infection** — adenoid tissue harbours pathogenic bacteria, especially biofilm-forming organisms, that can ascend to the middle ear via the ET. Third, **immunological dysfunction** — chronic adenoiditis produces inflammatory mediators that cause ET mucosal oedema and dysfunction. These three mechanisms are frequently tested in examinations.

### Infection

Viral upper respiratory tract infection is the most common trigger for OME. The principal viruses include rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenovirus, and influenza. The viral infection causes mucosal inflammation and oedema within the nasopharynx and ET, impairing ventilation and drainage. Secondary bacterial infection follows, with the most important organisms being *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, non-typeable *Haemophilus influenzae*, and *Moraxella catarrhalis*. These organisms are capable of forming biofilms on the middle ear mucosa, which is a critical factor in disease persistence.

### Allergy, Environment, and Genetic Factors

Atopy and [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|allergic rhinitis]] contribute to OME by causing IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation and eosinophilic infiltration of the middle ear and ET mucosa, resulting in mucosal oedema and obstruction. Environmental risk factors include **daycare or nursery attendance** (which increases viral exposure), **passive smoking** (which impairs mucociliary clearance and increases bacterial adherence), winter season and cold climate, **bottle-feeding** (which deprives the infant of passive immunity and promotes supine feeding), overcrowding, and low socioeconomic status. Genetic factors are highly significant — twin studies demonstrate heritability exceeding 60%, and polymorphisms in immune genes (TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-10), mucin gene variants (MUC5B), and HLA associations have all been identified.

### Craniofacial Anomalies and Gastroesophageal Reflux

Cleft palate is associated with near-universal OME because the disrupted insertion of the tensor veli palatini muscle renders the ET incapable of active opening. Down syndrome predisposes through a combination of a narrow nasopharynx, hypotonic ET muscles, and immune dysfunction. Other craniofacial syndromes such as Treacher Collins also carry elevated risk.

Gastroesophageal reflux (GER) is an increasingly recognised contributor. Pepsinogen has been detected in **84%** of OME effusions, and *H. pylori* antigens in approximately **16.3%**. Acid and pepsin from refluxate damage the ET and middle ear mucosa, perpetuating chronic inflammation.

### Risk Factors Summary

| Category | Key Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| **Eustachian tube** | Short, horizontal, floppy cartilage in children; impaired mucociliary clearance; deficient surfactant |
| **Adenoids** | Mechanical obstruction; bacterial reservoir; immunological dysfunction |
| **Infection** | Viral URTI trigger; *S. pneumoniae*, NTHi, *M. catarrhalis*; biofilms |
| **Allergy** | Atopy; allergic rhinitis; IgE-mediated ET mucosal oedema |
| **Environmental** | Daycare; passive smoking; bottle-feeding; winter; overcrowding |
| **Genetic** | Heritability >60%; TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-10, MUC5B polymorphisms |
| **Craniofacial** | Cleft palate (near-universal OME); Down syndrome; Treacher Collins |
| **Reflux** | Pepsinogen in 84% of effusions; *H. pylori* in 16.3% |
| **Immune** | Immature immune system; selective IgA deficiency; reduced secretory IgA |

## Part C: Pathophysiology of OME

The development of OME follows a well-characterised pathophysiological cascade that begins with Eustachian tube dysfunction and culminates in a self-perpetuating cycle of chronic effusion. Understanding this sequence as a flowing narrative is essential for both clinical reasoning and examination answers.

### The Pathophysiological Cascade

The process is initiated by a **trigger event** — most commonly a viral upper respiratory tract infection, an allergic episode, or mechanical ET obstruction by adenoid hypertrophy. This trigger causes mucosal inflammation and oedema within the Eustachian tube, impairing its three core functions: **ventilation**, **drainage**, and **protection** of the middle ear. In children, the anatomically immature ET (shorter, more horizontal, with floppy cartilage) and the less efficient tensor veli palatini muscle compound this dysfunction. The result is functional or mechanical obstruction of the ET.

Once the ET is obstructed, the middle ear becomes a sealed cavity. Gases within this cavity — oxygen, nitrogen, and carbon dioxide — are progressively **absorbed by the mucosal vasculature**. Oxygen is absorbed fastest, followed by nitrogen. This creates **negative pressure** within the middle ear, reaching values of -200 to -400 daPa. The negative pressure has two immediate consequences: it causes **transudation of fluid** from mucosal capillaries into the middle ear space, and it draws the **tympanic membrane medially** (retraction) due to the pressure differential between the atmosphere and the middle ear cavity.

Persistent negative pressure and the associated chronic low-grade inflammation then induce a critical pathological change: **mucosal metaplasia**. The normal flat cuboidal epithelium of the middle ear mucosa transforms into **pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium** — essentially respiratory-type mucosa. This transformation is most marked in the anterior mesotympanum and around the ET orifice, and is driven by inflammatory cytokines and growth factors. The metaplastic epithelium contains a greatly **increased number of goblet cells** (mucus-secreting cells), and submucosal gland hypertrophy and hyperplasia follow. The result is a massive increase in mucus production within the middle ear. The mucin composition changes, with **MUC5B** becoming the predominant mucin — this is the molecule responsible for the thick, tenacious "glue" consistency of chronic effusion. MUC5AC is also increased. Because the ET remains obstructed, this mucus cannot drain and accumulates within the middle ear.

The stagnant, protein-rich effusion provides an ideal environment for **bacterial colonisation**. Pathogenic organisms ascend from the nasopharynx through the dysfunctional ET. The principal organisms — *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, non-typeable *Haemophilus influenzae*, and *Moraxella catarrhalis* — are cultured from **30-50%** of OME effusions that were previously considered sterile. PCR-based studies detect bacterial DNA in up to **80%** of effusions, indicating that infection plays a far larger role than culture-based methods alone would suggest. These bacteria stimulate an **intense inflammatory cascade**, with release of prostaglandins (PGE2, causing vasodilation and increased vascular permeability), leukotrienes (LTB4 and LTC4, causing chemotaxis and mucus hypersecretion), cytokines (IL-1beta, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha), histamine (from mast cell degranulation, especially in allergic OME), and reactive oxygen species (which cause tissue damage and perpetuate inflammation). These mediators increase vascular permeability, recruit neutrophils and macrophages, and stimulate yet further mucus production.

A pivotal mechanism in the chronicity of OME is **biofilm formation**. Bacteria organise into structured communities encased in an extracellular polysaccharide matrix on the middle ear mucosa. Biofilms are found in **60%** of OME middle ear mucosa compared to only **10%** of controls. These biofilms are approximately **1000 times more resistant** to antibiotics than planktonic (free-floating) bacteria and are also resistant to host immune defences. Critically, biofilms are capable of intermittent shedding of planktonic bacteria, which explains acute exacerbations of OME. Biofilm formation is the key mechanism explaining both the **chronicity** of OME and the well-documented **failure of antibiotic therapy**.

The combination of transudation from negative pressure, mucus hypersecretion from goblet cell metaplasia, inflammatory exudate, and impaired drainage results in **progressive fluid accumulation** — the effusion of OME. Over time, this creates a **self-perpetuating vicious cycle**: negative pressure promotes transudation and mucosal oedema, which worsens ET dysfunction; infection drives inflammation, which causes further mucosal oedema and ET obstruction, impairing drainage and allowing bacterial persistence; and biofilms confer antibiotic resistance, maintaining chronic infection and chronic inflammation. This cycle maintains the disease state and is the mechanism by which acute OME transitions to **chronic OME**.

### Types of Effusion

The nature of the middle ear effusion evolves over time and reflects the stage and severity of the disease.

| Type | Characteristics | Stage |
|------|----------------|-------|
| **Serous** | Thin, watery, straw-coloured; low viscosity; predominantly transudate | Early/mild OME; acute phase |
| **Mucoid ("Glue")** | Thick, tenacious, viscous; high mucin content (MUC5B); yellow-brown | Chronic OME; goblet cell metaplasia established |
| **Purulent** | Contains pus; high neutrophil count; opaque | Active bacterial infection; overlap with AOM |
| **Mixed** | Combination of above types | Transitional phases |

### Composition of Middle Ear Effusion

The effusion itself is a complex biological fluid. Its base is water and electrolytes, with a composition similar to plasma transudate. The dominant macromolecules are **mucins** — MUC5B (responsible for the viscosity of glue ear) and MUC5AC, both increased in chronic OME. The effusion is rich in inflammatory mediators including prostaglandins (PGE2), leukotrienes (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4), histamine, and platelet-activating factor, alongside cytokines such as IL-1beta, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, and interferon-gamma. Immunoglobulins are present in significant concentrations, with IgG predominating; IgA, secretory IgA, IgM, and IgE (elevated in allergic OME) are also found. Complement activation products (C3, C4) and cellular elements — neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocytes, and eosinophils (in allergic OME) — are routinely identified. Bacteria or their products (endotoxin, peptidoglycan) are detected in the majority of effusions. Pepsinogen, a marker of gastroesophageal reflux, is present in approximately **84%** of samples, and *H. pylori* antigens in roughly **16.3%**. Innate immune defence proteins (lysozyme, lactoferrin) and destructive enzymes (collagenase, elastase, other proteases) are also present, with the latter contributing to tissue damage and structural sequelae.

### Theories of OME Pathogenesis

Multiple theories have been proposed to explain the pathogenesis of OME, and all have some validity. The **hydrops ex vacuo** theory (Politzer) holds that negative middle ear pressure from gas absorption causes transudation. The **inflammatory/infective theory** — the most widely accepted current model — proposes that viral and bacterial infection drives inflammation and effusion. The **Eustachian tube theory** places primary ET dysfunction at the centre of all downstream effects. The **adenoid theory** emphasises the adenoids as both a source of infection and a mechanical obstruction. The **allergy theory** highlights IgE-mediated inflammation of the middle ear and ET mucosa. The **reflux theory** implicates gastroesophageal reflux as a cause of chemical inflammation of the nasopharynx and ET. The **biofilm theory** focuses on chronic biofilm colonisation as the mechanism maintaining low-grade infection. Current consensus favours the **unified (multifactorial) theory**, which recognises that OME arises from a combination of all these factors operating in varying proportions in different patients.

## Part D: Sequelae of OME

The sequelae of OME are broadly divided into hearing-related consequences, structural changes to the middle ear and tympanic membrane, and other systemic effects. These sequelae are the primary justification for intervention in persistent OME.

### Hearing-Related Sequelae

The hallmark of OME is **conductive hearing loss**, typically in the range of **20-40 dB HL**. This loss is fluctuating in nature and results from mass loading of the tympanic membrane, damping of the ossicular chain, and impedance mismatch at the tympanic membrane. Bilateral involvement produces greater functional impairment than unilateral disease. In young children, hearing loss during the critical period for speech and language development (ages 1-3 years) leads to **speech and language delay** — delayed phonological development and reduced vocabulary acquisition are well documented. The educational consequences include poor attention and concentration, behavioural problems (the child may be perceived as inattentive or disruptive), and difficulty in noisy classroom environments, all contributing to **educational underperformance**. Long-term follow-up studies have also reported **high-frequency sensorineural hearing loss** (above 8 kHz), thought to result from passage of inflammatory mediators through the round window membrane causing cochlear hair cell damage; this component may be subclinical.

### Structural Sequelae

Persistent negative middle ear pressure causes the tympanic membrane to **retract** medially toward the promontory. This retraction can be graded using the **Sade classification** or the Tos classification.

| Sade Grade | Description |
|------------|-------------|
| I | Mild retraction — TM retracted but not touching incus |
| II | TM retracted onto incus/stapes |
| III | TM retracted onto promontory (atelectasis) |
| IV | TM adherent to promontory (adhesive otitis media) |

Localised areas of retraction, termed **retraction pockets**, most commonly develop in the posterosuperior quadrant of the pars tensa and in the pars flaccida (Prussak's space). These pockets are classified as self-cleaning or non-self-cleaning. The most feared structural sequela is **[[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]]** — when a retraction pocket traps desquamated keratin, squamous epithelium invaginates into the middle ear to form an acquired cholesteatoma. This occurs in 0.1-0.5% of OME patients and requires surgical management.

**Tympanosclerosis** results from hyaline degeneration and calcium/phosphate deposition in the lamina propria of the tympanic membrane and/or middle ear mucosa. It appears as white chalky plaques on otoscopy. Notably, its incidence is significantly increased after grommet insertion (up to 40-60%). The term myringosclerosis refers to involvement limited to the TM, while tympanosclerosis indicates involvement of both TM and middle ear structures.

**Ossicular erosion** occurs through chronic inflammation, enzymatic degradation, and the effects of negative pressure, which together cause resorption of ossicular bone. The **long process of the incus** is the most commonly affected structure, owing to its thin calibre and tenuous blood supply. This erosion can cause persistent conductive hearing loss even after effusion resolution.

**Adhesive otitis media** represents the end stage of chronic retraction, in which dense fibrosis and granulation tissue bind the tympanic membrane to the promontory, ossicles, and middle ear walls. The result is severe, fixed conductive hearing loss that is extremely difficult to treat surgically. The **atelectatic ear** — in which the TM becomes thin, atrophic, and drapes over the ossicles and promontory like tissue paper, losing its structural rigidity — represents advanced retraction (Sade Grade III-IV). **Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM)** may develop from a persistent TM perforation related to OME changes or after grommet extrusion with ongoing middle ear infection. **Granular myringitis**, characterised by granulation tissue on the TM surface, may also occur after grommet insertion or chronic OME.

### Other Sequelae

- **Recurrent acute otitis media (rAOM)** — OME predisposes to repeated episodes of AOM because the persistent middle ear fluid serves as a culture medium for bacterial growth
- **Balance disturbance** — Mild vestibular dysfunction from the effusion altering labyrinthine pressure dynamics; may manifest as clumsiness in children
- **Psychological impact** — Frustration, social withdrawal, and behavioural changes in the child; parental anxiety and stress; impact on family dynamics
- **Quality of life** — Reduced overall quality of life as measured by OM-specific instruments (OM-6 questionnaire)
- **Sleep disturbance** — Associated with snoring and obstructive sleep-disordered breathing, reflecting the shared adenoid aetiology

## Part E: Management Overview

### Watchful Waiting (First-Line)

The cornerstone of initial management is **active observation**, justified by the fact that **90% of OME episodes resolve spontaneously** within three months. During this period, serial audiometry and otoscopy are performed at regular intervals to monitor for persistent or worsening disease. Parents should be educated about the natural history of OME and reassured appropriately. Practical strategies to mitigate hearing difficulties during the observation period include preferential seating in the classroom and face-to-face communication techniques.

### Medical Management

Medical therapy has a limited role in OME. **Nasal corticosteroid sprays** may provide short-term benefit and can be tried in children with allergic rhinitis, but no long-term efficacy has been proven. **Autoinflation** (using the Otovent balloon) has positive short-term evidence — it improves middle ear pressure and promotes ET opening in a non-invasive manner, and can be used in children older than three years. **Oral or systemic antibiotics** produce only short-term benefit and are not recommended for long-term use due to the risk of resistance and minimal lasting improvement. **Oral steroids** similarly offer transient benefit but carry significant side effects and are not routinely recommended. **Antihistamines and decongestants** have **no proven benefit** in OME and are not recommended by any guideline. **Mucolytics** lack sufficient evidence and are not routinely used.

### Surgical Management

**Myringotomy with ventilation tube insertion (grommets)** is the gold standard surgical intervention for OME. The ventilation tube bypasses the dysfunctional Eustachian tube, equalises middle ear pressure, allows drainage of the effusion, and permits aeration to reverse mucosal metaplasia. Indications include bilateral OME with hearing loss greater than 25-30 dB HL persisting for more than three months, unilateral OME with hearing loss persisting for more than three months with significant functional impact, OME in "at-risk" children (those with speech delay, developmental delay, craniofacial anomalies, or cleft palate), and recurrent AOM superimposed on OME.

Grommet types are divided into short-term tubes (Shah or Shepard pattern, which extrude spontaneously in 6-12 months) and long-term tubes (T-tubes, which remain in situ for 18-24 months). Complications include otorrhoea (the most common, occurring in 10-26%), premature extrusion, blockage, tympanosclerosis (up to 40-60%), persistent perforation (2-5% for short-term tubes, up to 17% for T-tubes), and cholesteatoma at the tube site (rare).

**Adenoidectomy** is recommended when the child is older than three years (below this age, there is insufficient adenoid tissue for a meaningful effect). It reduces OME recurrence by removing the nasopharyngeal bacterial reservoir and relieving mechanical ET obstruction. The benefit is independent of adenoid size, reflecting the importance of the immunological reservoir function. Adenoidectomy is particularly beneficial at the time of second grommet insertion. UK NICE guidelines recommend bilateral OME with documented hearing loss greater than 25-30 dB HL persisting for more than three months as the threshold for grommets with or without adenoidectomy. Hearing aids are considered as an alternative to surgery in selected cases.

### Management in Special Populations

| Population | Consideration |
|------------|--------------|
| **Cleft palate** | Near-universal OME; early grommet insertion often required; may need repeated insertions; long-term T-tubes considered |
| **Down syndrome** | High prevalence; narrow ear canals make grommet insertion technically challenging; higher complication rates; hearing aids may be preferable |
| **Aboriginal/Indigenous populations** | Higher prevalence and severity; CSOM is a common sequela; early aggressive management recommended |
