## Part A: Classification of Ossicular Defects (Q106)

### Austin–Kartush Classification

The most widely used system for classifying ossicular chain defects when the incus is absent. First proposed by Austin (1971), modified by Kartush (1994).

**Austin Classification (absent incus):**

| Group | Ossicular Status | Abbreviation | Prevalence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Malleus handle AND stapes superstructure present | M+ S+ | 60% |
| B | Malleus handle present, stapes superstructure absent | M+ S− | 23% |
| C | Malleus handle absent, stapes superstructure present | M− S+ | 8% |
| D | Malleus handle AND stapes superstructure absent | M− S− | 8% |

**Kartush Modifications (1994):**

| Group | Additional Categories |
|---|---|
| O | Intact ossicular chain |
| E | Ossicular head fixation |
| F | Stapes fixation |

### Ossicular Chain Defects in Chronic Middle Ear Disease

| Defect | With [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]] (n=692) | Without Cholesteatoma (n=519) |
|---|---|---|
| Ossicular chain intact | 27% | 71% |
| Incus alone eroded | 26% | 18% |
| Incus and stapes eroded, malleus intact | 24% | 7% |
| Incus, stapes and malleus eroded | 11% | 2% |
| Incus and malleus eroded, stapes intact | 8% | 2% |
| Stapes alone eroded | 2% | 0.4% |

*Erosion of the long process of incus is the most common ossicular chain defect.*

### Prognostic Factors

**Middle Ear Risk Index (MERI) — Kartush (1994, modified 2001):**

| Otologic Factor | Maximum Score |
|---|---|
| Otorrhoea (Belluci classification: 0 dry, 1 occasionally wet, 2 persistently wet, 3 wet + cleft palate) | 3 |
| Perforation | 1 |
| Cholesteatoma | 2 |
| Ossicular status (Austin–Kartush) | 4 |
| Middle ear granulation | 2 |
| Previous surgery | 2 |
| Smoking | 2 |
| **Total** | **16** |

Alternative scoring: OOPS index (Ossiculoplasty Outcome Parameter Staging), SPITE factors (Surgical, Prosthetic, Infection, Tissue, Eustachian).

### Key Prognostic Factors
- **Presence of stapes superstructure** is the most significant factor — multivariate analyses show poorer outcomes when absent
- Middle ear status (aeration, infection) is most important overall factor
- Results deteriorate over time — most studies have short follow-up (1 year)

## Part B: Materials for Ossiculoplasty (Q106)

### Autograft

| Material | Details |
|---|---|
| **Incus** | Most widely used autograft; sculpted with ossicle forceps and microdrill; body placed on stapes head, short process directed towards malleus; excellent biocompatibility; low extrusion risk; risk of residual cholesteatoma if enveloped by disease |
| **Malleus head** | Used when incus unavailable; brought into direct contact with TM |
| **Cortical bone** | Harvested from mastoid or EAC; used for bridging small ISJ defects |
| **Cartilage** | Less stable than ossicle; prone to displacement/resorption; less commonly used for ossiculoplasty |

*Advantage:* Excellent biocompatibility, low extrusion risk, low cost
*Limitation:* Incus not always available in diseased ears; sculpting skill required; cholesteatoma risk

### Homograft (Allograft)

- Pre-prepared donor tissue: cortical bone, cartilage, ossicles, en bloc ossicular chain with TM
- Shorter operative time (pre-prepared)
- Risk of disease transmission: CJD (2 cases via ear surgery — dura mater and pericardium); no reported CJD via homograft ossicles
- Serological screening + appropriate preparation reduces risk
- Fallen out of favour in many countries due to CJD concerns and improved alloplastic materials

### Alloplastic (Prosthetic) Materials

| Material | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Titanium** | Introduced 1990s; high rigidity, low weight; complex designs possible (open head, claw attachments); biocompatible; extrusion rate <5%; most popular current alloplastic material |
| **Hydroxyapatite** | Calcium-based bioceramics similar to bone; low extrusion rates; popular and proven; often used as head component in compound prostheses |
| **Plasti-Pore (PTFE porous)** | Widely used since 1976; high extrusion when touching TM — requires cartilage interposition; foreign body giant cell reaction on histology |
| **Solid plastics (PTFE, polyethylene)** | Early materials; largely abandoned — absorption at bony interfaces and extrusion |
| **Stainless steel, gold** | Early metals; largely abandoned |
| **Bone cement (glass ionomer)** | For reconstructing long process of incus; 80% achieve ABG <20 dB at 1 year; risk of spilling onto footplate |

### Prosthesis Terminology
- **PORP** (Partial Ossicular Replacement Prosthesis): For use with intact stapes superstructure
- **TORP** (Total Ossicular Replacement Prosthesis): For use when stapes superstructure absent — connects footplate to TM/malleus
- **Columella:** Sound transmission restored with ossicle-to-TM connection
- **Assembly:** Ossicle-to-ossicle connection

## Part C: Management of Erosion of Long Process of Incus (Q106)

Erosion of the long process of the incus is the **most common ossicular chain defect** in chronic middle ear disease.

### Options Based on Defect Size

**1. Small Defect (Tenuous ISJ Connection Remains):**
- **Bone cement:** Glass ionomer cement applied to bridge small gap; 80% achieve ABG closure to <20 dB at 1 year; care needed to avoid spillage onto footplate or unintended fixation to posterior canal wall
- **Bone pate:** Collected by drilling healthy mastoid cortex; works best with partial connection remaining; may be mixed with blood and/or fibrin glue for adhesion

**2. Complete Erosion with Intact Malleus and Stapes (Austin–Kartush Type A):**
- **Incus interposition (autograft):** Most widely used; body placed on stapes head, short process directed towards malleus handle; multiple configuration options
  - Classical: short process rests against malleus handle, medial to it
  - Modified: groove in short process resting on malleus handle, or lateral to handle
- **Malleus head reconstruction:** When incus unavailable; sculpted malleus head placed on stapes in stapes-to-drum configuration
- **PORP (alloplastic):** Titanium or hydroxyapatite prosthesis placed between stapes superstructure and malleus handle or TM
  - Stapes-to-malleus: preserves catenary lever mechanism; prosthesis medial to handle; disadvantage — offset angle causes inefficient energy transfer if malleus very anterior (tilting force at footplate when angle >45°)
  - Malleus relocation: separating malleus from TM, dividing tensor tympani, stretching anterior suspensory ligament to reposition malleus over stapes — reduces tilting force
  - Stapes-to-TM: used when malleus orientation unfavourable; loses catenary lever mechanism; cartilage interposition to reduce extrusion

**3. Malleus Present, Stapes Absent (Austin–Kartush Type B):**
- **TORP:** Long prosthesis from footplate to malleus or TM
- **Homograft incus:** Long process on footplate, sculpted notch accommodates malleus
- **Footplate shoe prosthesis:** Anchors medial end of TORP on footplate; reduces displacement and improves force vector
- Inherently less stable — absence of stapes superstructure is key determinant of poorer outcomes

**4. Malleus Absent, Stapes Present (Austin–Kartush Type C):**
- Absence of malleus = major independent prognostic factor for poorer outcomes
- **Stapes-to-TM PORP:** Similar to Type A but without malleus attachment
- **Neomalleus:** Created from incus, cortical bone, or cartilage strip; placed medial to TM graft — superior results compared to columella approach (Black 2002)
- **Malleus replacement prosthesis:** Newer development (Vincent et al.); improved hearing over standard TORP/PORP

**5. Malleus AND Stapes Absent (Austin–Kartush Type D):**
- Most challenging defect; poorest outcomes
- TORP from footplate to TM; consider neomalleus or malleus replacement prosthesis for stability

### Staged vs Primary Reconstruction
- **Primary reconstruction preferred** when: safe dry ear, well-aerated middle ear, low recurrence risk
- **Staged reconstruction** when: significant risk of residual cholesteatoma, poor ET function/retraction risk, active infection
- DW-MRI increasingly allows radiological cholesteatoma surveillance without necessitating second-look surgery, enabling primary reconstruction in more cases

### Operative Considerations
- Lower-tension reconstructions (shorter prosthesis) produce better sound transmission but increased displacement risk
- Prosthesis in contact with TM should have cartilage interposition (most surgeons agree, though not all)
- Results tend to deteriorate over time
- Surgeons should audit own results to enable accurate patient consent

## Part D: CSF Otorrhea After Temporal Bone Trauma (Q93)

### Mechanism
- Temporal bone fractures may disrupt the dura and arachnoid overlying the temporal bone
- CSF leak occurs through fracture lines into middle ear → through perforated TM (otorrhea) or via intact TM → Eustachian tube → rhinorrhea ([[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhea]] from ear)
- Longitudinal fractures: more common (~80% of TB fractures); may tear tegmen tympani
- Transverse fractures: less common; may disrupt otic capsule, IAC

### Diagnosis

**Clinical Features:**
- Clear watery discharge from ear following head trauma
- May present as rhinorrhea (CSF via ET)
- May be delayed if TM intact (CSF accumulates in middle ear)
- Associated: hearing loss (conductive from haemotympanum/ossicular disruption; SNHL if otic capsule fracture), [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]], vertigo

**Investigations:**

| Test | Details |
|---|---|
| **Beta-2 transferrin** | Gold standard biochemical test; specific to CSF (also in perilymph and aqueous humour); not found in blood, tears, nasal secretions or middle ear effusion |
| **Beta-trace protein** | Alternative CSF marker; faster result than beta-2 transferrin |
| **HRCT temporal bone** | Identifies fracture lines, tegmen defect, opacified middle ear/mastoid; delineates anatomy for surgical planning |
| **CT cisternography** | Intrathecal contrast → CT; localizes active leak site; useful if HRCT inconclusive |
| **MRI cisternography** | Non-invasive alternative; T2-weighted sequences show CSF signal through defect |
| **Glucose testing** | Unreliable — glucose present in nasal secretions; NOT recommended as sole diagnostic test |

### Management

**Conservative Management (First Line):**
- ~80% of traumatic CSF leaks resolve spontaneously within 7–10 days
- Strict bed rest with head elevation (30°)
- Avoid straining, nose blowing, sneezing with mouth closed
- Prophylactic antibiotics: controversial — no strong evidence; some centres use as meningitis prophylaxis
- Lumbar drain: if conservative measures fail after 7–10 days; reduces CSF pressure to promote healing
- Pneumococcal vaccination: recommended given increased meningitis risk

**Surgical Management (if conservative fails >7–14 days or recurrent):**

| Approach | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Middle fossa approach (MFA)** | Tegmen defects; allows direct visualization of floor of middle cranial fossa; can repair small dural defects with temporalis fascia/muscle, bone pate/wax, hydroxyapatite |
| **Transmastoid approach** | Mastoid roof or posterior fossa plate defects; reinforcement of tegmen with fascia/bone/cartilage from mastoid side |
| **Combined approach** | Larger or complex defects |

**Repair Materials:**
- Temporalis fascia, muscle flaps
- Bone pate, bone wax
- Hydroxyapatite cement
- Cartilage
- Multilayer closure preferred (underlay + overlay technique)

### Complications of CSF Leak
- **Meningitis:** Most feared complication; risk increases with duration of leak; drives urgency for surgical repair if conservative management fails
- **Brain abscess:** Rare; from ascending infection
- **Encephalocele:** May herniate through tegmen defect

## Part E: Cochleostomy — Surgical Steps for CI Surgery (Q43)

### Surgical Approach (Transmastoid / Posterior Tympanotomy)

**Step-by-Step:**

1. **Post-auricular incision and soft tissue exposure:**
   - Post-auricular skin incision; elevate periosteal flap
   - Identify landmarks: spine of Henle, temporal line (linea temporalis)

2. **Cortical mastoidectomy:**
   - Saucerize mastoid cortex
   - Identify: lateral semicircular canal, tegmen, sigmoid sinus, short process of incus (key landmark)
   - Thin posterior canal wall

3. **Posterior tympanotomy (facial recess approach):**
   - Identify triangular space bounded by: fossa incudis (superiorly), chorda tympani nerve (laterally), facial nerve (medially)
   - Open facial recess using diamond burr
   - Visualize: round window niche, promontory, stapes, incudostapedial joint

4. **Implant bed preparation:**
   - Drill shallow well in cortical bone of temporal squama for receiver-stimulator
   - May include tie-down holes for securing device

5. **Cochleostomy (Two Options):**

   **Option A — Bony Cochleostomy (anteroinferior to round window):**
   - Identify round window niche
   - Drill anteroinferior to the round window membrane on promontory using 1 mm diamond burr
   - Create "blue-line" of endosteum without opening
   - Remove final bone shell with pick/small hook to enter scala tympani
   - Goal: enter scala tympani at basal turn
   - Soft surgery techniques: no suction in cochleostomy; blood kept out; slow insertion

   **Option B — Round Window Approach (increasingly preferred):**
   - Round window membrane identified and exposed by carefully drilling overhanging bone of round window niche
   - RW membrane incised with needle/pick
   - Electrode inserted directly through round window membrane into scala tympani
   - Advantages: direct entry into scala tympani; less drilling; potentially less trauma; better hearing preservation

6. **Electrode insertion:**
   - Electrode array gently advanced into scala tympani
   - Full insertion aimed for (except short electrodes for EAS)
   - Slow, atraumatic insertion technique critical for hearing preservation
   - Soft tissue seal around electrode entry point (muscle, fascia) to prevent perilymph leak

7. **Secure device and closure:**
   - Receiver-stimulator secured in bony well (tie-down sutures or tight fit)
   - Excess electrode lead coiled in mastoid cavity
   - Periosteal flap replaced
   - Wound closure in layers
   - Post-operative X-ray/imaging confirms electrode position (detect tip fold-over)

### Round Window vs Bony Cochleostomy

| Feature | Round Window | Bony Cochleostomy |
|---|---|---|
| Entry point | Through RW membrane | Anteroinferior to RW on promontory |
| Scala tympani entry | Direct, guaranteed | Usually ST; risk of scala vestibuli entry |
| Trauma | Potentially less | More drilling |
| Hearing preservation | Potentially better | Variable |
| Visualization | Limited by RW niche overhang | Better after niche drilling |
| Popularity trend | Increasingly preferred | Traditional standard |

### Cochlear Ossification (Challenging Cases)
- Post-meningitis most common cause; ranges from small basal turn deposit to complete obliteration
- Partial ossification: drill through new bone, identify patent lumen distally; partial insertion or dual-array electrode
- Complete ossification: may require drill-out cochleostomy or alternative device (ABI)
- Imaging may not fully predict degree of ossification (progression between imaging and surgery)
