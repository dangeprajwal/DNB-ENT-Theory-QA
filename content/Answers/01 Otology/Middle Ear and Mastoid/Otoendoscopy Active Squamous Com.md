# Oto-endoscopy and Management of Active Squamous Chronic Otitis Media

**Question:** *Write a brief note on oto-endoscopy. What are the advantages and disadvantages of it? Describe the management of active squamous chronic otitis media.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Volume 2 – Chapter 87: Otoendoscopy (Bowdler, Leong & Pothier); Chapter 83: Chronic Otitis Media (Browning)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Oto-endoscopy represents a paradigm shift in ear surgery from the traditional microscopic approach to an endoscope-assisted or fully endoscopic technique. Modern advances in endoscope design have provided new tools for examination and surgery of the middle ear and beyond. Active squamous chronic otitis media ([[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]]) is a retraction pocket filled with keratinous debris associated with inflammation and pus production, requiring primarily surgical management. The endoscope has become increasingly important in cholesteatoma surgery by providing superior visualization of hidden recesses of the middle ear.

---

## PART A: OTO-ENDOSCOPY - A BRIEF NOTE

### History

Although the fibre-optic endoscope was invented in 1954 by Hopkins, oto-endoscopy only began with the fibre-optic hypodermic microscope used by Long in 1965. From the early 1980s onwards, oto-endoscopy was increasingly used to inspect and diagnose middle ear disease, particularly residual disease after canal wall-up mastoidectomy. Thomassin strongly influenced the field by emphasizing the use of endoscopes to search for and remove disease in the anterior epitympanic recess, tubal orifice and sinus tympani, and observed a distinct reduction in residual cholesteatoma rates.

### Endoscope Specifications

Two schools of thought exist regarding endoscope selection:

1. **Diagnostic/adjunct use:** Shorter 2.7 mm diameter 0, 30, 45 and 70 degree endoscopes used alongside the microscope
2. **[[Endoscopic Ear Surgery|Endoscopic Ear Surgery]] (EES):** Longer 14-18 cm, 3-4 mm diameter 0 degree Hopkins rod (the same endoscope used for sinus surgery), with the majority of surgery performed endoscopically

The direction of view is variable depending on the angulation of the endoscope employed (0, 30, 45, or 70 degrees). A high-quality three CCD (charge-coupled device) camera is recommended, as single CCD cameras are prone to "red-out" in the presence of bleeding.

### Pre-operative Imaging

High-resolution CT is recommended before any middle ear procedure. For endoscopic surgery, CT images reconstructed along the plane of the external auditory meatus (rather than standard axial/sagittal/coronal views) are most helpful for pre-operative planning.

## PART B: ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES OF OTO-ENDOSCOPY

### Advantages

**Table: Advantages of Oto-endoscopy over the Microscope**

| Advantage | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Wide field of vision | Panoramic view of middle ear structures |
| Variable angle of view | 0, 30, 45, 70 degree endoscopes allow visualization "around corners" |
| Superior access to blind spots | Sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, protympanum, facial sinus recess, hypotympanum |
| Minimally invasive | No postauricular/endaural incision needed in fully endoscopic approach |
| Reduced tissue violation | Less removal of healthy bone purely for access |
| Reduced residual disease | Thomassin showed residual disease rate at second look reduced from 47% to 5% with routine otoendoscopy |
| Minimally invasive second look | 1 cm stab incision for second-look surgery instead of reopening the full incision |
| Better documentation | Intra-operative photodocumentation and video recording |
| Proximity of image | Close-up, magnified view of structures |

**Key evidence:** Bowdler and Walsh compared accessibility of middle ear subsites in canal wall-up temporal bone dissections using endoscopes versus the microscope. The microscope failed to give a view of the sinus tympani either through the posterior tympanotomy or via the ear canal, whereas the 0 degree endoscope provided good views of all areas, and the 70 degree endoscope gave excellent views of the sinus tympani and Eustachian tube.

**The facial recess advantage:** If approached through transcanal endoscopy, the facial recess ceases to be a recess and becomes merely a small depression on the posterior wall of the mesotympanum reachable within minutes. In contrast, microscopic technique requires cortical mastoidectomy, identification of the facial nerve, and provides only limited keyhole access between the chorda tympani and facial nerve.

### Disadvantages

| Disadvantage | Detail |
|--------------|--------|
| One-handed surgery | Endoscope occupies one hand, leaving only one hand to operate |
| Loss of binocular vision | No true 3D (stereoscopic) vision, though with experience this has limited impact |
| Thermal injury risk | Risk of heat injury to middle ear structures from the endoscope light source |
| Fogging and smearing | Peri-operative haemorrhage causes fogging of the endoscope tip, requiring frequent cleaning |
| Learning curve | Surgeons accustomed to microscopic technique need time to adapt |
| Limited in curved EAC | Extreme curvature of the external auditory canal may limit otoendoscopy |
| Fragility | Smaller diameter endoscopes (1.7 mm) are extremely fragile and cannot tolerate bending |
| No improvement in mastoid access | Endoscopy does not improve access to mastoid disease |
| Risk of ossicular injury | Indelicate tissue handling may lead to forceful dislocation or fracture of ossicles |
| Red-out with single CCD | Single CCD cameras suffer complete saturation in a bleeding field |

### Safety: Thermal Injury

An in vivo canine study found temperature rise at the lateral semicircular canal increased in relation to endoscope diameter. The 3 mm Hopkins rod produced heating equivalent to or greater than a warm 44 degrees C water caloric test. A 5 mm burn on the medial wall of the middle ear was found when using a 3 mm Hopkins rod. However, measurements taken 5 mm from the tip were far lower and cooling occurs rapidly when the endoscope is removed.

## PART C: APPLICATIONS OF OTO-ENDOSCOPY

### Diagnostic Applications

1. **Retraction pockets and cholesteatoma:** Most common diagnostic indication; assessment of extent in blind spots (sinus tympani, attic, protympanum, hypotympanum)
2. **Ossicular continuity through perforation:** Can be performed in outpatient setting through an existing TM perforation
3. **Through myringotomy:** For unexplained conductive hearing loss, perilymphatic fistulas, occult cholesteatoma
4. **Evaluation of round window patency:** Relevant for intratympanic drug delivery
5. **Stapes fixation assessment:** Endoscopic observation of stapedial reflex through laser-assisted myringotomy

### Therapeutic Applications

1. **[[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|Tympanoplasty]]/[[Ossiculoplasty CSF Otorrhea Cochleostomy|ossiculoplasty]]:** Endoscopic type I tympanoplasty first described in 1992 with 92% graft take rate and air-bone gap closure <10 dB in 83%
2. **Retraction pocket surgery:** Ability to retrace the sac from mesotympanum through its twists and turns around ossicles, facilitating complete matrix removal while preserving ossicles
3. **Cholesteatoma surgery:** Tarabichi described transcanal endoscopic management with 7% recurrence rate in 168 patients
4. **Canal wall-down mastoidectomy:** One-third of cases had disease extending into sinus tympani, made visible with side-viewing endoscopes
5. **Minimally invasive second-look surgery:** 1 cm stab incision anterior to original postauricular incision, with endoscope inserted into mastoid cavity
6. **Endoscopic stapedotomy:** Excellent exposure of oval window niche without removing healthy bone, avoiding chorda tympani manipulation
7. **Endoscopic [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]]:** Electrode routed through epitympanum (controversial, still evolving)
8. **Neuro-otological surgery:** Endoscopy of the cerebellopontine angle, [[Vestibular Schwannoma|acoustic neuroma]] surgery, inner ear surgery

## PART D: CLASSIFICATION OF CHRONIC OTITIS MEDIA

Understanding the classification is essential before discussing management.

**Table: Classification of COM**

| COM Type | Otoscopic Findings |
|----------|-------------------|
| Healed COM | Thinning/opacification of pars tensa; no perforation or retraction |
| Inactive mucosal COM | Permanent perforation; non-inflamed middle ear mucosa |
| Active mucosal COM | Permanent TM defect; inflamed mucosa producing mucopus |
| Inactive squamous COM | Retraction of pars flaccida/tensa with potential to become active |
| Active squamous COM (cholesteatoma) | Retraction with retained squamous debris, inflammation and pus |

**Important:** The terms "safe" and "unsafe" are incorrect and misleading as complications can occur from any ear with active COM irrespective of pathology. The earlier "tubotympanic" and "atticoantral" distinction is redundant.

## PART E: MANAGEMENT OF ACTIVE SQUAMOUS COM (CHOLESTEATOMA)

### Natural History

Active squamous epithelial disease is a retraction pocket filled with keratinous debris which may remain active or become inactive. The natural history is for anatomical progression over a variable timescale with inevitable involvement of the ossicular chain and possible erosion of the lateral semicircular canal. Life-threatening intracranial complications can occur. However, the timescale is extremely variable and many patients live with the disease with minimal disability.

Hearing is often preserved until a very late stage despite the ossicular chain being disrupted, because the cholesteatoma sac bridges the gap between the functioning ossicular chain and the inner ear.

### Presentation

- Classically: foul-smelling otorrhoea and hearing impairment
- Many patients complain only of hearing impairment (small quantities of pus dry up as crusts)
- A small crust in the attic can be mistaken for wax (wax only occurs there if pushed by a cotton bud)
- An aural polyp obscuring the attic or posterior pars tensa should be assumed cholesteatoma until proven otherwise

### Examination

Microscopic examination with thorough cleaning of discharge is required. In very inflamed ears, cholesteatoma may not be visible at first presentation. Rigid endoscope examination is helpful when the extent of the retraction is not visible with the microscope.

### Imaging

- **CT:** Standard imaging; good resolution of anatomical landmarks; poor differentiation of cholesteatoma from granulation tissue; sensitivity for fistulae is unreliable
- **Non-echoplanar DW-MRI:** Very reliable at detecting cholesteatoma and differentiating it from other soft tissues; reduces necessity for second-look surgery
- The surgeon should always assume a fistula is present and the facial nerve is dehiscent to minimize complications

### Aims of Management

The aims are to:
1. Relieve the patient's symptoms
2. Minimize risks of complications

### Aims of Surgery

1. **Eradication of disease**
2. **An epithelialized, self-cleaning ear**
3. **Hearing maintenance or improvement**

The idea that the aim of surgery is simply to create a "safe ear" is obsolete. Many patients present because of hearing impairment and will not be satisfied with management that provides no apparent benefit.

### Conservative Management

- Indicated for elderly/unfit patients where risks of surgery may outweigh risks of disease
- Careful aural toilet with removal of squamous debris
- Topical treatment at regular intervals
- Hearing aids when hearing disability is present

### Surgical Techniques

#### 1. Canal Wall-Down (CWD) Mastoidectomy

**Traditional method:** Modified radical mastoidectomy using posterior-to-anterior approach. Mastoid opened behind EAC, cholesteatoma followed forwards through aditus into attic with removal of posterior bony wall. Results in a large cavity.

**Small cavity mastoidectomy (atticoantrostomy):** Anterior-to-posterior approach. Cholesteatoma identified in epitympanum/posterior mesotympanum and followed backwards. Results in a much smaller cavity (mean 1.4 cm3 vs 2.4 cm3 after modified radical mastoidectomy).

**Advantages of CWD:**
- Lower recurrence rates (5-15%)
- Recurrences easily identified in outpatient clinic
- Second-look operations rarely necessary
- Residual cholesteatoma should be a rarity in competent hands

**Requirements for a trouble-free cavity:**
- Rounded, smoothly contoured with no overhanging ridges
- No facial ridge (low facial ridge)
- TM repaired to close communication between cavity and Eustachian orifice
- Adequate meatus size relative to cavity (meatoplasty almost always required)
- Use conchal cartilage to reduce cavity size

**Problems:** 20-25% continue to have otorrhoea (though experienced surgeons report only 5% moist at 5 years).

#### 2. Canal Wall-Up (CWU) Mastoidectomy

**Advantages:**
- Preservation of intact external auditory canal
- No mastoid cavity

**Disadvantages:**
- Higher incidence of recurrent and residual cholesteatoma (20-50%)
- Second-look operations after 12-18 months necessary in most cases
- Technically more difficult, longer operating time
- At least one further operation may be required

Second look can often be avoided nowadays by the use of DW-MRI.

#### 3. CWU vs CWD Comparison

| Feature | Canal Wall-Down | Canal Wall-Up |
|---------|----------------|---------------|
| Recurrence rate | 5-15% | 20-50% |
| Second-look required | Rarely | Usually (12-18 months) |
| Mastoid cavity | Present (can be problematic) | Absent |
| Technical difficulty | Less | Greater |
| Operating time | Shorter | Longer |
| Long-term hearing | No significant difference | No significant difference |
| Detection of recurrence | Easy (clinic exam) | Requires DW-MRI or surgery |

There is no evidence that long-term hearing results differ between procedures. Toner and Smyth reported better hearing outcomes 1 year after CWU, but at long-term follow-up (mean 9 years), there was no significant difference.

#### 4. Pars Tensa Cholesteatoma

May be confined to the middle ear, growing into the facial recess or spreading anteriorly beneath the pars tensa. Managed by opening the facial recess. If doubt over clearance, close the TM and reopen after 12-18 months.

#### 5. Endoscopic Cholesteatoma Surgery

- Transcanal endoscopic approach as described above allows access to blind spots without external incisions
- Minimally invasive second-look surgery possible through 1 cm stab incision
- Otoendoscopy at primary surgery reduced residual disease from 47% to 5% (Thomassin)
- Endoscopy revealed 44% residual disease in epitympanum and 34% in retrotympanum overlooked by otomicroscopy (Ayache)

### Ossiculoplasty in Active Squamous COM

The incus is the most commonly used autologous prosthesis for ossicular reconstruction. There is theoretical risk that an incus from an ear with cholesteatoma may contain squamous cells, but no case of recurrent cholesteatoma arising from an allograft incus has been reported in any extensive surgical series.

### Complications of Surgery

| Complication | Incidence |
|-------------|-----------|
| Facial palsy | 1% (UK audit) |
| Dead ear | 2% (UK audit) |
| Persistent otorrhoea | 20-25% |

The incidence of facial palsy is widely accepted to be rare in the hands of expert surgeons.

### Problem Mastoid Cavities

Causes of ongoing discharge:
- High facial ridge
- Sump in cavity below floor of EAC
- Perforation in tympanic membrane
- Small external auditory meatus

Management options:
- **Revision surgery:** Meticulous correction of defects (89% clean and dry after revision - Jackson et al.)
- **Obliteration:** Bone pate, hydroxyapatite granules or posterior canal wall reconstruction; covered by vascularized fascioperiosteal flap; 80-95% dry ears

### Complications of Active COM

**Table: Relative Incidence of Complications**

| Extracranial (32%) | Intracranial (56%) |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| Postauricular abscess (75%) | Brain abscess (51%) |
| Facial palsy (6%) | Subdural abscess (20%) |
| Bezold abscess (2%) | Lateral sinus thrombosis (20%) |
| Petrous apicitis (0.2%) | Meningitis (12%) |
| | Extradural abscess (10%) |

Both squamous and mucosal disease carry significant risk of complications (41% squamous vs 59% mucosal in extracranial; 59% squamous vs 41% mucosal in intracranial).

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The microscope's straight-line view is its main limitation; the endoscope navigates around anatomical corners to provide variable direction and wide field of view.
> 2. The sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, protympanum and facial sinus recess are "blind spots" for the microscope but well visualized endoscopically.
> 3. Otoendoscopy at primary cholesteatoma surgery reduced residual disease rates from 47% to 5% (Thomassin).
> 4. Thermal injury is a real risk: 3 mm endoscopes produce heating equivalent to a 44 degrees C caloric stimulus at the LSCC.
> 5. Two schools of endoscope use: 2.7 mm adjunct endoscopes versus 3-4 mm EES (Endoscopic Ear Surgery) as the primary technique.
> 6. Active squamous COM (cholesteatoma) is classified as a retraction pocket with retained squamous debris, inflammation and pus production.
> 7. The terms "safe/unsafe" and "tubotympanic/atticoantral" are outdated.
> 8. The aims of cholesteatoma surgery are: disease eradication, self-cleaning ear, and hearing maintenance/improvement.
> 9. CWD has lower recurrence (5-15%) but creates a cavity; CWU has higher recurrence (20-50%) but preserves the EAC.
> 10. Long-term hearing outcomes show no significant difference between CWD and CWU (Toner and Smyth).
> 11. Non-echoplanar DW-MRI is very reliable at detecting cholesteatoma and can reduce the need for second-look surgery.
> 12. 1% facial palsy and 2% dead ear after mastoid surgery in the UK (lower in expert hands).


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. A crust in the attic area should always raise suspicion of cholesteatoma - wax only occurs there if pushed by a cotton bud.
> 2. Always assume a labyrinthine fistula is present and the facial nerve is dehiscent during cholesteatoma surgery to minimize complications.
> 3. Small cavity mastoidectomy (atticoantrostomy) produces a cavity of 1.4 cm3 vs 2.4 cm3 after modified radical mastoidectomy with the same disease clearance rate.
> 4. Hearing may be paradoxically preserved in established cholesteatoma because the sac bridges the ossicular chain gap; surgery may initially worsen hearing.
> 5. Three CCD cameras are essential for otoendoscopy - single CCD cameras are prone to "red-out" in the narrow, bloody surgical field.
> 6. The endoscopic approach to the facial recess takes minutes compared to the microscopic approach which requires cortical mastoidectomy and facial nerve identification.
> 7. For CT planning in endoscopic ear surgery, reconstruct images along the plane of the external auditory meatus rather than standard axial/coronal views.
