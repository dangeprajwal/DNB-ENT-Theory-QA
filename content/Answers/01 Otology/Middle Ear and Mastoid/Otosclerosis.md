## INTRODUCTION

Otosclerosis is a localized hereditary disorder of bone metabolism of otic capsule enchondral bone characterized by disordered resorption and deposition of bone. It results from increased and pathologic bone remodelling - the basic lesion consists of areas of bone resorption by osteoclasts and new bone formation by osteoblasts, accompanied by vascular proliferation and tissue stroma.

The condition is unique to humans and affects the otic capsule, which normally exhibits limited remodelling compared to the rest of the skeleton. The prevalence of clinical otosclerosis in the white population is approximately 2%, with histological otosclerosis found in 2.5-3.4% of unselected temporal bones.

---

## PART A: DEFINITIONS AND CLASSIFICATION

### Types of Otosclerosis

**Clinical Otosclerosis:** Lesions affecting the stapes, stapediovestibular joint, or round window membrane causing conductive hearing loss. In mixed hearing loss, lesions also affect the cochlear endosteum.

**Cochlear Otosclerosis:** Lesions involving the cochlear endosteum without affecting the stapes or stapediovestibular joint, causing pure sensorineural hearing loss. Considered very rare.

**Histological Otosclerosis:** Histopathological lesions that do not affect the stapes, stapediovestibular joint, or cochlear endosteum - remain asymptomatic during life. Prevalence estimated at 2.5-8.3%.

**Far Advanced Otosclerosis (FAO):** Average air-conduction over 85 dB with bone-conduction unmeasurable due to limits of audiometric equipment.

---

## PART B: HISTOPATHOLOGY

### Bone Remodelling in Otic Capsule

The otic capsule exhibits a gradient of decreased remodelling rate from periphery towards membranous labyrinth. Remodelling is related to the ratio of two cytokines:

- **OPG (Osteoprotegerin):** Potent inhibitor of osteoclast maturation
- **RANKL:** Activator of osteoclast RANK receptor

Impedance of this signalling pathway may lead to increased bone remodelling and development of abnormal otosclerotic pattern.

### Light and Electron Microscopy Findings

The otosclerotic process begins with:

- Resorption of enchondral bone around blood vessels
- Enlargement of perivascular spaces
- Deposition of immature (woven) bone
- Continuous remodelling to mature lamellar bone

**Active foci:** Increased vascularity and bone turnover

**Inactive (sclerotic) foci:** Dense mineralized bone

Both types may coexist in the same focus. The connective tissue stroma contains fibroblasts and osteocytes with complete absence of acute inflammatory cells.

### Sites of Involvement

Most common sites (in order of frequency):

- Cochlear wall anterior to oval window (most common)
- Round window niche
- Cochlear apex
- Foci posterior to oval window
- Walls of internal auditory canal
- Around cochlear duct
- Semicircular canals
- Entire footplate

Rare: Invasion of labyrinthine spaces. Not reported: Invasion of IAC or facial nerve canal.

## PART C: AETIOLOGY AND GENETICS

### Genetic Predisposition

Monogenetic otosclerosis loci identified:

- OTSC1 (chromosome 15q25-26)
- OTSC2 (chromosome 7q34-36)
- OTSC3 (chromosome 6p21.3-22.3)
- OTSC4 (chromosome 16q21-23.2)
- OTSC5 (chromosome 3q22-24)
- OTSC7 (chromosome 6q13-16.1)

**SERPINF1 gene:** First disease-associated gene identified in otosclerosis - encodes PEDF (pigment epithelium-derived factor), a regulator of bone density.

Complex otosclerosis (most common form) involves both genetic and environmental factors. Associated genes: COL1A1, TGFB1, RELN.

### Viral Infection Theory

Evidence supporting measles virus involvement:

- Histological similarity to Paget's disease
- Measles virus proteins in active otosclerotic lesions
- Increased CD46 isoforms in otosclerotic footplates
- Lower anti-measles IgG in virus-positive patients
- Decreased otosclerosis following vaccination programs

However, complete measles virus RNA sequence has never been reported and isolation of virus from otosclerotic samples has been unsuccessful.

### Other Aetiological Factors

- **Autoimmune disease:** Elevated antibodies against type II and IX collagen reported (conflicting evidence)
- **Cytokines:** TGF-beta1 and BMP involvement in pathological bone remodelling
- **Hormonal factors:** RAAS system may play a role (conflicting evidence)

## PART D: CLINICAL FEATURES

### Incidence and Demographics

- Clinical otosclerosis prevalence: ~2% in white population
- Histological otosclerosis: 2.5-3.4%
- Equal gender distribution for air-bone gap less than 30 dB
- Women 3x more likely when air-bone gap greater than 30 dB
- Incidence increases with age (4x higher in 60-80 years vs 18-40 years)
- Bilateral disease in ~75% of patients
- Extremely rare in Negroid races, uncommon in Mongoloid races

### Symptoms

**Conductive hearing loss:**

- Progressive hearing loss in early adulthood
- Classic pattern: greater at low frequencies
- Carhart notch at 2 kHz

**Sensorineural hearing loss:**

- May occur with cochlear endosteum involvement
- Related to spiral ligament hyalinization and stria vascularis atrophy

**Vestibular symptoms:**

- Present in 10-30% of patients
- Correlated with degree of sensorineural impairment
- May be caused by comorbid [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]]

**Other features:**

- Deterioration during pregnancy (50% bilateral, 25% unilateral cases)
- Schwartz sign (flamingo flush) - rarely seen

---

## PART E: DIAGNOSIS

### Otoscopy

- Normal, mobile tympanic membrane
- Schwartz sign (red blush over promontory) - rare

### Pure-Tone Audiometry

**Air-bone gap characteristics:**

- Greater at low frequencies
- Minimum 10 dB for diagnosis (traditionally 20 dB for surgery)
- Three-frequency average (0.5, 1, 2 kHz) or four-frequency average

**Carhart notch:**

- Depression at 2 kHz in bone-conduction
- Occurs in all conductive hearing loss (not specific to otosclerosis)
- Disappears after successful surgery
- Carhart effect averages 12 dB improvement over 0.5, 1, 2 kHz

### Tympanometry

- Compliance less than normal but range overlaps
- Does not generally aid diagnosis
- Useful to exclude [[OME Pathophysiology|otitis media with effusion]]

### Radiology

**HRCT findings:**

- Active otosclerosis: hypodense/lucent areas in otic capsule (typically anterior to oval window)
- Thickened stapes footplate
- Narrowing of oval and round window niches
- Double ring sign around cochlea
- Inactive phase: bone densities increase toward normal

**Sensitivity:** 70-90% (improving with better techniques)

**Indications for CT:**

- Diagnosis in doubt
- Early-age onset
- Associated vertigo
- [[Cochlear Implant|Cochlear implantation]] contemplated
- Pre-operative (obligatory in France)

**Cone beam CT:** Similar sensitivity with lower radiation doses

### Surgical Diagnosis

Gold standard - at surgery:

- Whiter bone around oval window
- No clear junction between otosclerotic and normal bone
- Stapes fixation confirmed after incudostapedial joint division
- Exclude congenital abnormalities, tympanosclerosis

## PART F: MANAGEMENT

### Medical Management

#### Sodium Fluoride

- Inhibits osteoclast activity, stabilizes bone turnover
- Increases calcium deposition in otospongiotic foci
- Danish RCT: 40 mg daily reduced hearing deterioration
- Typically 2 years treatment recommended
- Side effects: synovitis, GI disturbance, plantar fasciitis, anaemia
- Limited use in UK due to side effects and excellent surgical results

#### Bisphosphonates

- May reduce progressive sensorineural hearing loss
- Third-generation bisphosphonates showing promise
- Requires further investigation

### Hearing Aids

**Conventional aids:**

- Effective for conductive component
- Particularly effective as air-bone gap is being aided
- Modern aids very discreet
- Trial recommended before surgery

**Bone-anchored hearing aids ([[Bone Conduction MEI Devices|BAHA]]):**

- For only hearing ear with otosclerosis
- Post-fenestration cavity
- Avoids dead ear risk

**Direct Acoustic Cochlear Stimulation (DACS/Codacs):**

- For mixed hearing loss
- Indication: BC over 30 dB + air-bone gap over 30 dB
- Active middle ear implant attached to stapes prosthesis

### Surgical Management

#### Indications

- Air-bone gap minimum 10-20 dB
- Normal tympanic membrane
- Confirmed conductive hearing loss

#### Contraindications

- Active infection
- Pregnancy (relative)
- Only hearing ear (relative)
- Complete round window obliteration
- Meniere's disease (caution if BC less than 35 dB at 500 Hz)

#### Surgical Techniques

**Stapedotomy (most common):**

1. Tympanomeatal flap elevation
2. Bony annulus reduction
3. Incudostapedial joint division
4. Stapedius tendon division
5. Posterior crus division (laser or scissors)
6. Measurement: incus to footplate
7. Stapedotomy in posterior third of footplate
8. Prosthesis insertion (0.4-0.6 mm fenestra)
9. Tissue seal placement

**Stapedectomy:**

- Posterior third or whole footplate removed
- Vein graft covers defect
- Prosthesis placed on graft
- 19% still use this technique

**Prosthesis selection:**

- Length: 0.25 mm longer than incus-to-perilymph distance
- Usually 4.25-4.5 mm
- Options: Teflon piston, wire prosthesis, titanium

#### Surgical Challenges

**Anatomical hazards:**

- Dehiscent facial nerve (10%)
- Facial nerve overhanging oval window
- Persistent stapedial artery
- Narrow oval window niche

**Footplate problems:**

- Floating footplate (less than 1%)
- Obliterative otosclerosis
- Thick footplate

**Ossicular issues:**

- Malleus head fixation (1-10%)
- Incus displacement
- Incudomalleolar joint fixation

### Far Advanced Otosclerosis Management

**Definition:** AC over 85 dB, BC unmeasurable

**Options:**

1. **Stapedotomy + hearing aids:** Success in 46-82%, first-line treatment
2. **Cochlear implantation:** Best overall results, reserved for failures
3. **DACS device:** For mixed loss with significant air-bone gap

**Decision algorithm factors:**

- Speech discrimination less than 30% - consider primary CI
- Severe CT changes - consider primary CI
- Small air-bone gap - consider primary CI

**Cochlear implantation in otosclerosis:**

- ~10% of adult CI programs
- Results comparable to non-otosclerotic patients
- Pitfalls: round window obliteration, facial nerve stimulation, incomplete electrode placement

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Otosclerosis is a localized hereditary disorder of otic capsule bone metabolism with disordered resorption and deposition
> 2. Clinical otosclerosis prevalence is ~2% in whites; histological otosclerosis is 2.5-8.3%
> 3. Most common site is cochlear wall anterior to oval window
> 4. SERPINF1 is the first disease-associated gene identified
> 5. Carhart notch at 2 kHz is characteristic but not specific - occurs in all conductive hearing loss
> 6. Carhart effect averages 12 dB improvement after successful surgery
> 7. HRCT sensitivity is 70-90% for detecting otosclerotic foci
> 8. Stapedotomy is the preferred surgical technique with 0.4-0.6 mm fenestra
> 9. Facial nerve dehiscence occurs in ~10% of cases
> 10. Far advanced otosclerosis: AC over 85 dB with unmeasurable BC
> 11. Cochlear implantation achieves results comparable to non-otosclerotic patients
> 12. Sodium fluoride may slow progression but is limited by side effects


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Only 12% of histological otosclerosis cases develop stapes ankylosis (clinical disease)
> 2. The Schwartz sign (flamingo flush) is rarely seen despite being classically described
> 3. Deterioration during pregnancy occurs in 50% of bilateral cases - always ask in history
> 4. CT scanning is now obligatory before stapes surgery in France
> 5. Malleus fixation is easily missed - always check after incudostapedial joint division
> 6. For FAO, stapedotomy with hearing aids offers good first-line treatment before considering cochlear implantation
