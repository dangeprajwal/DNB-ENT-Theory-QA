## INTRODUCTION

Otosclerosis is a **localized disorder of bone remodelling exclusive to the otic capsule**, in which normal enchondral bone is replaced by a spongy vascular bone — a process called otospongiosis in its active phase. The fundamental lesion consists of foci of resorption by osteoclasts and new bone formation by osteoblasts, accompanied by vascular proliferation within a loose connective tissue stroma. Over time, these foci may mature into dense sclerotic bone. When these foci involve the stapes footplate and the stapediovestibular joint, progressive conductive hearing loss results. Involvement of the cochlear endosteum adds a sensorineural component.

The condition has fascinated clinicians and researchers for over a century. Valsalva first described it in 1704, but it was Politzer who characterized the pathology systematically in 1893. Today it remains a surgically treatable cause of progressive hearing loss with excellent outcomes when correctly managed.

---

## PART A: DEFINITIONS AND CLASSIFICATION

Understanding the distinction between histological and clinical otosclerosis is fundamental to interpreting the epidemiological data:

**Clinical Otosclerosis:** Lesions affecting the stapes, stapediovestibular joint, or round window membrane, causing conductive hearing loss. When lesions additionally involve the cochlear endosteum, a sensorineural component is added, producing mixed hearing loss. This is the form that presents clinically and requires treatment.

**Cochlear Otosclerosis:** A rare form in which lesions involve only the cochlear endosteum, causing pure sensorineural hearing loss without stapes fixation. Diagnosis is difficult, often made by exclusion, and HRCT may show the characteristic halo sign around the cochlea.

**Histological Otosclerosis:** Foci of pathological bone remodelling that do not affect the stapes, stapediovestibular joint, or cochlear endosteum, and therefore remain completely asymptomatic during life. These are discovered only at post-mortem temporal bone examination. Their prevalence is **2.5–8.3%** in temporal bone studies — far higher than clinical disease, indicating that most otosclerotic foci never progress to cause symptoms.

**Far Advanced Otosclerosis (FAO):** Defined as an average air-conduction threshold greater than **85 dB HL** with bone-conduction thresholds unmeasurable at the limits of audiometric equipment. This represents the most severe end of the clinical spectrum and requires special management considerations.

## PART B: HISTOPATHOLOGY

### I. Bone Remodelling in the Otic Capsule

The otic capsule is unique in the human body — it is the densest bone in the skeleton and, after formation, undergoes almost no physiological remodelling throughout life. This metabolic quiescence is maintained through a finely balanced system of cytokine signalling. Two molecules are pivotal:

**Osteoprotegerin (OPG)** is a potent inhibitor of osteoclast maturation. It acts as a decoy receptor for RANKL, preventing RANKL from binding to its receptor (RANK) on osteoclast precursors, thereby suppressing bone resorption.

**RANKL (Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB Ligand)** activates the RANK receptor on osteoclast precursors, driving their differentiation into mature bone-resorbing osteoclasts.

In otosclerosis, disruption of this OPG–RANKL balance — either from genetic susceptibility, viral triggers, or inflammatory cytokines — leads to inappropriate osteoclast activation in foci within the otic capsule. The normal bone remodelling that the rest of the skeleton performs continuously but the otic capsule resists becomes pathologically activated in these focal areas.

The otic capsule demonstrates a **gradient of decreasing remodelling rate from the periphery inward** toward the membranous labyrinth. Otosclerotic foci most commonly arise at the periphery — particularly at the **fissula ante fenestram**, a developmental cleft anterior to the oval window that represents a weak point in the enchondral bone layer.

---

### II. Microscopic Findings

#### A. Active (Otospongiotic) Phase

The active phase is characterized histologically by a loose, richly vascular connective tissue stroma containing proliferating blood vessels, fibroblasts, and activated osteoclasts and osteoblasts in a state of heightened turnover. The bone architecture in these foci shows:

- **Resorption of enchondral bone** around blood vessels, with enlargement of perivascular spaces
- **Deposition of woven (immature) bone** — randomly oriented collagen fibrils, lacking the organized lamellar structure of normal bone
- **Rich vascularity** — this increased vascularity in active foci is responsible for the clinical **Schwartz sign** (flamingo flush), a reddish blush visible through the TM over the promontory when foci are highly vascular. While classically described, it is rarely seen in clinical practice.
- **Absence of acute inflammatory cells** — a key distinguishing feature from infectious or inflammatory conditions

The connective tissue stroma in active foci contains fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and osteocytes in a loose matrix. This is the phase when sodium fluoride treatment has its maximum effect — inhibiting osteoclast activity and reducing the rate of bone turnover.

#### B. Inactive (Sclerotic) Phase

With maturation, the woven bone undergoes progressive remodelling toward lamellar bone through continuous remodelling cycles. In the inactive phase, the foci appear as areas of **dense, hypermineralized, lamellar bone** — the "sclerotic" appearance that gives the disease its name in the final form. Both active and inactive foci can coexist within the same patient and even within the same focus.

---

### III. Sites of Predilection

The distribution of otosclerotic foci is not random. The most common sites, in order of frequency:

1. **Anterior to the oval window (fissula ante fenestram)** — the single most common site (approximately 80% of clinical cases); fixation here causes classic stapedial otosclerosis
2. **Round window niche** — important because obliteration of the round window is a contraindication to stapes surgery (and an indication for cochlear implant over stapedotomy)
3. **Cochlear apex** — associated with cochlear (sensorineural) involvement
4. **Posterior to the oval window** — less common
5. **Walls of the internal auditory canal**
6. **Around the cochlear duct and scala media**
7. **Semicircular canals** — rare
8. **Entire stapes footplate** — "obliterative otosclerosis"

Importantly, invasion of labyrinthine spaces (perilymph or endolymph) has not been reported, and invasion of the facial nerve canal or IAC has not been described.

## PART C: AETIOLOGY AND GENETICS

### I. Genetic Architecture

Otosclerosis is an **oligogenic or complex genetic disease** — it has a clear heritable component but does not follow simple Mendelian inheritance in the majority of cases. There is strong familial clustering, with approximately 25% of patients reporting a first-degree relative with otosclerosis, but penetrance is incomplete (estimated at 25–40%).

**Monogenic otosclerosis loci** identified through linkage analysis in large affected families:

| Locus | Chromosome | Notes |
|-------|------------|-------|
| OTSC1 | 15q25–26 | First locus identified |
| OTSC2 | 7q34–36 | |
| OTSC3 | 6p21.3–22.3 | HLA region — immune implication |
| OTSC4 | 16q21–23.2 | |
| OTSC5 | 3q22–24 | |
| OTSC7 | 6q13–16.1 | |

**SERPINF1** encodes PEDF (Pigment Epithelium-Derived Factor), a regulator of bone density and vascularity. It was the **first disease-associated gene** identified in otosclerosis families. PEDF normally inhibits angiogenesis and promotes bone mineralization; its dysfunction may contribute to both the vascular proliferation and abnormal bone remodelling seen in otosclerotic foci.

**Complex otosclerosis** (the vast majority of clinical cases) involves multiple genetic variants acting together with environmental triggers. Key associated genes include:
- **COL1A1** — collagen type I alpha 1, encoding the major structural protein of bone matrix
- **TGFB1** — transforming growth factor beta 1, a cytokine that modulates osteoblast and osteoclast activity
- **RELN** — reelin, involved in cellular migration and bone development

---

### II. Viral Infection Theory — Measles Virus

Compelling evidence links the measles (rubeola) paramyxovirus to otosclerotic pathogenesis:

- Histological similarity between otosclerotic lesions and Paget's disease of bone — another condition linked to paramyxovirus
- Immunohistochemical detection of measles virus antigens in active otosclerotic foci
- Elevated CD46 isoform expression on otosclerotic footplate cells — CD46 is the cellular receptor for measles virus
- Reduced anti-measles IgG titres in patients with measles virus-positive lesions
- Epidemiological data showing a **decline in the incidence of clinical otosclerosis following widespread measles vaccination programs** — one of the strongest indirect lines of evidence

Despite this circumstantial evidence, complete measles viral RNA sequence has never been identified in otosclerotic tissue, and isolation of intact virus from affected specimens has not been achieved. The viral theory therefore remains plausible but unproven.

---

### III. Other Contributing Factors

**Autoimmune Mechanisms:** Elevated serum antibodies against type II and type IX collagen (components of the cochlear endosteum) have been reported in some series, suggesting an autoimmune element. However, the evidence is conflicting and not consistent across studies.

**Cytokines and Growth Factors:** TGF-β1 is overexpressed in otosclerotic tissue and promotes abnormal osteoblast activity. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are also implicated in the dysregulated bone remodelling.

**Hormonal Factors:** Clinical observations — particularly the well-documented deterioration of hearing during pregnancy — implicate hormonal influences. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) has been proposed as a modulator of otic capsule bone metabolism. Estrogen receptors have been identified in otosclerotic tissue. However, mechanistic evidence remains conflicting, and the precise hormonal pathway has not been established.

**Fluoride Deficiency:** Low dietary fluoride intake has historically been associated with increased otosclerosis prevalence, providing the rationale for sodium fluoride therapy.

## PART D: CLINICAL FEATURES

### I. Epidemiology

Clinical otosclerosis has a prevalence of approximately **2% in the white European population**. In contrast, histological otosclerosis (asymptomatic foci at autopsy) affects 2.5–8.3% of the same population — indicating that most foci never cause clinical disease. Only approximately 12% of individuals with histological otosclerosis develop stapes ankylosis (clinical disease).

Otosclerosis is extremely rare in people of Black African descent and uncommon in Asian (Mongoloid) populations. This racial variation has contributed to research into the genetic basis of the condition.

**Gender distribution** shows a more complex pattern than often stated. There is an equal gender distribution for mild degrees of hearing loss (air-bone gap less than 30 dB), but women are approximately **three times more likely** than men to develop more severe hearing loss (air-bone gap >30 dB). The precipitating effect of estrogen — via hormonal modulation of otic capsule bone metabolism — likely accounts for this difference.

**Age of onset** is typically in the second or third decade of life. The incidence increases approximately **fourfold** between the 18–40 age group and the 60–80 age group, meaning that while it presents in young adults, it also newly presents and progresses in the elderly.

**Bilateral disease** is present in approximately **75% of patients**, though often asymmetric in severity. Unilateral disease may eventually become bilateral in a proportion of patients.

---

### II. Symptoms

#### A. Conductive Hearing Loss

Progressive conductive hearing loss is the cardinal symptom, typically beginning insidiously in early adulthood. The patient or family may notice difficulty in conversation, increased television volume, or preference for noisy environments (paradoxical improvement in noisy conditions — **paracusis Willisii** — occurs because speakers unconsciously raise their voices in noise, benefiting the patient with conductive loss). The hearing loss is classically **greater at low frequencies** in the early stages, reflecting the impedance characteristics of a stiffened ossicular chain.

#### B. Tinnitus

Low-frequency tinnitus is common, reflecting the pattern of conductive loss. It may be the presenting complaint in some patients, particularly in the early stages before significant hearing loss is apparent.

#### C. Sensorineural Component

A sensorineural hearing loss component may develop as otosclerotic foci extend to involve the cochlear endosteum. The mechanism involves compression of the spiral ligament (resulting in stria vascularis dysfunction and endolymph composition changes), direct enzymatic damage to spiral ligament hyalinization, and mechanical distortion of the membranous labyrinth. The combined loss (mixed hearing loss) represents more advanced disease.

#### D. Vestibular Symptoms

Vestibular symptoms — including episodic dizziness and imbalance — occur in approximately **10–30% of patients** with otosclerosis. There is a correlation between the degree of sensorineural impairment and vestibular dysfunction, suggesting cochlear-vestibular involvement in the same pathological process. Some patients with otosclerosis may have concurrent **Menière's disease** — the combination requires careful assessment before stapes surgery, as the sensorineural component and hydrops affect the surgical risk-benefit calculation.

#### E. Pregnancy-Related Deterioration

**Deterioration during pregnancy** is a well-recognized clinical feature, occurring in approximately **50% of patients with bilateral disease** and 25% with unilateral disease. The mechanism likely involves increased levels of estrogen promoting osteoclast activation in susceptible otosclerotic foci. This history should always be specifically sought in female patients.

#### F. Schwartz Sign

The **Schwartz sign (flamingo flush)** is a reddish-pink blush visible through the tympanic membrane over the promontory, reflecting the increased vascularity of active otospongiotic foci. While pathognomonic when present, it is seen only rarely in clinical practice — the majority of patients have inactive or less vascular foci at the time of examination.

## PART E: DIAGNOSIS

### I. Otoscopy

The tympanic membrane is normal, mobile, and intact — this is invariable in pure otosclerosis. A normal tympanic membrane in the context of progressive conductive hearing loss with a negative middle ear should prompt consideration of otosclerosis (or other ossicular pathologies). The Schwartz sign, if present, is pathognomonic.

---

### II. Pure Tone Audiometry

Audiometry is the most important clinical investigation. The characteristic pattern includes:

**Air-Bone Gap:** The gap is predominantly at low frequencies in early disease, as stiffness-dominated ossicular fixation affects low-frequency transmission most severely. As the disease progresses, the air-bone gap extends across all frequencies. A minimum air-bone gap of **10 dB** (some centres use 20 dB) at one or more frequencies is traditionally required for surgical consideration.

**Carhart Notch:** A characteristic mechanical depression of bone-conduction thresholds at **2 kHz**, producing a notch in the bone-conduction audiogram that may make the hearing loss appear worse than the true sensorineural level. This is a **mechanical artefact** — it occurs because stapes fixation interferes with the inertial bone-conduction mechanism (which is normally enhanced by mass of the ossicular chain at 2 kHz). The Carhart notch is NOT specific to otosclerosis — it occurs in any condition causing stapes fixation — and it **disappears after successful stapes surgery**. Practically, the Carhart effect produces an average improvement of approximately **12 dB** at 0.5, 1, and 2 kHz after successful surgery, which should not be misinterpreted as true improvement in sensorineural threshold.

**Sensorineural Reserve:** Bone-conduction thresholds at frequencies unaffected by the Carhart effect (particularly 0.5 and 1 kHz, and 4 kHz) provide the best estimate of the true cochlear reserve and predict the post-operative bone-conduction outcome.

---

### III. Tympanometry

Tympanometry may show reduced compliance (shallow type A or As pattern), reflecting the increased stiffness of the ossicular chain. However, the compliance range in early otosclerosis overlaps considerably with normal, making tympanometry a supplementary rather than diagnostic test. Its primary clinical value is in **excluding otitis media with effusion** (type B flat tympanogram) as the cause of conductive hearing loss — an important differential diagnosis in children and young adults.

---

### IV. Acoustic Reflexes

Acoustic reflexes (stapedial reflexes) are absent in otosclerosis because the fixed stapes cannot contract in response to loud sound. This finding, combined with a flat tympanogram and conductive hearing loss, strongly supports the diagnosis. Early disease may show an "on-off" reflex pattern (a biphasic deflection without sustained contraction) as the stiffened stapes begins to fix.

---

### V. Radiological Diagnosis (HRCT)

High-resolution CT of the temporal bone is not required routinely for a classic clinical and audiometric presentation of otosclerosis, but it is an important investigation in specific situations.

**HRCT Findings in Otosclerosis:**

*Active phase:* The most consistent HRCT finding is a **hypodense (lucent) halo** around the cochlea — the "double ring sign" — representing the hypomineralized, vascular otospongiotic bone replacing the normally dense enchondral layer. A lucent area or "halo" anterior to the oval window (at the fissula ante fenestram site) is the classic finding. The stapes footplate may appear thickened. The oval window niche may appear narrowed.

*Inactive phase:* As otosclerotic bone matures toward dense lamellar bone, the hypodense areas may become isodense or even slightly sclerotic, making radiological detection in inactive disease unreliable.

**Sensitivity** of HRCT for active otosclerotic foci is approximately **70–90%**, improving with newer multi-detector CT and thin-slice (0.5 mm) acquisition protocols. **Cone beam CT** provides superior resolution with lower radiation exposure and is increasingly the modality of choice.

**Indications for CT in Otosclerosis:**
1. Diagnosis in doubt (atypical presentation, unusual audiogram)
2. Early age of onset (consider congenital ossicular anomaly or other pathology)
3. Associated vertigo (assess labyrinthine involvement, exclude other pathology)
4. Pre-cochlear implant assessment (assess round window, labyrinthine ossification, cochlear anatomy)
5. Assessment of round window obliteration (planning for surgical approach)
6. In France — CT before stapes surgery is now considered **obligatory** as standard of care

---

### VI. Surgical Diagnosis (Gold Standard)

The **definitive diagnosis** of otosclerosis is made at surgery, making it simultaneously the diagnostic and therapeutic procedure. Intraoperative findings confirming otosclerosis:

- **Whiter, less translucent bone** in the region of the anterior oval window (compared to normal pink bone of the promontory)
- **No clear demarcation** between otosclerotic bone and normal bone
- **Fixed stapes** confirmed by gentle palpation after incudostapedial joint division — the stapes footplate does not move when the superstructure is deflected
- **Absence of alternative diagnoses**: congenital ossicular anomaly, tympanosclerosis fixation, malleus head fixation — each excluded by systematic intraoperative assessment

**Malleus fixation** is an important and frequently missed diagnosis. After confirming stapes fixation, the malleus must be tested by palpation to exclude concurrent or isolated malleus head fixation in the epitympanum — present in 1–10% of cases. Failure to recognize malleus fixation will result in a suboptimal hearing outcome from stapes surgery alone.

## PART F: MANAGEMENT

### I. Medical Management

#### A. Sodium Fluoride

Sodium fluoride has been used for decades to slow the progression of otosclerosis, based on the principle that fluoride ions substitute for hydroxyl ions in hydroxyapatite, forming the more stable and harder fluorapatite — which is more resistant to osteoclastic resorption and slows the abnormal bone turnover of active otosclerosis.

A **Danish randomized controlled trial** demonstrated that sodium fluoride (40 mg daily) significantly reduced the rate of audiometric deterioration in patients with active otosclerosis over a 2-year treatment period. The standard course is typically **2 years of treatment** for active progressive disease.

Side effects include synovitis (joint pain), gastrointestinal disturbance, plantar fasciitis, and anaemia. These limit its acceptance and use — in the United Kingdom, sodium fluoride has limited use, partly due to its side-effect profile and partly because excellent surgical results mean surgery is often preferred.

#### B. Bisphosphonates

Bisphosphonates inhibit osteoclast activity by a different mechanism to fluoride and have shown promise in slowing the progressive sensorineural component of advanced otosclerosis. Third-generation bisphosphonates (particularly alendronate, risedronate) are under investigation. Their role in preventing cochlear involvement and cochlear otosclerosis progression is being studied but is not yet established in standard practice.

---

### II. Hearing Aids

#### A. Conventional Hearing Aids

Conventional behind-the-ear or in-the-canal hearing aids are effective for the conductive component of otosclerosis hearing loss and may be the patient's preferred choice or may be appropriate as a trial before surgery. Modern hearing aids are highly effective, cosmetically acceptable, and technologically sophisticated. Because the inner ear sensorineural reserve is often excellent in early otosclerosis, the aided benefit can be substantial.

A trial of hearing aids **before surgery** is recommended to allow patients to experience aided hearing and make an informed decision about surgical versus non-surgical management.

#### B. Bone-Anchored Hearing Aids (BAHA)

BAHA is indicated in specific situations:
- **Only hearing ear**: In a patient with otosclerosis in their only hearing ear, the risk of dead ear from stapes surgery, though small, may be unacceptable. BAHA avoids any risk to the only ear.
- **Post-fenestration cavity**: In patients who have previously undergone a fenestration procedure (older surgical technique) and developed a cavity.
- Patients who decline surgery or are medically unfit.

#### C. Direct Acoustic Cochlear Stimulator (DACS/Codacs)

For patients with **mixed hearing loss** from advanced otosclerosis — particularly where bone-conduction thresholds exceed 30 dB — conventional amplification may be insufficient because the sensorineural component limits the ceiling of amplification benefit. The **Codacs device** (Direct Acoustic Cochlear Stimulation) is an active middle ear implant attached to a modified stapes piston prosthesis. It directly drives the stapes footplate (or cochlear fluid via cochleostomy) with an actuator, achieving high-output acoustic stimulation of the cochlea.

**Indication:** Mixed hearing loss with BC threshold >30 dB and significant residual air-bone gap (>30 dB).

---

### III. Surgical Management

#### A. Indications

Surgery is indicated when:
- **Air-bone gap** of at least 10–20 dB at speech frequencies (0.5, 1, 2 kHz)
- **Normal tympanic membrane** (rules out middle ear effusion or TM perforation as cause)
- **Confirmed conductive hearing loss** — non-organic hearing loss must be excluded
- Patient preference for surgical over conservative management

#### B. Contraindications

| Contraindication | Type | Rationale |
|-----------------|------|-----------|
| Active middle ear infection | Absolute | Risk of labyrinthitis |
| Only hearing ear | Relative | Risk of dead ear (~1%) must be weighed |
| Complete round window obliteration | Relative | Sound transmission requires mobile RW; poor result expected |
| Menière's disease (BC <35 dB at 500 Hz) | Relative | Surgery may precipitate acute hydrops |
| Pregnancy | Relative | Avoid general anaesthesia if possible |
| Obliterative otosclerosis | Relative | Technically demanding; higher complication risk |

#### C. Stapedotomy — Current Gold Standard Technique

**Stapedotomy** (partial footplate removal with small fenestra and piston prosthesis) has replaced stapedectomy as the preferred technique in most centres due to lower risk of complications including labyrinthine damage and perilymph fistula.

The procedure is performed under local or general anaesthesia, using the operating microscope (and increasingly the otoendoscope for improved visualization):

**Step-by-Step Technique:**
1. **Tympanomeatal flap elevation**: Incision in the external auditory canal with elevation of a skin-tympanic membrane flap, entering the middle ear
2. **Middle ear inspection**: Assess ossicular chain, confirm stapes fixation, inspect facial nerve course, assess round window
3. **Bony annulus curettage**: If access is restricted, careful curettage of the posterosuperior bony canal wall (scutum) to improve visualization of the oval window
4. **Assessment for anomalies**: Check for persistent stapedial artery, dehiscent facial nerve, unusual ossicular anatomy
5. **Incudostapedial joint division**: The joint is divided sharply, separating the incus from the stapes
6. **Measurement**: Distance from the footplate surface to the undersurface of the incus long process is measured precisely — the prosthesis length is this measurement plus 0.25 mm (to allow the tip to sit 0.25 mm into the vestibule)
7. **Stapedius tendon division**: The tendon is divided with scissors or laser
8. **Posterior crus fracture/division**: The posterior crus is fractured or divided (laser or microinstruments) to mobilize the stapes superstructure
9. **Superstructure removal**: The mobilized superstructure is removed, leaving the fixed footplate in place
10. **Stapedotomy fenestra**: A precise fenestra of **0.4–0.6 mm diameter** is made in the posterior third of the footplate using a hand drill, microburr, or — most precisely — a **laser (CO₂ or KTP)**. The posterior third is preferred as it is furthest from the saccule (anteriorly) and facial nerve (superiorly)
11. **Tissue seal**: A small pledget of vein, perichondrium, or fat is placed around the fenestra to prevent perilymph leak
12. **Prosthesis placement**: The selected prosthesis is inserted through the fenestra into the vestibule and crimped around the long process of the incus

**Prosthesis Selection:**
- **Length**: 0.25 mm longer than the measured incus-to-footplate distance (standard 4.25–4.5 mm in most patients)
- **Diameter**: Matches the fenestra size (typically 0.4–0.6 mm)
- **Materials**: Teflon piston with wire (gold, platinum, nitinol), or all-titanium prostheses. Titanium prostheses are MRI-compatible, a significant advantage.

**Stapedectomy** — removal of the entire footplate — is now used by a minority of surgeons (approximately 19% according to surveys). A connective tissue graft (vein or fat) covers the open vestibule, and the prosthesis is placed on this graft. While effective, the larger opening carries slightly higher risk of perilymph fistula and sensorineural damage compared to the small fenestra of stapedotomy.

---

### IV. Surgical Challenges

The stapedotomy is technically demanding and the consequences of error can be permanent profound hearing loss. Challenges encountered include:

#### A. Anatomical Hazards

**Dehiscent Facial Nerve:** The facial nerve (horizontal tympanic segment) is dehiscent — lacking a bony canal — in approximately **10% of cases**. When dehiscent, it may overhang or even partially obscure the oval window, making visualization and access to the footplate difficult or dangerous. The nerve must be carefully identified before any instrument is introduced near the footplate.

**Overhanging Facial Nerve:** Even without frank dehiscence, a low-hanging facial nerve creates restricted access. Drill reduction of the posterior canal wall bony overhang (scutum) may improve access without endangering the nerve.

**Persistent Stapedial Artery (PSA):** Seen in approximately 0.02–0.05% of ears, a PSA runs across the middle ear and through or near the stapes — often through the obturator foramen. Division would cause significant hemorrhage, potentially with intracranial consequences (it connects to the middle meningeal artery). If discovered, surgery should be abandoned and the patient managed with hearing aids or BAHA.

**Narrow Oval Window Niche:** A narrow niche limits access for drilling and prosthesis placement. Careful microdissection and laser may improve safety in this anatomical variant.

---

#### B. Footplate Problems

**Floating Footplate:** Occurs in approximately **less than 1%** of cases when the footplate is inadvertently fully mobilized during superstructure removal or crus division. A floating footplate cannot be easily controlled and risks sinking into the vestibule. Management involves either proceeding directly to total footplate removal and sealing with graft, or — if accessible — retrieving and proceeding carefully with a small fenestra in the retrieved plate. Excessive manipulation risks sensorineural damage.

**Obliterative Otosclerosis:** In some patients, the otosclerotic focus extends to obliterate the entire oval window niche with a thick bony mass. The stapes may be entirely buried. This is technically the most challenging form, requiring careful drilling through the obliterative bone to reach the vestibule without injuring the facial nerve or causing sensorineural damage. Laser stapedotomy is particularly valuable here for precision.

**Thick Footplate:** Even without complete obliteration, a thickened footplate (>1 mm) requires more drilling or laser energy for fenestration than a standard footplate and increases the risk of fenestra-related complications.

---

#### C. Ossicular Problems

**Malleus Head Fixation:** Present in 1–10% of cases, often unsuspected pre-operatively. After stapes surgery, if the expected improvement does not occur, the surgeon must test the malleus for mobility. Atticotomy and malleus mobilization or head removal may be required — but this is ideally recognized and addressed at the primary operation.

**Incudostapedial Joint Fragility:** In some cases, the joint cartilage or the long process of the incus may be fragile or atrophic. Crimping the prosthesis to a fragile incus long process risks fracture, which is a devastating complication — requiring alternative ossicular reconstruction.

---

### V. Post-Operative Care and Outcomes

Most patients experience **immediate significant hearing improvement** upon waking from anaesthesia, as the prosthesis transmits sound as soon as it is placed. Full assessment should wait 6–8 weeks for ossicular healing and adaptation.

**Expected outcomes with stapedotomy** (from large series):
- Air-bone gap closure to ≤10 dB: approximately 85–90% of cases
- Success (ABG ≤20 dB): approximately 90–95%
- Dead ear (profound SNHL): approximately 1%
- Transient sensorineural shift: more common, usually resolves

**Carhart effect improvement:** An average of 12 dB across 0.5, 1, 2 kHz is recovered after successful surgery — representing return of the mechanical contribution to bone-conduction rather than true sensorineural improvement.

## PART G: FAR ADVANCED OTOSCLEROSIS

### I. Definition and Characteristics

Far Advanced Otosclerosis (FAO) is defined as average air-conduction thresholds greater than **85 dB HL** with bone-conduction thresholds unmeasurable at the limits of audiometric equipment (typically >60–70 dB HL). This represents end-stage disease where both the conductive and sensorineural components are severely compromised. The diagnosis was first systematically described by House and Sheehy in 1961 in the context of cochlear implantation.

The key questions in FAO management are: (1) Is there sufficient cochlear reserve for stapedotomy + hearing aids to achieve useful hearing? (2) Should primary cochlear implantation be considered? (3) Are there CT changes suggesting a difficult surgical field for CI?

---

### II. Management Options

#### A. Stapedotomy Combined with Hearing Aids

Stapedotomy can still achieve meaningful hearing improvement in FAO by closing the conductive component, after which a powerful hearing aid amplifies the improved signal. Success rates of **46–82%** have been reported in selected series, making this the **first-line treatment** in FAO. The combined approach is attractive because it avoids cochlear implant surgery (with its higher risk and commitment to CI rehabilitation), and if stapedotomy fails, CI remains available.

#### B. Cochlear Implantation

Cochlear implantation achieves the best overall hearing outcomes in FAO and is the definitive treatment when stapedotomy and hearing aids have failed or are unlikely to succeed. Approximately **10% of adult CI programs** in Europe deal with otosclerosis-related profound hearing loss.

**Outcomes:** CI results in FAO are **comparable to non-otosclerotic patients** — otosclerosis generally spares the cochlear hair cells and spiral ganglion neurons, preserving the neural substrate for electrical stimulation.

**Specific challenges of CI in otosclerosis:**
- **Round window obliteration**: The round window may be completely or partially obliterated by otosclerotic bone, making the conventional round window electrode insertion impossible. The surgeon must use a cochleostomy approach instead. Careful pre-operative CT assessment of round window patency is essential.
- **Facial nerve stimulation**: Otosclerotic bone adjacent to the electrode array may conduct electrical current to the nearby tympanic segment of the facial nerve, causing facial twitching with CI stimulation. This occurs in up to 10–15% of otosclerosis CI cases. Management involves reprogramming to deactivate the responsible electrodes; surgical re-exploration is rarely needed.
- **Incomplete electrode insertion**: When obliterative otosclerosis fills the cochlear lumen (particularly the scala tympani), full insertion may be impossible. Drill-out of the cochlea may be required but carries higher risk of sensorineural and nerve damage.

#### C. DACS Device

For patients with mixed hearing loss in FAO (residual air-bone gap >30 dB with significant sensorineural component), the DACS/Codacs device offers an intermediate option, directly driving the ossicular chain or cochlear fluid with high acoustic power.

---

### III. Decision Algorithm for FAO

The choice between stapedotomy + hearing aids versus primary CI is guided by:

| Factor | Favors Stapedotomy + HA | Favors Primary CI |
|--------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Speech discrimination score | >30% (any residual) | <30% (minimal benefit expected) |
| CT changes | Minimal oval window involvement | Extensive labyrinthine changes |
| Air-bone gap | Large (significant conductive component) | Small or absent |
| Round window | Patent | Obliterated |
| Patient preference | - | - |

**If speech discrimination is below 30%** at maximum comfortable levels, primary CI is generally recommended because even a successful stapedotomy is unlikely to produce adequate speech understanding. **Severe CT changes** suggesting extensive cochlear involvement may similarly favor primary CI.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Otosclerosis is a **localized hereditary disorder of otic capsule bone metabolism** — disordered resorption (osteoclasts) and deposition (osteoblasts)
> 2. Only **12% of histological otosclerosis** develops stapes ankylosis (clinical disease)
> 3. Prevalence: clinical ~2% in whites; histological 2.5–8.3%
> 4. **Most common site**: Anterior to oval window (fissula ante fenestram)
> 5. **SERPINF1**: First disease-associated gene identified in otosclerosis
> 6. Measles virus theory: supported by declining incidence post-vaccination, but virus never fully isolated
> 7. **Carhart notch at 2 kHz**: Mechanical artefact; occurs in ALL conductive losses from stapes fixation — not specific to otosclerosis
> 8. **Carhart effect** = 12 dB average improvement at 0.5, 1, 2 kHz after successful surgery
> 9. HRCT sensitivity 70–90%; **double-ring sign** (halo) around cochlea in active disease
> 10. **Stapedotomy** preferred over stapedectomy — 0.4–0.6 mm fenestra in posterior third of footplate
> 11. **Dehiscent facial nerve in ~10%** — most important surgical hazard
> 12. **Floating footplate (<1%)**: Most feared intraoperative complication
> 13. **Persistent stapedial artery**: Surgery must be abandoned if found
> 14. **FAO**: AC >85 dB, BC unmeasurable; stapedotomy + HA first-line; CI if speech discrimination <30%
> 15. CI in otosclerosis: Comparable outcomes to non-otosclerotic patients; watch for facial nerve stimulation and round window obliteration

---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Schwartz sign (flamingo flush) is classically described but **rarely seen** in practice
> 2. Deterioration during pregnancy in **50% bilateral, 25% unilateral** cases — always ask in history
> 3. CT before stapes surgery is now **obligatory in France** — standard of care
> 4. **Malleus fixation is easily missed** — always test malleus mobility after incudostapedial joint division
> 5. The **Carhart notch disappears** after successful surgery — do not interpret as sensorineural worsening
> 6. Paracusis Willisii (paradoxical improvement in noise) is characteristic of conductive hearing loss
> 7. For FAO with speech discrimination <30%, **primary CI** gives better outcomes than stapedotomy + HA
