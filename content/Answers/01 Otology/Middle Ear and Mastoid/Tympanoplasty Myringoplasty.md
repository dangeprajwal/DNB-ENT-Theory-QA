## INTRODUCTION

Myringoplasty is defined as the surgical repair of the tympanic membrane. Wullstein in 1956 classified tympanoplasty, with myringoplasty being classified as a Type I tympanoplasty (repair of the tympanic membrane without ossicular reconstruction). Tympanoplasty encompasses a broader scope of middle ear reconstruction including ossicular chain repair. Myringoplasty is one of the most commonly performed otological procedures, with quoted success rates of 60-99% in adults and 35-94% in children. The procedure has evolved significantly since Berthold's first description using a full-thickness skin graft in 1878, with temporalis fascia becoming the most widely used graft material since its introduction by Heerman in 1958.

---

## PART A: DEFINITION AND CLASSIFICATION OF TYMPANOPLASTY

Wullstein's classification of tympanoplasty (1956):

- **Type I (Myringoplasty):** Repair of the tympanic membrane alone, with intact ossicular chain
- **Type II:** Graft placed onto the incus or remnant of the malleus
- **Type III:** Graft placed directly onto the stapes superstructure (columella effect)
- **Type IV:** Graft placed onto the footplate with air pocket over the round window
- **Type V:** Graft covers the fenestration of the horizontal semicircular canal

In modern practice, Types IV and V are rarely performed. The focus of this answer is on Type I tympanoplasty (myringoplasty) and factors relevant to graft success.

## PART B: MIDDLE EAR RISK INDEX (MERI)

### Development and Purpose

The Middle Ear Risk Index (MERI) was devised by Kartush in 1994 and generates a numeric value corresponding to severity of disease and the likelihood of a successful outcome following surgery. It can be used to guide case selection and pre-operative patient counselling but is perhaps most useful for research purposes.

### Components

The MERI is an amalgamation of the Austin-Kartush classification of ossicular defects, the Belluci classification of otorrhoea, and additional risk factors. Originally scored 0-12, it was modified in 2001 to include smoking and increase the weighting for granulation and [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]], giving a score of **0-16**.

| Otologic Factor | Maximum Score |
|-----------------|--------------|
| Otorrhoea (Belluci classification) | 3 |
| Perforation | 1 |
| Cholesteatoma | 2 |
| Ossicular status | 4 |
| Middle ear granulation | 2 |
| Previous surgery | 2 |
| Smoking | 2 |
| **Total** | **16** |

**Belluci Classification of Otorrhoea:**

- Dry ear: 0
- Occasionally wet: 1
- Persistently wet: 2
- Persistently wet + cleft palate: 3

### Clinical Use

- Higher MERI scores indicate more severe disease and lower likelihood of successful outcome
- Useful for comparing surgical results between surgeons and institutions
- Allows stratification of patients for research purposes
- Helps in pre-operative counselling regarding expected outcomes

### Alternative Scoring Systems

- **OOPS Index** (Ossiculoplasty Outcome Parameter Staging)
- **SPITE Factors** (Surgical, Prosthetic, Infection, Tissue, Eustachian tube)

## PART C: GRAFT MATERIALS FOR MYRINGOPLASTY

### Autologous Materials

**1. Temporalis Fascia (Most widely used)**
- Success rates of 77-99% in adults and 35-94% in children
- Easily harvested via postauricular or endaural approach
- Abundant tissue available
- A separate scalp incision, hidden in the hairline, may be used during a permeatal approach

**2. Perichondrium**
- Harvested from tragus or concha
- Tragal perichondrium preferred by several surgeons due to ease of harvest, particularly for permeatal approach
- Long-term reliability demonstrated

**3. Cartilage (Gaining popularity)**
- Harvested from tragus or concha
- Composite graft from tragus provides good thickness and curvature
- Very low metabolic rate
- Now considered one of the best materials for larger perforations (>50%), adhesive otitis media, and revision surgery
- Keeping cartilage attached to perichondrium aids graft stabilization
- Quoted closure rates of up to 100%
- "Butterfly cartilage" technique for perforations <6 mm: cartilage disc circumferentially incised by 1 mm, groove engaged into perforation rim (94% success)
- Short-term hearing with cartilage is not as good as fascia, but results at 1 year are equivalent due to partial resorption of cartilage over time

**4. Other autologous materials:** Periosteum, subcutaneous tissue, fat (from lobule or postauricular region), fascia lata, vein graft

### Allogenic Materials

- **AlloDerm:** Processed human allograft skin, immunologically inert; success rate of 87.5%, equivalent to temporalis fascia
- Extracellular matrix (experimental)

### Xenogenic Materials

- Equine and bovine pericardium (inferior to temporalis fascia)
- Basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF) combined with atelocollagen: 92% closure in 87 patients

### Simple Techniques

- **Paper patch:** Thin cigarette paper onlay placed as outpatient procedure; reasonable success for small perforations; least successful for large perforations

## PART D: TEMPORALIS FASCIA - ANATOMY AND ADVANTAGES

### Anatomy

The temporalis fascia is a tough, fibrous layer that covers the temporalis muscle on the lateral aspect of the skull. It attaches superiorly to the superior temporal line of the skull and inferiorly to the zygomatic arch, splitting into superficial and deep layers near the arch. The fascia lies deep to the superficial temporal artery and auriculotemporal nerve, and superficial to the temporalis muscle.

**Surgical access:** The temporalis fascia is readily accessible through:

- **Postauricular approach:** Through the postauricular incision, the fascia is exposed by retracting the soft tissues superiorly
- **Endaural approach:** Fascia harvested through superior extension of the endaural incision
- **Separate scalp incision:** Hidden in the hairline for permeatal approaches

The graft is harvested as a thin, flat sheet and allowed to dry on a surface to become firm and easier to handle before placement.

### Advantages of Temporalis Fascia

1. **Abundance of tissue:** Large grafts can be harvested easily, sufficient for any size of perforation
2. **Ease of harvest:** Readily accessible through standard otological approaches without additional incisions
3. **Close proximity to operative field:** Available within the same surgical field
4. **Excellent biocompatibility:** Autologous tissue with no risk of rejection
5. **Good handling properties:** Dries to a firm, manageable consistency
6. **High success rates:** 77-99% closure rates in adults
7. **Low metabolic rate:** Stable graft with good long-term durability
8. **No additional morbidity:** Donor site heals without significant complications

## PART E: SENSORY INNERVATION OF THE EXTERNAL EAR

The sensory supply of the ear is highly complex. Four cranial nerves and two cervical nerves contribute to the sensory innervation.

### Auricle (Pinna)

| Nerve | Origin | Area Supplied |
|-------|--------|---------------|
| Greater auricular | Cervical plexus (C2, C3) | Medial surface and posterior portion of lateral surface |
| Lesser occipital | Cervical plexus (C2, C3) | Superior portion of medial surface |
| Auricular branch of vagus (Arnold's nerve) | Cranial nerve X | Concha and antihelix; some supply to eminentia conchae |
| Auriculotemporal | Mandibular division of trigeminal (Vc) | Tragus, crus of helix and adjacent helix |
| Facial nerve (VII) | Cranial nerve VII | Probably supplies small region in root of concha |

The greater auricular nerve prevails on both the lateral and medial surfaces, with extensive overlap between nerve territories.

### External Auditory Canal

The external auditory canal receives sensory innervation from:

- **Auriculotemporal nerve (Vc):** Supplies anterior meatal wall skin and epithelium of the outer surface of the tympanic membrane
- **Arnold's nerve (auricular branch of vagus X):** Supplies posteroinferior quadrant of the tympanic membrane and posteroinferior meatal skin; stimulation causes the Arnold's ear-cough reflex during microsuction
- **Glossopharyngeal nerve (IX):** Minor contribution via Jacobson's nerve to the medial surface of the tympanic membrane
- **Facial nerve (VII):** Contribution corresponds roughly with vagal territory but is sparse

## PART F: LOCAL ANAESTHESIA TECHNIQUE FOR TYMPANOPLASTY

Myringoplasty can frequently be performed under local anaesthesia. This is particularly valuable for patients with multiple medical comorbidities where general anaesthesia carries increased risk. Day-case surgery for tympanoplasty is increasingly the norm.

### Technique

**1. Pre-medication:**
- Oral or intravenous sedation may be administered
- Topical anaesthetic (EMLA cream or lignocaine spray) applied to the ear canal

**2. Local Infiltration Points:**

Based on the sensory innervation described above, the four-quadrant injection technique is used:

- **Postauricular injection:** Targets the greater auricular nerve (C2, C3) and posterior auricular nerve (VII); 2% lignocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline infiltrated in the postauricular sulcus
- **Superior meatal injection:** At the 12 o'clock position of the bony-cartilaginous junction of the ear canal, targeting the auriculotemporal nerve branches
- **Posterior meatal injection:** At the 6 o'clock position of the bony-cartilaginous junction, targeting Arnold's nerve (vagal branch)
- **Anterior meatal injection:** At the 3 o'clock position (left ear) or 9 o'clock (right ear), targeting the auriculotemporal nerve

**3. Canal Infiltration:**
- Injection at the bony-cartilaginous junction at multiple points (typically 4 quadrants)
- Creates a wheal that blanches the canal skin
- Adrenaline provides vasoconstriction, reducing bleeding and improving surgical field visibility

**4. Additional Points:**
- If using a postauricular approach, infiltration along the planned incision line
- Temporalis fascia donor site may require separate infiltration
- The injection should be slow and steady to minimize patient discomfort
- Wait at least 5-10 minutes for full anaesthetic effect before commencing surgery

### Advantages of Local Anaesthesia for Tympanoplasty

- Avoids risks of general anaesthesia
- Suitable for day-case surgery
- Allows intra-operative hearing assessment
- Reduces cost
- Particularly useful in patients with comorbidities

## PART G: FACTORS INFLUENCING GRAFT UPTAKE

### Patient Factors

**Age:** There is debate about the role of age. Some advocate avoiding surgery in younger children, awaiting Eustachian tube maturity (morphological changes most marked after age 7). Others suggest age does not affect surgical outcomes. Success rates of 35-94% are quoted in children, with reperforation rates in children under 15 at 18%, over double that in adults.

**Smoking:** Locally, nicotine causes cutaneous vasoconstriction and increases thrombosis risk. Carbon monoxide reduces the oxygen carrying capacity of blood. Regionally, chemical irritation leads to increased mucous viscosity, reduced mucociliary clearance, and Eustachian tube obstruction.

**General medical conditions:** Diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and poor nutrition lead to poor tissue oxygenation and impaired [[Wound Healing Phases Types Factors|wound healing]].

### Ear-Related Factors

**Perforation size:** Perforations greater than 25% cause greater conductive hearing loss and may have lower closure rates. Post-operative audiometric gain correlates with increasing size of the repaired perforation.

**Infection at surgery:** Controversy exists. Some studies show infection negatively influences outcomes with higher rates of graft failure, retraction pocket formation, and myringitis. However, most studies show that infection does not negatively influence outcomes, and most surgeons would not be deterred from operating in the presence of active infection.

**Eustachian tube function:** Severe Eustachian tube dysfunction reduces graft uptake. A normal contralateral ear is a positive predictor of success, suggesting favourable overall ET function. Abnormalities in the opposite ear predict poorer graft uptake.

**Site of perforation:** Most failures involve the anterior aspect of the graft, the most tenuous area due to relative hypovascularity. Graft margin must be anchored beneath the annulus anteriorly.

**Previous surgery:** Revision surgery has lower success rates. Long-term studies show success rates falling from approximately 85% at 1 year to 78% at 10 years.

### Surgical Factors

**Technique:** Underlay technique is the most commonly used. The graft is placed medial to the fibrous layer of the tympanic membrane, making it less likely to be displaced by external ear canal movement.

**Graft material:** No one graft material is conclusively superior. Cartilage is preferred for larger perforations (>50%), adhesive otitis media, and revision surgery due to superior resilience.

**Approach:** Permeatal, endaural, or postauricular, depending on perforation location and canal size.

### Additional Factors

- **Mastoid status:** Some evidence suggests mastoidectomy improves outcomes in non-cholesteatomatous chronic otitis media by creating an aerated reservoir; however, insufficient evidence exists to recommend routine mastoidectomy for uncomplicated perforations
- **Adenoidectomy:** Majority of reviews suggest prior adenoidectomy confers no benefit in myringoplasty success rates

## PART H: PAEDIATRIC TYMPANOPLASTY

### Special Considerations in Children

Paediatric tympanoplasty presents unique challenges due to several factors:

**1. Eustachian Tube Immaturity:** The paediatric Eustachian tube is shorter, more horizontal, and less compliant than in adults. Morphological changes accompanying improvement in function are most marked after the age of 7 years. Many surgeons prefer to defer surgery until this age unless the child has problematic symptoms.

**2. Indications for Early Surgery:** Problematic perforations in children (recurrent infections, marked hearing loss causing developmental delay) may warrant earlier operative intervention despite the age-related concerns.

**3. Success Rates:** The literature quotes success rates of 35-94% in children compared to 60-99% in adults. Reperforation rates in children under 15 years are approximately 18%, over double that in adults.

**4. Contralateral Ear:** The state of the contralateral ear is an important guide to overall Eustachian tube function. Bilateral disease suggests poor ET function and potentially lower graft uptake rates.

**5. Graft Choice:** Cartilage grafts are increasingly preferred in paediatric tympanoplasty due to their greater resilience against the negative middle ear pressures common in children with Eustachian tube dysfunction. Cartilage provides more structural support than fascia alone.

**6. Surgical Approach:** Endoscopic techniques are gaining popularity in paediatric tympanoplasty, offering advantages of minimal access surgery, avoidance of visible scars, and excellent visualization of the middle ear.

**7. Timing Considerations:**
- Most manage children conservatively while relatively asymptomatic, deferring surgery until ET maturation
- Children with recurrent infections or significant hearing loss may benefit from earlier intervention
- The state of the contralateral ear guides decision-making

**8. Combined Procedures:** In paediatric cholesteatoma, tympanoplasty is often combined with mastoidectomy (canal wall up or canal wall down) depending on disease extent.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. The MERI score is most useful for research purposes and comparing surgical outcomes between institutions rather than for individual patient decision-making
> 2. AlloDerm (processed human allograft) provides an alternative when temporalis fascia is unavailable, with equivalent success rates (87.5%)
> 3. No high-quality evidence supports the need to waterproof the ear in the presence of a perforation, though it remains common practice
> 4. Evidence does not support routine adenoidectomy prior to myringoplasty to improve success rates
> 5. Day-case tympanoplasty under local anaesthesia is increasingly the norm and should be particularly considered for patients with medical comorbidities
> 6. The butterfly cartilage technique (circumferentially incised by 1 mm) is a simple and effective option for small perforations less than 6 mm with 94% success rates
