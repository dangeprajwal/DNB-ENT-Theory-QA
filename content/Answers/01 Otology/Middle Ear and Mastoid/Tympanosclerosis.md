# Tympanosclerosis of the Middle Ear

> [!question] Questions
> Write short note on tympanosclerosis of the middle ear. Discuss its pathology, clinical features, and management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 83

---

## INTRODUCTION

Tympanosclerosis is a sequela of chronic otitis media characterized by hyaline deposits of acellular material visible as white plaques and nodular deposits in the tympanic membrane and middle ear. It represents healed chronic otitis media and is found in approximately 25% of ears at chronic otitis media surgery. While tympanic membrane tympanosclerosis is usually asymptomatic, involvement of the ossicular chain can cause significant conductive hearing loss.

---

## DEFINITION

Tympanosclerosis is the deposition of hyaline material in the lamina propria of the tympanic membrane and/or within the middle ear mucosa, resulting from previous inflammatory episodes. It is classified as "healed COM" in the modern classification of chronic otitis media.

---

## PATHOLOGY

### Histopathological Features

The characteristic finding is deposition of hyaline material in the lamina propria, located between the epidermis and mucosal epithelium. Macroscopically, this appears as white plaques and white nodular deposits. Microscopically, there are thickened collagen fibres (types I, II and III) admixed with calcium phosphate deposits. Over time, osseous metaplasia can occur.

### Sites of Involvement

The disease can affect multiple sites with varying clinical significance:

**Tympanic Membrane:** White plaques are visible on otoscopy. This is usually an incidental finding with no effect on hearing as the membrane remains mobile.

**Epitympanum (Attic):** Deposits can fix the malleus and incus heads, causing conductive hearing loss.

**Stapes Superstructure:** Causes varying degrees of immobility of the stapes.

**Oval Window:** Stapes footplate fixation leads to significant conductive loss.

**Promontory:** May be visible at surgery but usually not clinically significant.

---

## AETIOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS

Tympanosclerosis develops following middle ear inflammation and damage to the tympanic membrane. The pathogenic sequence involves:

1. Recurrent or chronic middle ear inflammation
2. Release of matrix metalloproteinases in middle ear effusion
3. Damage to tympanic membrane structure
4. Healing with hyaline deposition

**Risk Factors:**
- Chronic otitis media (present in ~25% at surgery)
- Ventilation tube insertion (higher prevalence in treated ears)
- Multiple ear infections causing cumulative inflammatory damage
- Previous ear surgery causing iatrogenic trauma
- [[OME Pathophysiology|Otitis media with effusion]] as a predisposing factor

---

## EPIDEMIOLOGY

Long-term studies by De Beer et al. followed 358 subjects and found:
- At 8 years: 92% prevalence of abnormalities
- At 18 years: 46% prevalence (indicating spontaneous improvement)
- Tympanosclerosis prevalence remained high in those treated with ventilation tubes

Tympanosclerosis is generally thought to occur less commonly in active squamous disease ([[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]]). It may partially resolve over time in some cases.

---

## CLINICAL FEATURES

### Symptoms

The presentation depends on the extent of disease:

**Hearing Loss:** Conductive type; severity depends on ossicular involvement. The air-bone gap can be up to 60 dB when the ossicular chain is significantly affected. Many cases are asymptomatic, discovered incidentally in elderly patients.

**Other Symptoms:** Usually absent unless there is coexisting perforation or other pathology.

### Factors Affecting Hearing

1. Size of perforation (if present)
2. Erosion of ossicular chain
3. Granulation tissue around ossicles
4. Tympanosclerosis around ossicular chain
5. Stapes fixation

### Examination Findings

**Otoscopy:** White plaques visible on the tympanic membrane. The pars tensa may show local or generalized opacification. Ossicles may be visible if the membrane is atrophic.

**Audiometry:** Conductive hearing loss with air-bone gap. The magnitude of the gap indicates severity of ossicular involvement. Mixed loss may occur if there is an associated sensorineural component.

---

## INVESTIGATIONS

**Pure-tone Audiometry:** Essential to assess the magnitude of conductive loss and determine candidacy for intervention.

**Microscopic Examination:** Assess extent of tympanosclerosis visible on the tympanic membrane.

**CT Temporal Bones:** Indicated if surgery is contemplated. Assesses ossicular chain involvement and helps surgical planning.

**Tympanometry:** Assesses middle ear compliance; may show reduced compliance with ossicular fixation.

---

## MANAGEMENT

### Conservative Management

**Hearing Aids:** Should always be considered as a treatment option. They are optimal for bilateral disease and avoid surgical risks. Contraindicated if the ear is actively discharging.

### Surgical Management

**Indications:**
- Significant hearing disability affecting quality of life
- Patient preference over hearing aids
- Bilateral disease with significant handicap

**Surgical Objectives:** The aim is reduction in hearing disability, not just air-bone gap closure. The better-hearing ear must be considered when assessing potential benefit. If successful closure would still leave the operated ear as the poorer hearing ear, benefit is dubious.

### Surgical Options

**When Ossicular Chain is Intact but Fixed:**

*Option 1 - Remove Incus and Malleus Head:* When only the incus and head of malleus are fixed in the attic, these can be removed and the ossicular chain reconstructed between the handle of malleus (or tympanic membrane) and stapes.

*Option 2 - Atticotomy with Mobilization:* The outer attic wall is removed and the malleus and incus are mobilized. The problem with this approach is the tendency for refixation by fibrous tissue or bone.

**When Stapes is Involved:**

*Stapedotomy:* The preferred technique for stapes footplate fixation.

*Stapes Mobilization:* Less predictable long-term results due to refixation.

### Surgical Outcomes

| Study | Outcome Measure | Results |
|-------|-----------------|---------|
| Giddings & House | Post-op ABG <20 dB | 72% (111/154 operations) |
| Vincent et al. | Post-op ABG <20 dB | 70% (45/64 operations) |

**Important:** Results in tympanosclerosis are NOT as good as in [[Otosclerosis|otosclerosis]] surgery.

### Complications

**Sensorineural Hearing Loss:**
- Albu et al.: 4% dead ear/SNHL rate
- Gormley: 5% SNHL (3/67 cases)

These complication rates are higher than in otosclerosis surgery.

**Other Complications:**
- Refixation (common after mobilization procedures)
- Persistent conductive loss
- Failed [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]]

---

## PROGNOSIS

**TM Tympanosclerosis Only:** Generally stable with no hearing impact.

**Attic Tympanosclerosis:** Guarded prognosis for surgical correction due to refixation risk.

**Stapes Involvement:** Less favourable outcomes than otosclerosis; higher complication rates.

**Extensive Disease:** Poor surgical outcome; hearing aid is preferable. End-stage pathology may be unreconstructable.

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Found in approximately 25% of ears at COM surgery
> 2. Hyaline deposits with calcium phosphate in lamina propria
> 3. White plaques on otoscopy are pathognomonic
> 4. Osseous metaplasia can occur over time
> 5. Air-bone gap up to 60 dB possible with ossicular involvement
> 6. [[Stapes Surgery|Stapes surgery]] achieves 70-72% success (ABG <20 dB)
> 7. SNHL complication rate 4-5% (higher than otosclerosis)
> 8. Refixation is common after mobilization procedures
> 9. Higher prevalence in patients with previous ventilation tubes
> 10. May partially resolve spontaneously over time
> 11. Hearing aids should always be considered
> 12. End-stage pathology may be unreconstructable


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - White plaques on TM are usually incidental and do not affect hearing
> - Always consider hearing aids before surgery - avoid unnecessary surgical risk
> - Results are inferior to otosclerosis surgery - counsel patients appropriately
> - Refixation is the major long-term problem after mobilization
> - Consider the better-hearing ear when planning surgery
> - CT scan essential for surgical planning
