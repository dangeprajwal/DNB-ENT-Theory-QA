## Part A: Overview of the Coagulation Cascade

Haemostasis is the physiological process that arrests bleeding following vascular injury. It proceeds through two overlapping phases -- primary haemostasis (formation of a platelet plug) and secondary haemostasis (stabilisation of that plug with fibrin) -- followed by regulated fibrinolysis once tissue repair is complete. Understanding each phase is essential for diagnosing bleeding disorders and for planning safe surgical intervention.

### Primary Haemostasis

Primary haemostasis is the immediate response to vascular injury and culminates in the formation of a temporary platelet plug. The process begins when endothelial disruption exposes subendothelial collagen and releases tissue factor into the surrounding blood. This triggers reflex vasoconstriction of the local vascular smooth muscle, mediated principally by endothelin-1 and thromboxane A2, which reduces blood flow to the injured site and limits early blood loss.

Circulating platelets then adhere to the exposed collagen through their glycoprotein Ib/IX/V surface receptor, using von Willebrand Factor (vWF) as a molecular bridge between the platelet and the collagen fibril. Once adherent, the platelets undergo activation: they change shape from discoid to spherical with pseudopod formation, degranulate (releasing ADP, thromboxane A2, serotonin, and calcium from their dense and alpha granules), and express phosphatidylserine on their outer membrane -- a critical step because this negatively charged phospholipid surface serves as the assembly platform for the coagulation cascade enzymes that follow.

Activated platelets then recruit additional platelets through ADP- and thromboxane-mediated signalling, and fibrinogen molecules bridge adjacent platelets by binding to glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptors on their surfaces. The result is a loosely cross-linked platelet aggregate -- the **primary haemostatic plug** -- at the site of injury. This plug is fragile and must be reinforced by the fibrin network generated by secondary haemostasis.

### Secondary Haemostasis (Coagulation Cascade) -- Key Principles

Secondary haemostasis reinforces the fragile platelet plug with a stable fibrin mesh through a series of enzymatic reactions. The coagulation cascade consists of sequential serine-protease reactions in which each inactive zymogen (proenzyme) is converted to its active enzyme form. Each activated factor in turn catalyses the activation of the next, producing a powerful amplification effect. Many of these reactions require calcium ions (Ca2+) and phospholipid membrane surfaces (provided by activated platelets) as obligate cofactors. The cascade is classically divided into three convergent pathways -- the intrinsic pathway, the extrinsic pathway, and the common pathway -- with the final product being a stable, cross-linked fibrin clot.

## Part B: Intrinsic Pathway of Coagulation (DNB 2021 Q37 -- Part 1)

### Definition and Physiological Basis

The intrinsic pathway is so named because every component required for its activation is present *intrinsic* to the circulating blood; no external tissue factor is needed. It is initiated by **contact activation** when blood is exposed to negatively charged surfaces such as subendothelial collagen, glass, or kaolin.

### The Intrinsic Cascade -- A Flowing Narrative

The cascade begins with the **contact activation phase**. When circulating Factor XII (Hageman factor) encounters an exposed negatively charged surface -- typically subendothelial collagen, but also glass or kaolin in laboratory settings -- it undergoes a conformational change and is converted to its active form, Factor XIIa. Two cofactors are essential for efficient contact activation: high-molecular-weight kininogen (HMWK), which serves as a non-enzymatic cofactor that anchors the reaction to the surface, and prekallikrein (Fletcher factor), which is converted to kallikrein by the newly formed Factor XIIa. Kallikrein, in turn, further activates additional Factor XII molecules, establishing an important positive feedback loop that amplifies the initial contact signal.

Factor XIIa next acts on Factor XI, which circulates in plasma as a homodimer bound to HMWK. The cleavage of Factor XI to Factor XIa occurs on the negatively charged surface and again requires HMWK as a cofactor. This surface-dependent reaction ensures that coagulation is localised to the site of vascular injury rather than propagating systemically.

Factor XIa then activates Factor IX (Christmas factor) through limited proteolysis. This reaction requires calcium ions and marks the first vitamin-K-dependent step in the intrinsic pathway, since Factor IX is a vitamin-K-dependent serine protease whose gamma-carboxylated glutamic acid residues are necessary for calcium-mediated membrane binding.

The activation of Factor X represents the critical rate-limiting step of the intrinsic pathway and requires the assembly of the **tenase complex** (also called the intrinsic tenase or Xase complex). Although Factor IXa alone can cleave Factor X, the reaction is extremely slow without cofactors. The full tenase complex consists of four components: Factor IXa (the enzyme), Factor VIIIa (a non-enzymatic cofactor), calcium ions (for membrane binding), and a phospholipid membrane surface (provided by the activated platelet surface). Together, these components accelerate Factor X activation by several orders of magnitude.

Factor VIII deserves special attention in this pathway. In its inactive form, Factor VIII circulates in plasma bound to von Willebrand Factor, which shields it from premature proteolytic degradation. Factor VIII is activated to Factor VIIIa by thrombin (Factor IIa) -- a critical positive feedback mechanism by which the end-product of the cascade amplifies its own generation. Deficiency of Factor VIII causes Haemophilia A, the most common severe inherited bleeding disorder (X-linked recessive inheritance), while deficiency of Factor IX causes Haemophilia B (Christmas disease), which is also X-linked recessive.

Once Factor Xa has been generated, the intrinsic pathway converges with the extrinsic pathway at the first step of the **common pathway**.

### Common Pathway

The common pathway is the final convergence point where both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways unite to generate the stable fibrin clot. It proceeds through three sequential steps.

First, Factor Xa forms the **prothrombinase complex** together with its non-enzymatic cofactor Factor Va, calcium ions, and the phospholipid surface of activated platelets. Factor Va itself is generated by thrombin-mediated cleavage of Factor V -- yet another positive feedback loop. This complex converts prothrombin (Factor II) to thrombin (Factor IIa), the central enzyme of the coagulation cascade.

Second, thrombin acts on fibrinogen (Factor I), cleaving fibrinopeptides A and B from the molecule to yield fibrin monomers. These monomers spontaneously polymerise end-to-end and side-to-side, forming a soluble fibrin polymer held together by hydrogen bonds.

Third, thrombin activates Factor XIII (fibrin-stabilising factor) to Factor XIIIa, a transglutaminase that introduces covalent gamma-glutamyl-epsilon-lysine cross-links between adjacent fibrin monomers. This converts the soluble fibrin polymer into an insoluble, mechanically stable fibrin clot that is resistant to premature fibrinolysis.

### Extrinsic Pathway (Brief Overview)

The extrinsic pathway provides the primary initiating signal for coagulation *in vivo* and is considerably faster than the intrinsic pathway because it involves fewer enzymatic steps. Tissue factor (TF, also called Factor III) is released from damaged subendothelial cells and binds to circulating Factor VII in plasma to form the TF--Factor VIIa complex. This complex directly activates Factor X, channelling the cascade into the common pathway. It also activates Factor IX (the Josso pathway), providing important cross-talk with the intrinsic system. The extrinsic pathway is measured clinically by the Prothrombin Time (PT).

### Thrombin as the Central Amplifier

Thrombin occupies a unique position in haemostasis because it drives multiple positive feedback loops that massively amplify clot formation:

1. It activates **Factor V to Factor Va**, the cofactor of the prothrombinase complex.
2. It activates **Factor VIII to Factor VIIIa**, the cofactor of the tenase complex.
3. It activates **Factor XI to Factor XIa**, amplifying the intrinsic pathway independent of Factor XII.
4. It activates **Factor XIII to Factor XIIIa**, enabling fibrin cross-linking.
5. It activates **platelets**, providing additional phospholipid surface for all membrane-dependent reactions.

### Natural Anticoagulant Mechanisms

The body counterbalances procoagulant activity through several endogenous anticoagulant systems. **Antithrombin III (AT III)** is the principal circulating inhibitor; it inactivates thrombin, Factor Xa, IXa, XIa, and XIIa, and its action is enhanced approximately 1000-fold by heparin. The **Protein C / Protein S system** provides another regulatory check: thrombomodulin, an endothelial surface receptor, binds thrombin and redirects its enzymatic activity toward Protein C activation; activated Protein C, with Protein S as its cofactor, then proteolytically inactivates Factors Va and VIIIa, dampening further thrombin generation. Finally, **Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI)** directly inhibits the TF--Factor VIIa complex and Factor Xa, regulating the initiation phase of coagulation.

## Part C: Evaluation of a Patient with Coagulation Disorder Prior to Surgery (DNB 2021 Q37 -- Part 2)

A systematic evaluation of any patient suspected of having a coagulation disorder before surgery rests on four pillars: a thorough clinical history, a focused physical examination, appropriate laboratory investigations, and correct interpretation of results. Of these, the clinical history is widely regarded as the single most important step.

### 1. Clinical History

#### A. Personal Bleeding History

The personal bleeding history should explore the following features, each of which carries specific diagnostic significance:

- **Easy or spontaneous bruising** suggests a platelet or vascular disorder, or an underlying factor deficiency.
- **Prolonged bleeding from cuts or wounds** (beyond what is expected for the wound size) indicates impaired haemostasis.
- **Recurrent [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]]** -- particularly when bilateral, prolonged beyond 20 minutes, or requiring nasal packing -- is one of the most common presenting symptoms in ENT practice and should raise suspicion for von Willebrand disease (vWD), a platelet disorder, or hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia.
- **Menorrhagia** is the most common symptom of vWD in women and should be specifically asked about.
- **Post-operative bleeding**, especially after dental extractions, [[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]], or adenoidectomy, is highly informative because the oral and nasopharyngeal mucosa possesses high fibrinolytic activity that stresses the haemostatic system.
- **Delayed post-traumatic bleeding** (bleeding that initially stops but recurs hours later) is characteristic of coagulation factor deficiency.
- **Gastrointestinal bleeding or haematuria** may indicate a systemic bleeding disorder.
- **Haemarthrosis** (spontaneous joint bleeding) **or deep muscle haematomas** are hallmarks of severe factor deficiency, particularly Haemophilia A or B.
- **Intracranial haemorrhage** represents the most serious complication of severe bleeding disorders and should be enquired about in the family history as well.

#### B. Family History

A detailed family history should document bleeding disorders in relatives and any consanguinity (which increases the risk of autosomal recessive conditions). The pattern of inheritance often points toward the diagnosis:

- **X-linked recessive** inheritance (males affected, females carriers) is characteristic of Haemophilia A and Haemophilia B.
- **Autosomal dominant** inheritance is seen in most types of von Willebrand disease and Factor V Leiden.
- **Autosomal recessive** inheritance is typical of the rare factor deficiencies.

#### C. Drug History

A meticulous drug history is essential because many commonly prescribed and over-the-counter medications impair haemostasis:

| Drug Category | Examples | Effect |
|--------------|----------|--------|
| Anticoagulants | Warfarin, heparin (UFH/LMWH), DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) | Impairs coagulation cascade |
| Antiplatelets | Aspirin, clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, dipyridamole | Impairs platelet function |
| NSAIDs | Ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen | Reversible COX inhibition; impairs platelet function |
| SSRIs | Fluoxetine, sertraline | Reduce platelet serotonin; increase bleeding risk |
| Herbal supplements | Ginkgo biloba, garlic, ginger, fish oil, vitamin E | Impair platelet function |
| Antibiotics | Broad-spectrum (reduce vitamin K-producing gut flora) | Impair vitamin K-dependent factor synthesis |

#### D. Relevant Medical Conditions

Several systemic conditions predispose to coagulopathy and must be identified pre-operatively:

- **Liver disease** impairs synthesis of virtually all coagulation factors (with the exception of Factor VIII and vWF, which are produced by endothelial cells).
- **Renal failure** produces uraemic platelet dysfunction.
- **Malignancy** may cause coagulopathy through bone marrow infiltration or by triggering disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
- **DIC** itself is a consumptive coagulopathy that depletes clotting factors and platelets simultaneously.
- **Vitamin K deficiency**, whether from malabsorption, obstructive jaundice, or prolonged antibiotic use, selectively impairs the vitamin-K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X).
- **Autoimmune disorders** can produce acquired factor inhibitors or immune thrombocytopenia.
- **Hypothyroidism** is an under-recognised cause of acquired von Willebrand disease.
- **Myeloproliferative disorders** may cause qualitative platelet dysfunction despite normal or elevated platelet counts.

### 2. Clinical Examination

Physical examination should specifically look for signs that help distinguish the type and severity of the bleeding disorder:

- **Petechiae** (pinpoint, non-blanching lesions <2 mm) indicate a defect in primary haemostasis -- either a platelet disorder or a vascular disorder.
- **Purpura** (2 mm -- 1 cm) similarly points to a platelet or vascular defect.
- **Ecchymoses** (bruises >1 cm) may be seen with either platelet or factor disorders but tend to be larger and deeper with factor deficiencies.
- **Haemarthrosis** (swollen, warm, painful joints) is virtually pathognomonic of severe factor deficiency (Haemophilia A or B).
- **Hepatosplenomegaly** suggests liver disease (impaired factor synthesis), hypersplenism (thrombocytopenia), or a myeloproliferative disorder.
- **Jaundice** may indicate liver disease or obstructive jaundice with consequent vitamin K malabsorption.
- **Telangiectasia** on the skin, mucous membranes, lips, or tongue points to Hereditary Haemorrhagic Telangiectasia (Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome).
- **Mucosal bleeding** (gingival, nasal) is typical of platelet or vWF disorders.
- **Lymphadenopathy** may suggest an underlying haematological malignancy.

#### Distinguishing Platelet/Vascular Disorders from Coagulation Factor Disorders

| Feature | Platelet/Vascular Disorder | Coagulation Factor Disorder |
|---------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Bleeding pattern | Mucocutaneous (skin, mucous membranes) | Deep tissue (joints, muscles) |
| Petechiae | Present | Absent |
| Ecchymoses | Small, superficial | Large, deep |
| Bleeding onset | Immediate after injury | Delayed (hours after injury) |
| Bleeding after cuts | Prolonged | May stop initially, then restart |
| Post-surgical bleeding | Immediate | Delayed |
| Response to local pressure | Effective | Often ineffective |
| Common examples | ITP, vWD, aspirin effect | Haemophilia A, Haemophilia B |

### 3. Laboratory Investigations

#### First-Line (Screening) Tests

The initial laboratory workup for any patient with suspected coagulopathy should include a full blood count with platelet count (to detect thrombocytopenia, anaemia, or leucocyte abnormalities suggesting marrow pathology), an APTT (to screen the intrinsic and common pathways for deficiencies of Factors XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, or fibrinogen), a PT/INR (to screen the extrinsic and common pathways, particularly Factor VII), a thrombin time (to assess fibrinogen function -- prolonged in DIC, fibrinolysis, heparin contamination, and dysfibrinogenaemia), a fibrinogen level (quantitative assessment; low in DIC and liver disease, elevated as an acute-phase reactant), and a peripheral blood smear (to evaluate platelet morphology and detect schistocytes suggestive of DIC or thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura).

#### Second-Line (Specific) Tests

When screening tests are abnormal or when clinical suspicion is high despite normal screening, more targeted investigations are warranted:

- **Bleeding time or PFA-100** assesses platelet function in vivo and in vitro and is useful for detecting von Willebrand disease and platelet function disorders.
- **Mixing studies** (1:1 mixing of patient plasma with normal plasma) are essential for distinguishing factor deficiency from a factor inhibitor: if the prolonged APTT or PT corrects with mixing, a factor deficiency is present; if it does not correct, an inhibitor (antibody) is responsible.
- **Specific factor assays** measure individual factor levels and are used to confirm and quantify deficiencies. In Haemophilia A, Factor VIII levels below 1% indicate severe disease, 1--5% moderate, and 5--40% mild.
- **vWF antigen level** quantifies circulating vWF (reduced in Types 1 and 3 vWD).
- **vWF activity** (ristocetin cofactor assay) measures functional vWF activity and is abnormal in all types of vWD.
- **vWF multimer analysis** determines the pattern of vWF multimers and is critical for diagnosing Type 2A and 2B vWD (which show loss of high-molecular-weight multimers).
- **Platelet aggregation studies** test platelet response to various agonists (ADP, collagen, arachidonic acid, ristocetin) and diagnose conditions such as Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard-Soulier syndrome, and storage pool disorders.
- **D-dimer and fibrin degradation products (FDPs)** are elevated in DIC, active thrombosis, and ongoing fibrinolysis.
- **Lupus anticoagulant and anti-cardiolipin antibodies** identify antiphospholipid syndrome, which characteristically prolongs the APTT but paradoxically causes a thrombotic (not bleeding) tendency.
- **Thromboelastography (TEG) or ROTEM** provides a global point-of-care assessment of haemostatic function and is invaluable for guiding blood product replacement intra-operatively.

#### Interpretation Algorithm

The pattern of screening test abnormalities guides further workup:

- **Prolonged APTT with normal PT** indicates an intrinsic pathway defect. Mixing studies that correct suggest a factor deficiency (VIII, IX, XI, or XII); failure to correct suggests an inhibitor (acquired haemophilia or lupus anticoagulant).
- **Normal APTT with prolonged PT** points to an extrinsic pathway defect, most commonly Factor VII deficiency, early warfarin effect, or early liver disease.
- **Both APTT and PT prolonged** indicates either a common pathway defect or multiple factor deficiencies. Causes include DIC, severe liver disease, late warfarin effect, vitamin K deficiency, and deficiencies of Factors X, V, II, or fibrinogen.
- **Normal APTT and PT with clinical bleeding** should prompt investigation for platelet function disorders (vWD, Glanzmann thrombasthenia, Bernard-Soulier syndrome), Factor XIII deficiency (which is not detected by either APTT or PT), mild factor deficiencies (APTT may remain normal if factor levels exceed 30%), vascular disorders, or alpha-2 antiplasmin deficiency (a fibrinolytic disorder).

## Part D: Pre-operative Preparation of a Patient with Coagulation Disorder (DNB 2021 Q37 -- Part 3)

### General Principles

Pre-operative preparation of a patient with a known or suspected coagulation disorder demands a structured, multidisciplinary approach. The core principles are as follows:

1. **Multidisciplinary team involvement** -- the surgeon, haematologist, anaesthetist, and blood bank/transfusion medicine specialist should all contribute to the management plan.
2. **Establish the specific diagnosis** before surgery -- a label of "abnormal coagulation" is insufficient; the precise deficiency or disorder must be identified.
3. **Optimise haemostatic parameters** before any operative intervention by correcting factor levels, platelet counts, or anticoagulant effects as appropriate.
4. **Plan factor replacement and blood products well in advance**, ensuring that specific concentrates, reversal agents, and laboratory support are available on the day of surgery.
5. **Assess the bleeding risk of the planned procedure** -- mucosal surgery (e.g. tonsillectomy, septoplasty, FESS) carries a higher fibrinolytic risk than non-mucosal surgery.
6. **Perform a risk-benefit analysis** -- particularly when modifying anticoagulation, the surgical bleeding risk must be weighed against the thrombotic risk of withholding anticoagulants.
7. **Document a written management plan** that covers the pre-operative, intra-operative, and post-operative periods.

### Condition-Specific Preparation

#### A. Haemophilia A (Factor VIII Deficiency)

For patients with Haemophilia A undergoing surgery, the pre-operative target is a Factor VIII level of 80--100% for major procedures. Replacement is achieved with recombinant Factor VIII concentrate (preferred) or plasma-derived Factor VIII. As a dosing guide, 1 unit/kg of Factor VIII raises the plasma level by approximately 2%. The pre-operative bolus should be administered 30--60 minutes before surgery, and the level should be confirmed before incision. Post-operatively, Factor VIII must be maintained above 50% for 7--14 days after major surgery, using either continuous infusion or bolus dosing every 8--12 hours (the half-life of Factor VIII is approximately 12 hours).

In patients with mild Haemophilia A (baseline Factor VIII >5%), **desmopressin (DDAVP)** may be used as first-line therapy. DDAVP raises Factor VIII levels 3--5 fold by stimulating release from endothelial Weibel-Palade bodies. The dose is 0.3 microg/kg IV, and a trial dose before surgery is recommended to confirm an adequate response.

**Tranexamic acid** (an antifibrinolytic agent) should be given as adjunctive therapy at a dose of 1 g three times daily orally or 10--15 mg/kg IV. It is especially important in mucosal surgery (oral, nasal, or pharyngeal procedures) where local fibrinolytic activity is high.

For patients with Factor VIII inhibitors (alloantibodies), standard factor replacement is ineffective; bypassing agents such as activated prothrombin complex concentrate (FEIBA/aPCC) or recombinant Factor VIIa (NovoSeven) must be used instead.

#### B. Haemophilia B (Factor IX Deficiency / Christmas Disease)

The pre-operative target for Haemophilia B is likewise a Factor IX level of 80--100% for major surgery. Replacement uses recombinant or plasma-derived Factor IX concentrate. Importantly, 1 unit/kg of Factor IX raises the plasma level by only approximately 1% (owing to its larger volume of distribution compared to Factor VIII), so higher doses are required. Post-operatively, Factor IX should be maintained above 50% for 7--14 days, with bolus dosing every 18--24 hours (half-life of Factor IX is approximately 24 hours). DDAVP is **not effective** in Haemophilia B.

#### C. von Willebrand Disease (vWD)

Management depends on the type and severity of vWD. For all types, the surgical target is a vWF activity level above 50%, and aspirin and NSAIDs must be avoided.

**Type 1 vWD** (mild quantitative deficiency; the most common type, ~70% of cases) responds well to DDAVP as first-line treatment, which stimulates release of vWF from endothelial stores. A trial dose is recommended pre-operatively. Tranexamic acid should be given as an adjunct.

**Type 2 vWD** (qualitative defects) requires more nuanced management. Types 2A and 2M may respond partially to DDAVP, but the response is often inadequate and vWF-containing Factor VIII concentrates (e.g. Humate-P, Wilate) are usually required. In **Type 2B**, DDAVP is **contraindicated** because it causes release of abnormal vWF that binds excessively to platelets, triggering thrombocytopenia; vWF-containing Factor VIII concentrates are the treatment of choice.

**Type 3 vWD** (severe, complete absence of vWF) does not respond to DDAVP at all because there are no endothelial vWF stores to release. vWF-containing Factor VIII concentrates are mandatory; cryoprecipitate should only be used if concentrates are unavailable.

#### D. Patients on Warfarin

Managing warfarin peri-operatively requires careful balancing of bleeding and thrombotic risk. The first step is to **assess the patient's thrombotic risk**: patients with mechanical heart valves, recent DVT/PE (within 3 months), or atrial fibrillation with a high CHADS2 score are at high thrombotic risk and may require bridging anticoagulation with therapeutic LMWH. Warfarin should be **stopped 5 days before surgery** to allow the INR to normalise. In high-risk patients, therapeutic LMWH is started 3 days after stopping warfarin, with the last dose given 24 hours pre-operatively (prophylactic dose) or 12 hours pre-operatively.

On the day of surgery, the **INR must be checked** and surgery should proceed only if the INR is less than 1.5. For urgent or emergency surgery when the INR remains elevated, reversal options include vitamin K (phytomenadione) 5--10 mg IV (which takes 6--12 hours for full effect), fresh frozen plasma (FFP) at 15 mL/kg (immediate but short-lived), or -- preferably -- prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) at 25--50 units/kg (faster onset, smaller volume, and more predictable response than FFP). Warfarin is resumed post-operatively once haemostasis is secure, typically 12--24 hours after low-bleeding-risk procedures.

#### E. Patients on Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

| DOAC | Half-life | Pre-operative Cessation | Reversal Agent |
|------|-----------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Dabigatran** (direct thrombin inhibitor) | 12--17 h | 24--48 h (48--72 h if CrCl <50 mL/min) | **Idarucizumab** (Praxbind) 5 g IV |
| **Rivaroxaban** (Factor Xa inhibitor) | 5--13 h | 24--48 h | **Andexanet alfa** (if available); PCC |
| **Apixaban** (Factor Xa inhibitor) | 8--15 h | 24--48 h | **Andexanet alfa**; PCC |
| **Edoxaban** (Factor Xa inhibitor) | 10--14 h | 24--48 h | PCC (non-specific) |

Several important principles apply to all DOACs. No routine coagulation monitoring is required, although anti-Xa levels or a dilute thrombin time can be used when needed. Renal function assessment is critical -- especially for dabigatran, which is 80% renally excreted and requires a longer cessation interval if creatinine clearance is below 50 mL/min. For emergency surgery with significant bleeding, the specific reversal agent (idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa for rivaroxaban or apixaban where available) should be used, supplemented by PCC and tranexamic acid. Bridging with heparin is generally not required for DOACs because of their short half-lives.

#### F. Patients on Antiplatelet Therapy

| Drug | Mechanism | Pre-operative Cessation | Special Considerations |
|------|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **Aspirin** | Irreversible COX-1 inhibition | 7--10 days | If cardiac stent <12 months (DES) or <6 weeks (BMS): consult cardiologist |
| **Clopidogrel** | Irreversible P2Y12 ADP receptor inhibitor | 7 days | Dual antiplatelet: high thrombotic risk if stopped |
| **Prasugrel** | Irreversible P2Y12 inhibitor | 7 days | More potent than clopidogrel |
| **Ticagrelor** | Reversible P2Y12 inhibitor | 5 days | Reversible binding; shorter offset |
| **Dipyridamole** | Phosphodiesterase inhibitor | 24 hours | -- |

Platelet transfusion may be considered in emergency situations when the antiplatelet effect must be overcome immediately. In patients with recent coronary stent placement, any modification of antiplatelet therapy must be undertaken in close consultation with the patient's cardiologist because of the potentially catastrophic risk of stent thrombosis.

#### G. Thrombocytopenia

The approach to surgery in a thrombocytopenic patient depends on the platelet count and the nature of the procedure:

| Platelet Count | Management |
|----------------|------------|
| >100,000/microL | Generally safe for all surgery |
| 50,000--100,000/microL | Safe for most surgery; platelet transfusion for neurosurgery or ophthalmic surgery |
| <50,000/microL | Platelet transfusion required before surgery (target >50,000) |
| <10,000--20,000/microL | Spontaneous bleeding risk; urgent platelet transfusion |

In addition to platelet transfusion, the underlying cause should be investigated and treated. In immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) are first-line therapies. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia requires withdrawal of the offending agent. In DIC, the underlying trigger must be addressed. Each unit of platelet concentrate raises the count by approximately 5,000--10,000/microL. For patients with refractory thrombocytopenia, HLA-matched platelets or thrombopoietin receptor agonists (eltrombopag, romiplostim) may be necessary.

#### H. Liver Disease

Patients with liver disease present a complex coagulopathy because the liver synthesises virtually all coagulation factors (except Factor VIII and vWF). The approach involves multiple interventions:

- **Vitamin K** (10 mg IV for 3 days) may correct the coagulopathy if hepatic synthetic function is sufficiently preserved, but will be ineffective in severe parenchymal disease.
- **FFP** (15 mL/kg) provides immediate but transient correction of all factor levels. PCC is used for more urgent correction.
- **Cryoprecipitate** should be given if the fibrinogen level is below 1 g/L (cryoprecipitate is rich in fibrinogen, Factor VIII, vWF, and Factor XIII).
- **Platelet transfusion** is required if thrombocytopenia (from portal hypertension and hypersplenism) drops the count below 50,000/microL.
- **Thromboelastography (TEG/ROTEM)**-guided transfusion is particularly valuable in liver disease because it provides a global picture of haemostatic function and allows targeted blood-product replacement.

#### I. Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

The cornerstone of DIC management is treatment of the underlying cause (sepsis, malignancy, obstetric complication, or massive trauma). Supportive measures include FFP for prolonged PT/APTT with active bleeding, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen falls below 1 g/L, platelet transfusion if the count drops below 50,000/microL with active bleeding, and cautious use of tranexamic acid (with awareness of the risk of microvascular thrombosis). **Elective surgery should be postponed** until DIC has been fully resolved.

### Intra-operative Haemostatic Measures

Meticulous surgical technique is the foundation of intra-operative haemostasis. Bipolar diathermy is preferred over monopolar for its precision, and careful tissue handling with avoidance of blind clamping minimises unnecessary bleeding. Topical haemostatic agents -- including Surgicel (oxidised regenerated cellulose), Gelfoam (absorbable gelatin sponge), bone wax (for bony bleeding), fibrin glue/sealant, and topical thrombin -- should be readily available.

Tranexamic acid may be given intravenously (1 g bolus followed by 1 g over 8 hours) or applied topically on soaked gauze for nasal or oral surgery. Maintaining physiological conditions is critical: core temperature should be kept above 36 degrees C (hypothermia impairs both coagulation enzyme function and platelet activity), tissue perfusion should be maintained with adequate blood pressure, and pH should remain above 7.2 (acidosis below this level significantly impairs coagulation). Cell salvage should be considered for procedures where major blood loss (>500 mL) is anticipated, and point-of-care testing with TEG/ROTEM allows real-time, goal-directed blood product replacement.

### Post-operative Management

Post-operative care focuses on maintaining adequate haemostasis while monitoring for delayed bleeding. Factor replacement should continue according to the haematology plan, typically for 7--14 days after major surgery. Oral tranexamic acid (1 g three times daily for 5--7 days) is an important adjunct, particularly after mucosal surgery such as tonsillectomy, septoplasty, FESS, or oral procedures.

Regular monitoring should include APTT/PT, specific factor levels (in haemophilia patients), and platelet counts, supplemented by careful clinical assessment for signs of delayed haemorrhage. Vigilance for secondary haemorrhage is essential -- this typically occurs 5--10 days after tonsillectomy and may indicate inadequate factor replacement or premature cessation of therapy.

The timing of anticoagulant resumption depends on the balance between surgical bleeding risk and thrombotic risk: for low-bleeding-risk surgery, anticoagulants may be resumed 12--24 hours post-operatively, while high-bleeding-risk surgery warrants a delay of 48--72 hours. Resumption should always be decided in consultation with the haematologist and/or cardiologist. Patients should be counselled to avoid trauma to the surgical site, to follow a soft diet after oral or pharyngeal surgery, to avoid nose blowing after nasal surgery, and to strictly avoid aspirin, NSAIDs, or other antiplatelet/anticoagulant medications unless specifically prescribed.

## ENT-Specific Relevance

Coagulation assessment is of particular importance in ENT practice for several reasons:

1. **Tonsillectomy** is one of the most common procedures associated with post-operative haemorrhage (secondary haemorrhage rate 2--4%), and the high fibrinolytic activity of the oropharyngeal mucosa makes it an especially demanding test of haemostatic integrity.
2. **Epistaxis** is frequently the presenting symptom of an underlying bleeding disorder; recurrent bilateral epistaxis in a child should always prompt investigation for von Willebrand disease.
3. **FESS** ([[FESS Steps Complications|Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery]]) involves dissection in close proximity to the orbit and skull base, where any coagulopathy increases the risk of serious complications.
4. **Adenoidectomy** involves nasopharyngeal mucosal surgery with inherently high fibrinolytic risk.
5. **Head and neck cancer surgery** involves extensive, prolonged procedures with significant potential for major blood loss.
6. **Middle ear surgery** requires a bloodless field for adequate visualisation of critical anatomical structures; even minor haemorrhage can significantly impair the surgical view.
7. **Pre-operative screening** -- a structured bleeding history has been shown to be more sensitive than routine coagulation screening in detecting clinically significant bleeding disorders.

## Key Points for DNB Exam

1. The intrinsic pathway is activated by **contact activation** (Factor XII on negatively charged surfaces) and involves Factors XII, XI, IX, and VIII.
2. The **tenase complex** (IXa + VIIIa + Ca2+ + phospholipid) is the rate-limiting step of the intrinsic pathway.
3. APTT measures the **intrinsic + common pathway**; PT measures the **extrinsic + common pathway**.
4. **Thrombin** is the central amplifier of coagulation through multiple positive feedback mechanisms.
5. **Mixing studies** differentiate factor deficiency (corrects) from inhibitor (does not correct).
6. A structured **bleeding history** is more sensitive than routine laboratory screening.
7. **Multidisciplinary approach** (haematologist + surgeon + anaesthetist) is essential for managing surgical patients with coagulation disorders.
8. **Factor replacement targets:** 80--100% pre-operatively, >50% maintenance for 7--14 days post-operatively for major surgery.
9. **DDAVP** is first-line for mild Haemophilia A and Type 1 vWD; contraindicated in Type 2B vWD.
10. **Tranexamic acid** is an essential adjunct in all mucosal ENT surgery in patients with bleeding disorders.
11. Warfarin: stop 5 days pre-op, check INR <1.5; DOACs: stop 24--48 hours (renal function dependent).
12. Antiplatelet cessation requires **cardiology consultation** if recent coronary stent placement.
