## PART A: Approach to the Endolymphatic Sac (Endolymphatic Sac Decompression/Surgery)

Endolymphatic sac surgery is a non-destructive, hearing-preservation procedure designed to decompress the endolymphatic system and thereby reduce endolymphatic hydrops. It is the least invasive of the surgical options available for Meniere's disease and carries the lowest morbidity among surgical interventions for this condition. The procedure was originally described by Portmann and subsequently modified by Paparella and Shea, with the goal of draining or decompressing the endolymphatic system to alleviate episodic vertigo.

### Indications

- **Intractable Meniere's disease:** The patient must have failed an adequate trial of medical management, typically defined as a minimum of six months of conservative treatment including dietary salt restriction, diuretic therapy, and betahistine administration.
- **Preserved serviceable hearing:** Because this is a non-destructive procedure, it is specifically chosen when the affected ear retains useful hearing that warrants preservation.
- **Disabling episodic vertigo:** The vertigo episodes must be sufficiently severe to significantly impair the patient's quality of life, typically corresponding to AAO-HNS functional levels 4 through 6 despite ongoing medical therapy.
- **Aim of surgery:** The fundamental goal is drainage or decompression of the endolymphatic system to reduce endolymphatic hydrops and thereby control vertiginous episodes.

### Preoperative Evaluation

A thorough preoperative workup is essential before proceeding with endolymphatic sac surgery. Audiometry must confirm the presence of serviceable hearing, with both pure-tone audiometry and speech discrimination scores documented. Vestibular function testing, including caloric testing and vestibular evoked myogenic potentials, provides baseline data and helps lateralize the disease. MRI of the brain with gadolinium enhancement is mandatory to exclude retrocochlear pathology, particularly [[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]], which can mimic Meniere's disease. CT of the temporal bone is obtained to assess mastoid pneumatization and define the relevant surgical anatomy, including the position of the sigmoid sinus and the degree of mastoid cellularity.

### Surgical Steps

The endolymphatic sac approach is performed through a standard post-auricular [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]] corridor, with identification of the sac guided by a series of well-defined anatomical landmarks. The procedure proceeds through seven sequential steps.

1. **Positioning and incision.** The patient is placed supine under general anaesthesia with the head turned to the opposite side. A standard post-auricular skin incision is made approximately 2 to 3 cm behind the post-auricular crease, extending from the mastoid tip inferiorly to the level of the temporal line superiorly.

2. **Periosteal flap elevation.** An anteriorly-based periosteal (Palva) flap is elevated from the mastoid cortex, and soft tissues are retracted anteriorly to expose the mastoid cortex widely. This flap will be used for coverage during closure.

3. **Cortical mastoidectomy.** The mastoid cortex is saucerized using a large cutting burr, and a complete cortical (simple) mastoidectomy is performed. Critical landmarks are identified in a systematic fashion. The tegmen tympani is identified superiorly as a thin plate of bone forming the roof. The sigmoid sinus is followed posteriorly until the blue-line of the sinus becomes visible, establishing the posterior limit. The lateral semicircular canal (LSCC) is identified at the depth of the antrum as a smooth bony prominence at the level of the fossa incudis; this is the key landmark. The posterior semicircular canal (PSCC) is then identified by drilling inferiorly and medially to the LSCC.

4. **Identification of critical landmarks for the endolymphatic sac.** Several anatomical landmarks must be identified to locate the endolymphatic sac safely. The sigmoid sinus forms the posterior boundary, as the sac lies anteromedial to it. The posterior fossa dura lies deep to the sac, which rests on its anterior surface medial to the sigmoid sinus. The lateral SCC provides a key reference point, since the sac lies below this level. The posterior SCC must be identified to avoid injury, as the sac lies posterior and inferior to it. Trautmann's triangle, bounded by the tegmen, sigmoid sinus, and superior petrosal sinus, indicates the general region; the sac is located at the inferior margin of this triangle. The single most important surgical landmark is **Donaldson's line** — an imaginary line drawn along the long axis of the lateral semicircular canal, extending posteriorly to bisect the posterior semicircular canal. The endolymphatic sac lies inferior and posterior to this line.

5. **Identification of the endolymphatic sac.** Bone is drilled in the area between the sigmoid sinus posteriorly and the posterior SCC anteriorly, exposing the posterior fossa dura in this retrolabyrinthine region. The endolymphatic sac is identified on the posterior fossa dura by its characteristic thickened, opaque, whitish appearance, which contrasts distinctly with the thin, translucent, normal surrounding dura. It is located anteromedial to the sigmoid sinus. The sac has two anatomical portions: a lateral (extraosseous) portion lying on the posterior fossa dura, and a medial (intraosseous) portion situated within the vestibular aqueduct.

6. **Decompression and drainage.** Several surgical techniques exist, ranging from least to most invasive. Simple decompression involves bone removal only, widely exposing the sac by removing overlying bone without opening it. Sac incision (marsupialization) involves incising the lateral wall of the sac to allow endolymph drainage into the mastoid cavity. An endolymphatic-mastoid shunt involves inserting a silastic tube or sheet into the sac lumen, draining into the mastoid cavity to maintain long-term patency. An endolymphatic-subarachnoid shunt involves inserting a silastic tube from the sac lumen through the posterior fossa dura into the subarachnoid space. The most commonly performed technique is sac decompression with endolymphatic-mastoid shunt placement, in which a small T-shaped or rectangular silastic sheet is inserted into the opened sac lumen with the other end positioned in the mastoid cavity.

7. **Closure.** The anteriorly-based periosteal (Palva) flap is replaced over the mastoid defect. Subcutaneous tissue and skin are closed in layers, and a mastoid pressure dressing is applied for 24 to 48 hours.

### Complications

The complications of endolymphatic sac surgery are relatively few, reflecting the conservative nature of the procedure. Sensorineural hearing loss occurs in 1 to 5 percent of cases and results from inadvertent trauma to the labyrinth or cochlea during drilling near the posterior semicircular canal. CSF leak may occur if the posterior fossa dura is inadvertently breached during bone removal around the sac. Wound infection is a standard surgical risk common to all mastoid procedures. Recurrent or persistent vertigo represents the most frequent unfavourable outcome; the procedure may not fully control vertiginous episodes, with reported vertigo control rates in the literature ranging from 60 to 80 percent (notably, the Danish Sham Surgery Trial raised controversy regarding the efficacy of this procedure compared to placebo). Injury to the posterior semicircular canal can occur if landmarks are not correctly identified, potentially resulting in vestibular symptoms or hearing loss. Sigmoid sinus injury produces venous bleeding, which is controlled with Surgicel packing or bone wax application.

### Outcomes

Vertigo control rates are reported at approximately 60 to 80 percent in the literature, though considerable variability exists and the true efficacy remains debated. Hearing preservation is excellent, with greater than 90 percent of patients maintaining their preoperative hearing levels. Overall morbidity is low, making this the safest surgical option for Meniere's disease.

## PART B: Labyrinthectomy

Labyrinthectomy is an ablative, destructive surgical procedure that permanently and irreversibly destroys both vestibular and cochlear function in the operated ear. It achieves the highest vertigo control rate of any surgical procedure for Meniere's disease (greater than 95 percent) but at the cost of complete and permanent hearing loss. The procedure is therefore reserved exclusively for ears in which hearing is already non-serviceable.

### Indications

- **Intractable disabling vertigo with non-serviceable hearing:** The primary indication is an ear with complete or profound sensorineural hearing loss in which no useful hearing remains to preserve.
- **Failed prior conservative management:** This includes failure of adequate medical management as well as failure of prior conservative procedures such as intratympanic gentamicin injection or endolymphatic sac surgery.
- **Most commonly indicated in Meniere's disease:** It is the definitive surgical option for patients with unilateral vestibular disease and a dead or profoundly impaired ear.
- **Contraindicated in the only-hearing ear or bilateral disease with serviceable hearing:** Because the procedure destroys all residual hearing, it must never be performed on the patient's only-hearing ear.

### Types of Labyrinthectomy

Three types of labyrinthectomy are described. Transmastoid labyrinthectomy is the standard surgical approach, performed via a cortical mastoidectomy, and is the most common and complete technique. Transcanal labyrinthectomy is performed via the external auditory canal and is less extensive, used in limited cases but carries a higher risk of incomplete neuroepithelial removal. Chemical labyrinthectomy involves intratympanic gentamicin injection, exploiting the preferential vestibulotoxicity of aminoglycosides to achieve selective vestibular ablation; this non-surgical alternative preserves some hearing in many cases but carries a risk of sensorineural hearing loss.

### Transmastoid Labyrinthectomy — Surgical Steps

The transmastoid labyrinthectomy is a systematic procedure requiring meticulous identification and opening of all three semicircular canals, wide exposure of the vestibule, and complete removal of all five vestibular neuroepithelial end-organs. The following nine steps describe the standard technique.

1. **Positioning and incision.** The patient is placed supine under general anaesthesia with the head turned away from the operative side. A standard post-auricular incision is made, and an anteriorly-based periosteal flap is elevated.

2. **Cortical mastoidectomy.** A complete cortical mastoidectomy is performed, identical to that described for endolymphatic sac surgery. The tegmen tympani, sigmoid sinus, sinodural angle (Citelli's angle), antrum, lateral SCC, and incus short process with the fossa incudis are all systematically identified.

3. **Identify all three semicircular canals.** The lateral (horizontal) semicircular canal is the most superficial and is identified first, appearing as a smooth bony eminence in the medial wall of the antrum at the level of the fossa incudis and antral floor. The posterior semicircular canal is the most inferior canal, identified by drilling inferiorly and medially, deep to the mastoid segment of the facial nerve; it runs in a nearly vertical orientation. The superior (anterior) semicircular canal is the most superior, identified by drilling superiorly under the tegmen; it shares a common crus with the posterior canal. The three canals converge centrally at the vestibule.

4. **Open all three semicircular canals.** Using a diamond burr for controlled drilling, the lumen (blue-line) of each semicircular canal is systematically opened. The LSCC is opened first as it is most accessible. The PSCC is opened next by following it inferiorly. The SSCC is opened last by following it superiorly. The membranous labyrinth becomes visible within each bony canal as a thin membrane.

5. **Identify and open the vestibule.** The vestibule is the central chamber where all three semicircular canals converge. It lies medial to the oval window niche and lateral to the fundus of the internal auditory canal. The vestibule is opened widely by removing the bone between the three SCC openings. It contains the utricle (connected to the semicircular canals) and the saccule (connected to the cochlea via the ductus reuniens).

6. **Remove neuroepithelium.** This is the critical step for achieving complete vestibular ablation. Using micro-picks, micro-hooks, and fine suction, the surgeon removes the utricular macula from the utricle, the saccular macula from the saccule, and the crista ampullaris from the ampulla of each of the three semicircular canals. A total of five neuroepithelial end-organs must be removed: the crista of the lateral SCC (from the ampulla of the LSCC), the crista of the posterior SCC (from the ampulla of the PSCC), the crista of the superior SCC (from the ampulla of the SSCC), the utricular macula (from the elliptical recess of the vestibule), and the saccular macula (from the spherical recess of the vestibule).

7. **Ensure completeness.** All vestibular neuroepithelium must be meticulously removed for complete vestibular ablation. Incomplete removal leads to persistent vertigo and failure of the procedure. The cochlear duct and organ of Corti are also disrupted during this process, and complete cochlear destruction is an accepted consequence.

8. **Pack the vestibule.** The vestibule and semicircular canal openings are packed with small pieces of temporalis fascia and/or muscle. This step is critical to prevent CSF leak through the vestibule, because the fundus of the IAC communicates with the vestibule via the cribriform area through which nerve fibres pass. Bone wax may be applied around the edges for additional seal.

9. **Closure.** The periosteal flap is replaced over the mastoid defect. The wound is closed in layers (subcutaneous tissue, then skin), and a mastoid pressure dressing is applied.

### Post-operative Course

The postoperative course following labyrinthectomy follows a predictable pattern determined by the acute loss of unilateral vestibular function and the subsequent process of central vestibular compensation. In the immediate postoperative period, patients experience severe rotational vertigo and nausea lasting 2 to 5 days, accompanied by spontaneous nystagmus beating away from the operated side (towards the intact labyrinth), which reflects the acute vestibular asymmetry. Gradual central vestibular compensation occurs over 4 to 6 weeks, during which the brainstem recalibrates to the new unilateral vestibular input. Vestibular rehabilitation is essential during this period; Cawthorne-Cooksey exercises and customized vestibular rehabilitation therapy (VRT) accelerate the compensation process and improve functional outcomes. Permanent complete hearing loss in the operated ear is an expected and accepted outcome. Most patients achieve functional compensation by 6 to 8 weeks and can return to normal activities.

### Outcomes

Vertigo control following labyrinthectomy exceeds 95 percent, making it the most effective surgical procedure for Meniere's disease in terms of vertigo elimination. Morbidity is low apart from the irreversible hearing loss that is inherent to the procedure. It remains the most definitive surgical treatment available for unilateral vestibular disease when hearing is already non-serviceable.

## PART C: Translabyrinthine Approach to the Internal Auditory Canal (IAC)

The translabyrinthine approach is a lateral skull base surgical technique that provides direct access to the internal auditory canal and cerebellopontine angle by removing the labyrinthine structures to create a surgical corridor. Its principal advantage is that it permits early identification of the facial nerve at the IAC fundus before the tumour is encountered in the CPA, and it achieves this access without requiring any cerebellar retraction. The approach necessarily destroys all residual hearing in the operated ear and is therefore preferred when hearing is already non-serviceable or not worth preserving.

### Indications

- **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma):** This is the most common indication, and the approach is suitable for tumours of any size — small, medium, or large.
- **Other CPA/IAC pathology:** Meningioma, epidermoid cyst, and facial nerve tumours within the IAC and CPA are also amenable to this approach.
- **Non-serviceable hearing:** The approach is preferred when hearing in the affected ear is already lost or so severely impaired that preservation is not a realistic goal.
- **Any tumour size:** Unlike the middle fossa approach, which is limited to small intracanalicular tumours (less than 1.5 cm CPA extension), the translabyrinthine approach accommodates any tumour size.

### Advantages over Other Approaches

The translabyrinthine approach offers several distinctive advantages compared to alternative skull base approaches. It requires no cerebellar retraction whatsoever, in contrast to the retrosigmoid (suboccipital) approach where cerebellar retraction is mandatory and can cause postoperative headache and cerebellar oedema. The approach provides excellent access to the IAC fundus, allowing the surgeon to identify the facial nerve early in its anatomically predictable location anterior to Bill's bar; this contrasts with the retrosigmoid approach, where the facial nerve is identified late at the brainstem surface after tumour dissection has already begun, and where the fundus is poorly accessible, risking residual tumour. The translabyrinthine approach is suitable for any tumour size, unlike the middle fossa approach, which is restricted to small intracanalicular tumours. The postoperative headache rate is significantly lower than with the retrosigmoid approach. The principal disadvantage is that hearing is inevitably destroyed. Both the retrosigmoid and middle fossa approaches offer the possibility of hearing preservation, making them preferable when serviceable hearing exists.

### Surgical Steps

The translabyrinthine approach is the most complex of the three procedures described in this answer, involving complete mastoidectomy, labyrinthectomy, 180-degree skeletonization of the IAC, and ultimately tumour removal from the CPA. The following nine steps describe the standard technique.

1. **Patient positioning.** The patient is placed supine under general anaesthesia with continuous facial nerve monitoring via intraoperative EMG, with electrodes placed in orbicularis oculi and orbicularis oris. The head is turned to the opposite side on a horseshoe headrest or Mayfield clamp. Hair is shaved 3 to 4 cm behind the ear. A lumbar drain may be placed preoperatively to allow CSF drainage and brain relaxation during the intradural phase of the procedure.

2. **Incision and soft tissue preparation.** A wide C-shaped (or lazy-S) post-auricular incision is made, extending from 2 cm above the pinna to the mastoid tip and 4 to 5 cm behind the post-auricular crease. This larger incision, compared to a simple mastoidectomy, provides the wide exposure needed for skull base surgery. An anteriorly-based musculoperiosteal flap is elevated. Temporalis fascia is harvested for later dural closure, and an abdominal fat graft is obtained through a separate small periumbilical incision, as this will be needed for mastoid obliteration during closure.

3. **Complete cortical mastoidectomy.** An extended complete mastoidectomy is performed using large cutting burrs, progressing to diamond burrs near critical structures. All key landmarks are systematically identified: the tegmen tympani is skeletonized superiorly to expose the middle fossa dura plate; the sigmoid sinus is fully skeletonized from the sinodural angle to the jugular bulb, exposing the anterior surface of the sinus (appearing as a blue line through thin overlying bone); the sinodural angle (Citelli's angle) is defined at the junction of the tegmen and sigmoid sinus; the lateral SCC is identified at the medial wall of the antrum; the posterior and superior SCCs are identified by drilling inferiorly and superiorly respectively; the facial nerve in its descending (mastoid) segment is identified in the posterior wall of the middle ear, inferior to the LSCC, and must be preserved throughout the procedure; and the jugular bulb is identified inferiorly, noting any high-riding variant, which is an important anatomical consideration.

4. **Labyrinthectomy.** A complete labyrinthectomy is performed following the same technique described in Part B (Steps 3 through 6). All three semicircular canals (lateral, posterior, and superior) are opened using a diamond burr, the vestibule is opened widely, and all five neuroepithelial structures are removed. The labyrinthectomy is the "translabyrinthine" component of the procedure and creates the surgical corridor leading to the IAC.

5. **Skeletonize and expose the internal auditory canal.** This is the key step of the translabyrinthine approach. Drilling proceeds along the posterior fossa dura medial to the labyrinthine structures. The IAC runs as a bony tube from the vestibule and cochlea laterally (the fundus) to the cerebellopontine angle medially (the porus acusticus), spanning approximately 1 cm in length and directed laterally with a slight anterior and inferior angulation. Using a diamond burr, bone overlying the IAC is progressively removed, beginning at the vestibule (fundus/lateral end of the IAC) and following the canal medially towards the porus acusticus. Drilling continues until 180-degree exposure of the IAC is achieved by removing bone from both the superior and inferior aspects. The IAC is skeletonized to a thin eggshell of bone overlying the dura of the canal, which is left intact at this stage. During this drilling, the surgeon must remain aware of critical surrounding relationships: the cochlea lies anteroinferior to the IAC fundus; the vestibule constitutes the lateral (fundal) end of the IAC; the labyrinthine segment of the facial nerve runs anterosuperiorly from the IAC fundus; the jugular bulb lies inferior to the IAC and may be high-riding, posing a risk of injury; the posterior fossa dura lies posterior and inferior to the IAC; and the carotid artery lies anterior and inferior (deep), at risk with excessive anterior drilling.

6. **Identify key landmarks at the IAC fundus.** The fundus (lateral end) of the IAC is divided into four quadrants by two bony crests. The transverse crest (crista transversa) is a horizontal bony ridge separating the superior compartment from the inferior compartment. The vertical crest, known as Bill's bar (crista falciformis), is a vertical bony ridge present only in the superior compartment, separating the facial nerve anteriorly from the superior vestibular nerve posteriorly. Bill's bar is named after Dr. William F. House and is the single most important landmark for identifying the facial nerve at the IAC fundus. The four quadrants and their contents are as follows: the anterosuperior quadrant contains the facial nerve (CN VII), which must be identified and preserved, lying anterior to Bill's bar; the posterosuperior quadrant contains the superior vestibular nerve, which is the origin of most vestibular schwannomas, lying posterior to Bill's bar; the anteroinferior quadrant contains the cochlear nerve, below the transverse crest anteriorly; and the posteroinferior quadrant contains the inferior vestibular nerve, below the transverse crest posteriorly, with the singular nerve to the posterior SCC also exiting here.

```
        IAC FUNDUS (Cross-section, viewed from medial/CPA side)

              SUPERIOR
        +---------+---------+
        |  CN VII  |  SVN    |
        | (Facial) |(Sup.    |
        |          | Vest.)  |
  ANT   +----------+---------+  POST
        |Cochlear  |  IVN    |
        |  Nerve   |(Inf.    |
        |          | Vest.)  |
        +----------+---------+
              INFERIOR

  Horizontal line = Transverse crest
  Vertical line (superior only) = Bill's bar (Vertical crest)
```

7. **Open IAC dura and expose the CPA.** The thin eggshell of bone overlying the IAC is removed completely. The IAC dura is incised longitudinally along the posterior surface, and the posterior fossa dura is opened posterior to the IAC to enter the cerebellopontine angle. CSF is drained from the CPA cistern, which, combined with the lumbar drain if placed, allows brain relaxation. The CPA is now visible, and the tumour (vestibular schwannoma) is typically apparent as a rounded, greyish-yellow mass.

8. **Tumour removal (for vestibular schwannoma).** The facial nerve is identified at the IAC fundus first — this is the major advantage of the translabyrinthine approach, as the nerve is identified at a point where it is anatomically predictable, before encountering the tumour in the CPA. An intraoperative facial nerve stimulator is used to confirm the nerve's identity. The tumour typically arises from the superior vestibular nerve (most common origin) or the inferior vestibular nerve. Tumour removal proceeds systematically: (a) the vestibular nerves of origin are divided at the fundus; (b) internal debulking is performed using the CUSA (Cavitron Ultrasonic Surgical Aspirator) or by piecemeal removal for large tumours, reducing bulk to allow capsule dissection; (c) the arachnoid plane between the tumour capsule and the facial nerve is developed; (d) sharp dissection of the tumour capsule from the facial nerve is carried out using microsurgical technique under high magnification; (e) the tumour is dissected from CPA structures including the AICA (anterior inferior cerebellar artery, which is often draped over or within the tumour), the brainstem, the lower cranial nerves (CN IX, X, XI), and the trigeminal nerve (CN V) if the tumour is large; and (f) the tumour is removed completely if possible (gross total resection) while preserving facial nerve integrity. Facial nerve preservation is the primary surgical goal after complete tumour removal. If facial nerve integrity is in doubt, the nerve stimulator is used at the brainstem end — a response confirms continuity.

9. **Closure (critical for CSF leak prevention).** The mastoid defect, IAC, and labyrinthine defect are packed with autologous abdominal fat graft; this fat obliteration step is the single most important measure to prevent CSF leak. Any dural defects are sealed with temporalis fascia and fibrin glue. The eustachian tube orifice and middle ear are packed with bone wax and/or muscle to prevent [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]]. The musculoperiosteal flap is closed over the fat graft, followed by subcutaneous tissue and skin closure in layers. A firm mastoid pressure dressing (bolster dressing) is applied for 5 to 7 days.

### Complications of the Translabyrinthine Approach

The complications of the translabyrinthine approach reflect the magnitude of this skull base procedure and the proximity of critical neurovascular structures. CSF leak is the most common complication, occurring in approximately 10 percent of cases, though meticulous fat obliteration of the mastoid defect reduces this rate to 2 to 4 percent. [[Facial Nerve|Facial nerve palsy]] is the complication of greatest functional significance; the rate of anatomical preservation depends primarily on tumour size, with small tumours achieving greater than 90 percent anatomical preservation and large tumours having progressively lower preservation rates. Meningitis occurs in 1 to 2 percent of cases and the risk is increased in the presence of a CSF leak, which serves as a conduit for ascending infection. Wound infection is a standard surgical risk. Postoperative headache is less common than with the retrosigmoid approach because the translabyrinthine approach does not require drilling into the posterior fossa bone or cerebellar retraction. Lower cranial nerve palsies (CN IX, X, XI) may occur with large CPA tumours that extend inferiorly toward the jugular foramen. Cerebellar or brainstem injury is rare but more common with large tumours that compress or adhere to these structures. AICA injury can produce the lateral pontine syndrome, manifesting as facial weakness, hearing loss, vertigo, and cerebellar signs. Mortality is less than 1 percent in experienced centres.

### Post-operative Management

Postoperative care following the translabyrinthine approach requires ICU or high-dependency monitoring for 24 to 48 hours with close neurological observation including GCS scoring, pupil reactions, and limb power assessment. Facial nerve function is assessed using the House-Brackmann grading scale. The head is elevated 30 degrees to reduce intracranial pressure. Perioperative antibiotics are continued for 48 to 72 hours. If CSF leak is suspected, management includes lumbar drain placement, bed rest, and acetazolamide therapy, with surgical re-exploration reserved for persistent leaks unresponsive to conservative measures. Vestibular rehabilitation is initiated after recovery from the acute surgical period. MRI is performed at one year postoperatively and then at serial intervals to monitor for recurrence.

## Comparison of Surgical Approaches for Vestibular Schwannoma

| Feature | Translabyrinthine | Retrosigmoid (Suboccipital) | Middle Fossa |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------------------|--------------|
| **Hearing preservation** | No (labyrinth destroyed) | Yes (possible) | Yes (possible) |
| **Tumour size** | Any size | Any size | Small only (intracanalicular, <1.5 cm CPA extension) |
| **Cerebellar retraction** | None | Required | None |
| **IAC fundus access** | Excellent | Poor (limited by posterior lip) | Excellent |
| **Facial nerve identification** | Early (at fundus) | Late (at brainstem) | Early (at fundus) |
| **CSF leak risk** | ~10% (reduced with fat graft) | 5-10% | 2-5% |
| **Headache risk** | Low | High | Moderate |
| **Best indication** | Non-serviceable hearing, any tumour size | Hearing preservation, large tumours | Hearing preservation, small intracanalicular tumours |

## Key Points for DNB Examination

1. **Donaldson's line** is the most important landmark for the endolymphatic sac — drawn along the long axis of the LSCC, bisecting the PSCC; sac lies below and posterior to this line
2. **Bill's bar (vertical crest)** is the most important landmark for the facial nerve at the IAC fundus — separates the facial nerve (anterosuperior) from the superior vestibular nerve (posterosuperior)
3. **Transverse crest** separates the superior compartment (facial + superior vestibular nerves) from the inferior compartment (cochlear + inferior vestibular nerves)
4. **Facial nerve** is located in the **anterosuperior quadrant** of the IAC fundus — this must be memorized
5. **Labyrinthectomy** destroys ALL hearing — only performed in non-serviceable hearing ears
6. **Translabyrinthine approach** advantage = early facial nerve identification at fundus + no cerebellar retraction
7. **Fat obliteration** of the mastoid defect is the key step to prevent CSF leak after translabyrinthine surgery
8. **Five neuroepithelial end-organs** must be removed during labyrinthectomy: three cristae + utricular macula + saccular macula
9. **Vestibular schwannomas** most commonly arise from the **superior vestibular nerve**
10. The **endolymphatic sac** has a characteristic **thickened, opaque, whitish appearance** on the posterior fossa dura that distinguishes it from surrounding normal dura
