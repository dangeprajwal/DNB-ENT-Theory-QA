# High Pressure Nervous Syndrome in Relation To Diving

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the high pressure nervous syndrome (HPNS) in relation to diving, including its pathophysiology, clinical features, and management. Discuss the other otological injuries associated with diving.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Volume 2 – Chapter 91: Ear Trauma (Sections on Diving-Related Injuries)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Diving exposes the human body to a range of pressure-related physiological stresses that can result in significant otological pathology. The ear is particularly vulnerable because of the air-filled middle ear space and the delicate structures of the inner ear. Diving-related ear injuries can be classified based on the phase of the dive at which they occur: compression (descent) injuries, injuries at high pressure, and decompression (ascent) injuries. High-pressure nervous syndrome (HPNS), also known as high-pressure neurological syndrome, is a specific condition that occurs when divers are exposed to extremely high pressures, and represents one of the limiting factors of deep diving.

---

## PART A: PHYSICS AND PHYSIOLOGY OF DIVING

### Pressure-Volume Relationships

Boyle's law governs the relationship between pressure and gas volume during diving. As a diver descends, the ambient pressure increases proportionally to depth (1 atmosphere for every 10 m of seawater). The corresponding gas volume is halved at 10 m depth. This explains why a diver's impression of middle ear pressure change is maximal during the first 10 m of descent.

### Effect of Pressure on the Body

In a diver or aviator, the increased ambient pressure is equally applied to all body structures. Since the body is predominantly composed of incompressible fluids and tissues, there is no differential pressure across any membranes and function is essentially unchanged. The critical exception is when there is an air-filled cavity within the body, such as the middle ear. Key physiological principles include:

- The middle ear is a bony cavity that cannot distend or expand
- The intraluminal vascular pressures reflect the ambient pressure
- If a relatively negative middle ear pressure develops due to failure to equalize via the Eustachian tube, a pressure gradient exists between intraluminal vascular pressure and middle ear pressure
- Head-out immersion causes squeezing of blood volume from the lower limbs into the chest and head, raising intracranial pressure

### Gas Laws Relevant to Diving

- **Henry's Law:** The amount of gas dissolved in a tissue is proportional to the pressure on that tissue. During descent, increased ambient pressure results in greater amounts of nitrogen dissolved in arterial and venous blood.
- During ascent (decompression), if the gas is not metabolized or removed at a sufficiently fast rate, a gas phase will form within the tissue.
- Nitrogen has a low partition coefficient (water/oil, 0.19) as it is more soluble in lipids than in water. Consequently, its transfer from tissues to the vasculature is slow.

## PART B: HIGH-PRESSURE NERVOUS SYNDROME (HPNS)

### Definition and Pathophysiology

High-pressure nervous syndrome (also termed high-pressure neurological syndrome or isobaric gas counterdiffusion) occurs when divers are exposed to extremely high pressures. The depth at which the condition occurs depends on the compression rate. The syndrome is thought to result from the direct effects of extreme hydrostatic pressure on neural tissue, distinct from the narcotic effects of dissolved gases.

Unlike nitrogen narcosis where intellectual impairment predominates, in HPNS psychomotor impairment exceeds intellectual impairment. This is an important distinguishing feature.

### Clinical Features

The characteristic symptoms of HPNS include:

- **Early symptoms:** Tiredness and general dizziness
- **Tremors:** Both postural and intentional, affecting the hands and potentially the whole body
- **Progressive symptoms (with increasing pressure):**
  - Ataxia
  - Myoclonus
  - Vertigo and nausea with nystagmus

Symptoms may occur at high pressure, but characteristically appear soon after changing the inspired gas mixture to one that includes a second inert gas. The sudden onset of vertigo and nausea with nystagmus has been reported as part of this syndrome.

### Vestibular and Auditory Effects

- Permanent vestibular damage has been described
- There is no hearing loss associated with HPNS
- This is an important distinction from other diving-related inner ear conditions such as inner ear barotrauma and decompression illness, where sensorineural hearing loss is frequently present

### Key Differentiating Features from Nitrogen Narcosis

| Feature | HPNS | Nitrogen Narcosis |
|---------|------|-------------------|
| Pressure | Extreme high pressure | Moderate depths (~30 m+) |
| Predominant impairment | Psychomotor | Intellectual |
| Tremor | Present (characteristic) | Absent |
| Hearing loss | None | None |
| Vestibular damage | May be permanent | No |

## PART C: OTHER OTOLOGICAL INJURIES IN DIVING

### Classification by Phase of Dive

**Compression (Descent) Injuries:**
- External ear barotrauma
- Middle ear barotrauma
- Inner ear barotrauma

**Injuries at High Pressure:**
- High-pressure nervous syndrome (HPNS)

**Decompression (Ascent) Injuries:**
- Alternobaric vertigo
- Barotraumatic facial palsy
- Inner ear decompression illness
- Cerebral air gas embolism (CAGE)

### External Ear Barotrauma

This occurs when a pocket of air is trapped within the external auditory meatus by cerumen, earplugs, foreign bodies or exostoses, most commonly due to a tight-fitting diving hood. The pressure of the trapped meatal air becomes negative compared to the middle ear pressure, displacing the tympanic membrane outwards. Perforation may occur if force is sufficient.

- **Symptom:** Pain increasing with depth
- **Signs:** Ear canal skin and tympanic membrane injection, petechial haemorrhages
- **Prevention:** Avoid occlusive earplugs and tight headgear
- **Treatment:** Clear the external meatus, antibiotic drops for secondary infection, surgical repair if perforation fails to heal spontaneously

### Middle Ear Barotrauma (Barotitis Media)

This is the most frequent pressure-induced ear condition. It occurs wherever there is Eustachian tube dysfunction resulting in a transtympanic pressure gradient. Transient evidence of middle ear barotrauma has been demonstrated in 5% of adults and 25% of young children after flying.

**Mechanism of injury:**

The perception of the requirement to equalize occurs at a maximum of 86 cm depth (8.7 kPa or 60 mmHg pressure change). If no equalizing manoeuvre is performed, mucosal congestion and oedema occur. Eustachian locking occurs at approximately 1.3 m depth (13 kPa or 90 mmHg), when the levator palati muscles cannot overcome external closing pressures.

**Tympanic membrane grading (modified Teed's classification):**

| Grade | Findings |
|-------|----------|
| 0 | Symptoms, no signs |
| 1 | Redness and retraction |
| 2 | Intratympanic membrane haemorrhage |
| 3 | Gross TM haemorrhage |
| 4 | Haemotympanum |
| 5 | Perforation |

**Prevention:**

- Oral pseudoephedrine is effective for flying (Grade A evidence from RCTs) but should not be extended to scuba divers with acute Eustachian dysfunction due to greater risk and consequences
- Otovent nasal balloon (auto-Politzerization) may help those with difficulty performing equalization manoeuvres
- Consider Eustachian tube balloon dilatation
- Posterior septal surgery considerably increased the ability to equalize to 10 m compared to controls

**Prediction of risk:**

- Inability to equalize by Valsalva has 91% chance of developing middle ear barotrauma (vs 37% in controls)
- Nine-step inflation/deflation tympanometric test combined with Toynbee test gives 100% reliable predictive results
- Poor mastoid pneumatization correlates with increased risk (no patients with mastoid area >34.7 cm3 sustained barotrauma)

### Inner Ear Barotrauma

Three pathological entities result from compression barotrauma:

**1. Inner ear haemorrhage:** Transient or minimal vestibular symptoms with mild to moderate SNHL. Good recovery is expected.

**2. Labyrinthine membrane tears:** Permanent hearing loss, often at 1-2 kHz. May present similarly to acute [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]] with vertigo, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and characteristic low-frequency hearing loss. Temporal bone studies have demonstrated Reissner's membrane rupture.

**3. Perilymphatic fistula:** Occurs in approximately 0.5% of divers. Clinical features include:

- Sudden onset of vertigo, SNHL (sensorineural or mixed), and tinnitus
- Muffled hearing and persistent dysequilibrium
- Fluctuating hearing is typical
- Chronic symptoms: motion intolerance, mild nausea, positional vertigo, Tullio phenomenon

**Investigations:** Fistula tests are unreliable (positive in only 25-40% of surgically proven cases). Accurate pressure stimulation with tympanometry and electronystagmography improves detection to 90%. Cochlin-tonoprotein (16 kDa protein found in perilymph, not in other body fluids) has a claimed specificity of 98% and may provide a future confirmatory test.

**Treatment strategy is based on severity:**

- **Moderate to severe HL (>30-40 dB):** Immediate surgical exploration recommended
- **Mild to moderate HL (20-40 dB):** If <24 hours and vestibular symptoms predominate, immediate exploration; otherwise serial audiometry with low threshold for surgery
- **Minimal HL (high-frequency only):** Initial conservative approach with bed rest, head elevation 30-40 degrees
- Surgical results: vestibular symptom relief in 87%, hearing improvement in about 40%

### Caloric Vertigo

Asymmetrical contact of cold water with both tympanic membranes, or cold water ingress into one middle ear via a perforation, produces a caloric effect. Causes include wax, exostoses, otitis externa, tight diving hoods, or foreign bodies. It is usually short-lived and self-correcting upon surfacing.

### Alternobaric Vertigo

A condition of asymmetrical middle ear overpressure occurring in divers and air passengers. It usually occurs during ascent due to unilateral Eustachian tube dysfunction leading to relatively higher pressure in one middle ear. Of 2053 Swedish divers, 22% described symptoms consistent with alternobaric vertigo at some time during their diving careers. Episodes usually last a maximum of 10 minutes with no permanent sequelae. Twenty-seven percent of cases occurred during descent.

### Barotraumatic Facial Palsy

A transient [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]] caused by high middle ear pressure during ascent. It is probably due to a pressure-induced neuropraxia in an individual with a congenital dehiscence of the facial nerve in its intramastoid portion (present in 0.5-57% of all temporal bones). Animal studies have demonstrated decreased blood flow in the vasa nervora of the facial nerve when increased middle ear pressure is transmitted through a dehiscence. Blood flow and palsy rapidly return to normal when middle ear pressure is relieved.

- No other neurological symptoms or signs are present
- Often associated with mild middle ear barotrauma and alternobaric vertigo
- Onset is typically rapid with spontaneous resolution
- Myringotomy should be performed if a bulging tympanic membrane is identified and symptoms persist

### Inner Ear Decompression Illness

Decompression illnesses are classified into:

- **Type I:** Musculoskeletal pain predominates; also cutaneous features (itching, urticaria, burning)
- **Type II:** Cardiorespiratory and/or central nervous system involvement

As many as 70% of all divers with decompression illness have followed their decompression tables correctly. Isolated vestibular or hearing symptoms are rare; concurrent symptoms should be expected. Key features include:

- Localized pain (91.8%), numbness/paraesthesia (21.2%), weakness (20.6%), skin rash (14.9%), dizziness/vertigo (8.5%), auditory disturbance (0.3%)
- 50% of CNS (Type II) patients report symptom onset within 10 minutes of surfacing; 90% within 3 hours
- All cases must be screened for patent foramen ovale (present in 48-80% of cases)
- Recompression within 6 hours gives 53% complete resolution vs poor outcomes if treated after 6 hours

**Differential Diagnosis: Inner Ear Barotrauma vs Decompression Illness**

| Feature | Inner Ear Barotrauma | Inner Ear DCI |
|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| Phase of dive | Usually descent | Only during/after ascent |
| Equalization difficulty | Yes | Not usual |
| Associated ME barotrauma | Usual | Not usual |
| Non-otological symptoms | None | Usually present |
| Effect of recompression | Worsened | Improvement |
| Hearing loss | Mixed (common, 85%) | SNHL (38-66%) |

### Cerebral Air Gas Embolism (CAGE)

The most serious diving emergency. It occurs during any depth of dive, even in swimming pools, usually in inexperienced divers who breath-hold when surfacing. Ascent of only 1 m without exhalation is sufficient to produce microalveolar tears. Symptoms include sudden onset of chest pain, severe dyspnoea, subcutaneous crepitus, seizures, confusion, hemiplegia, and blood-stained frothy sputum. Vertigo is the presenting symptom in about 25% of cases. Emergency treatment is the Trendelenberg (head-down) position breathing oxygen, followed by immediate recompression.

### Hyperbaric Oxygen Treatment Complications

Middle ear barotrauma occurs in about 8% of patients undergoing repeated hyperbaric treatment. Vestibular symptoms with hearing loss occur in about 1.5% of dives or 3% of those treated. Unconscious patients or those with artificial airways have a 94% chance of barotrauma, requiring routine insertion of ventilation tubes.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. HPNS occurs at extreme high pressures and is characterized by tiredness, dizziness, tremor (postural and intentional), progressing to ataxia and myoclonus.
> 2. Unlike nitrogen narcosis, HPNS causes psychomotor impairment exceeding intellectual impairment.
> 3. HPNS characteristically occurs soon after changing the inspired gas mixture to include a second inert gas.
> 4. Permanent vestibular damage may result from HPNS, but there is no hearing loss.
> 5. Eustachian locking occurs at approximately 1.3 m depth (13 kPa pressure), making equalization impossible.
> 6. Middle ear barotrauma is graded 0-5 using the modified Teed's classification.
> 7. Three types of inner ear barotrauma exist: haemorrhage, labyrinthine tears, and perilymphatic fistulae.
> 8. Perilymphatic fistula occurs in 0.5% of divers; fistula tests are positive in only 25-40% of proven cases.
> 9. Inner ear decompression illness must be differentiated from inner ear barotrauma as recompression treatment helps DCI but worsens barotrauma.
> 10. Recompression within 6 hours for inner ear DCI gives 53% complete resolution vs poor outcomes if delayed.
> 11. Patent foramen ovale must be excluded in all cases of inner ear DCI (present in 48-80%).
> 12. Oral pseudoephedrine prevents middle ear barotrauma in flying (Grade A evidence) but is not recommended for divers with acute Eustachian dysfunction.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. The single most helpful piece of clinical information in diving injuries is the exact timing of symptom onset relative to the dive phase - descent injury suggests barotrauma, ascent/post-surfacing suggests decompression illness.
> 2. Recompression treatment for a misdiagnosed inner ear barotrauma (mistaken for DCI) may cause further middle and inner ear damage.
> 3. CAGE can occur from an ascent of only 1 m without exhalation, even in a swimming pool.
> 4. Alternobaric vertigo occurs in 22% of divers at some point in their careers and is usually self-limiting within 10 minutes.
> 5. Barotraumatic facial palsy is due to neuropraxia from compression of vasa nervora through a dehiscent facial nerve canal, not due to decompression illness.
> 6. Frequent high-pressure exposure itself has been refuted as a cause of sensorineural deafness; the predominant cause of hearing loss in commercial divers is noise-induced.
