## PART A: Osteoradionecrosis of the Temporal Bone

### Definition

Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) of the temporal bone is defined as **exposure and necrosis of a variable portion of previously irradiated petrous temporal bone that fails to heal over a period of 3 months**. It occurs as a late complication following high-dose [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] to the petrous temporal bone region.

### Aetiology and Pathogenesis

ORN of the petrous temporal bone occurs following high-dose radiotherapy for malignancies of:

- Parotid gland
- External auditory canal
- Middle ear
- Maxilla
- Nasopharynx
- Pituitary

The **pathogenesis** involves radiation-induced damage at multiple levels:

1. **Vascular damage** — radiation causes vasculitis leading to obliteration of blood vessels and avascular necrosis
2. **Cellular damage** — inhibition of mitosis and capacity for tissue repair
3. **Marx's characterization** — the irradiated tissue becomes **hypoxic, hypocellular and hypovascular** (the "3H" concept)
4. **Bone susceptibility** — the compact (rather than cancellous) nature of petrous temporal bone and poor blood supply of tympanic ring increase susceptibility

**Risk factors for ORN:**

- Megavoltage vs kilovoltage radiation (kilovoltage delivers higher bone dose)
- Microvascular disease (diabetics, atherosclerotics)
- Trauma after radiotherapy (analogous to dental extractions for mandibular ORN)
- High local doses of brachytherapy
- Proximity of bone to radiation field centre

### Pathology

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| Bone changes | Empty lacunae, osteoclast preponderance, demineralization, osteolysis, reparative fibrosis, secondary infection |
| Soft tissue | Epithelial hyperplasia, dermal atrophy, fibrosis, necrosis |
| Macroscopic | Skin/soft tissue loss, exposed bone, bony sequestration, secondary infection |
| Most susceptible | Tympanic ring |

### Classification and Clinical Features

The time interval between radiotherapy and clinically evident ORN varies from **<12 months to 23 years**.

**Ramsden's Classification:**

- **Localized form** — mild otalgia and otorrhoea, small areas of exposed bone in EAC, occurs when petrous bone is in periphery of field, CT shows small sequestration, can heal conservatively in 1–4 years
- **Diffuse/extensive form** — severe pain and otorrhoea, widespread bony destruction on CT, risk of facial canal erosion, inner ear involvement, intracranial complications (brain abscess, meningitis, death)

### Management

**Prevention:**
- Careful radiotherapy planning (IMRT, multiple fields, Hadron therapy)
- Complete surgical intervention prior to radiotherapy with full [[Wound Healing Phases Types Factors|wound healing]]
- Subtotal petrosectomy with mastoid obliteration and EAC oversewing if [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]] needed before RT for parotid neoplasms

**Treatment of Localized ORN:**
- Conservative management — aural toilet, careful sequestrum removal, local antibiotics, analgesics
- Healing may take up to 4 years
- Local rotational postauricular skin flap to cover small exposed bone areas

**Treatment of Diffuse ORN:**
- Conservative management is inadequate
- Radical surgical debridement (radical mastoidectomy or subtotal petrosectomy) to reach healthy vascularized bone
- Vascularized soft-tissue flap coverage at time of debridement
- **Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO)** — controversial but modest evidence suggests improved outcomes; Marx's RCT showed 4–5 times reduced complications with HBO support

## PART B: Xylocaine (Lignocaine) Toxicity and Local Anaesthesia for Intranasal Surgery

### Pharmacology of Lignocaine (Lidocaine/Xylocaine)

Lignocaine belongs to the **amide class** of local anaesthetics (as opposed to benzocaine which is an ester). Both classes work by binding to a receptor on the **sodium channel** in the axon, preventing channel opening and thereby preventing depolarization of the nerve.

### Manifestations of Lignocaine Toxicity

Local anaesthetic toxicity is dose-dependent and primarily affects the **CNS and cardiovascular system**:

**CNS Toxicity (occurs first at lower doses):**
- Perioral numbness and tingling
- Metallic taste in mouth
- Lightheadedness, dizziness
- [[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]]
- Visual disturbances
- Slurred speech
- Muscle twitching → generalized tonic-clonic seizures
- Loss of consciousness
- Respiratory arrest

**Cardiovascular Toxicity (at higher doses):**
- Bradycardia
- Hypotension
- Conduction abnormalities (prolonged PR, widened QRS)
- Ventricular arrhythmias
- Cardiac arrest

### Maximum Recommended Doses

| Agent | Without Adrenaline | With Adrenaline |
|-------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Lignocaine | 3 mg/kg | 7 mg/kg |
| Bupivacaine | 2 mg/kg | 2 mg/kg |
| Cocaine | 1.5 mg/kg | — (not used with adrenaline) |

### Management of LA Toxicity

1. **Stop injection** immediately
2. **Call for help** — anaesthetic emergency team
3. **Airway management** — secure airway, 100% oxygen, intubate if needed
4. **Seizure control** — IV midazolam or propofol (small doses)
5. **Cardiovascular support** — IV fluids, vasopressors if hypotensive
6. **Intralipid therapy** (lipid rescue) — **20% IV lipid emulsion** is the definitive treatment for severe LA toxicity
   - Bolus: 1.5 mL/kg over 1 minute
   - Infusion: 15 mL/kg/hr
   - Mechanism: acts as a "lipid sink" sequestering lipophilic LA molecules
7. **CPR** if cardiac arrest — may require prolonged resuscitation
8. **Avoid** propofol for cardiac depression and bretylium/lidocaine for arrhythmias

### Local Anaesthesia for Intranasal Surgery

**Topical Decongestion and Anaesthesia:**

The standard preparation for endoscopic sinus surgery includes:

- **Moffat's solution** — cocaine + adrenaline ± sodium bicarbonate, soaked on neuropatties, ribbon gauze or pledgets
- **Co-phenylcaine** — combination of **lignocaine 5% and phenylephrine 0.5%**, shown in RCT to be equally effective as cocaine
- **Oxymetazoline** alone — used effectively in centres where cocaine is restricted
- Topical agents are placed for 10–15 minutes before surgery

**Infiltration Anaesthesia:**

- **1–2 mL of 1% lignocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline** — injected into middle turbinate, nasal septum, and frontal process of maxilla
- **Greater palatine canal injection** — evidence shows decreased intraoperative blood loss during sinus surgery (produces 4.7% reduction in nasal blood flow)
- Always monitor total dose of cocaine + lignocaine combined

**Important considerations:**
- Lignocaine is effectively absorbed from mucosal surfaces — topical doses contribute to systemic levels
- Adrenaline causes temporary heart rate rise which may worsen operative field initially
- Allow time for vasoconstrictors to establish local effect before surgery
- Patients with acidotic tissues have reduced LA efficacy

## PART C: Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN)

### Definition

Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) is the intravenous delivery of all nutritional requirements — protein, carbohydrate, fat, vitamins, minerals and trace elements — directly into the bloodstream, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract entirely. It is delivered through a **dedicated central venous catheter**.

### Indications in ENT/Head and Neck

TPN is **rarely required** in patients with head and neck cancer (<1% incidence) as most patients have an accessible and functioning GI tract. Specific indications include:

- Obstructive tumours making enteral tube placement impossible
- Chyle leak unresponsive to oral/enteral diet modification
- Malnourished patients with non-functional, inaccessible or leaking GI tract
- Short-term nutritional bridge when enteral route is contraindicated
- Post-operative period when oral/enteral feeding cannot be established

### Components of TPN

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Carbohydrate | Dextrose (glucose) — primary energy source |
| Protein | Amino acid solutions — for tissue repair |
| Fat | Lipid emulsions (20%) — concentrated energy |
| Electrolytes | Na, K, Ca, Mg, Phosphate |
| Vitamins | Water-soluble and fat-soluble |
| Trace elements | Zinc, copper, manganese, selenium, chromium |

Standard target: **25 kcal/kg/24 hours** with balanced macronutrient mix.

### Complications of TPN

**Catheter-related complications:**
- Central line infection/sepsis (most common serious complication)
- Pneumothorax during insertion
- Air embolism
- Catheter thrombosis
- Central vein thrombosis/stenosis

**Metabolic complications:**
- Hyperglycaemia (most common metabolic) — linked to increased infection rates and poor wound healing
- Hypoglycaemia (on abrupt cessation)
- Electrolyte imbalances (hypo/hyperkalaemia, hypophosphataemia)
- **Refeeding syndrome** — dangerous metabolic complication when nutrition reintroduced in severely malnourished/starved patients; causes hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, fluid overload
- Hypertriglyceridaemia
- Acid-base disturbances

**Hepatobiliary complications:**
- Hepatic steatosis (fatty liver)
- Cholestasis and gallstone formation
- TPN-associated liver disease (with prolonged use)

**Management of TPN:**
- Withdrawal should be planned and stepwise with daily review
- Can be stopped once adequate oral/enteral nutrition is tolerated
- Delivered through dedicated feeding line
- Strict glycaemic monitoring and control
- Enteral nutrition is preferred over parenteral when possible (lower central line infection risk)

## PART D: Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

### Etiopathogenesis

Deep vein thrombosis is the pathological formation of blood clot within the deep venous system, most commonly the lower limbs. The pathogenesis is explained by **Virchow's Triad**, with three broad categories contributing to thrombus formation:

1. **Hypercoagulability** (problems within the blood)
2. **Haemodynamic/blood flow changes** (stasis)
3. **Endothelial injury or dysfunction** (vessel wall damage)

**Inherited thrombophilias (hypercoagulability):**

| Risk Factor | Relative Risk |
|------------|--------------|
| Antithrombin deficiency (hetero) | 5.0 |
| Factor V Leiden (hetero) | 8.1 |
| Factor V Leiden (homo) | 80.0 |
| Protein C deficiency | 3.1 |
| Prothrombin gene mutation | 2.8 |

**Acquired risk factors:**

| Risk Factor | Relative Risk |
|------------|--------------|
| Hospitalization | 11.1 |
| Immobilization | 8.9 |
| Surgery | 5.9 |
| Oral contraceptive | 3.8 |
| Pregnancy | 4.2 |
| Puerperium | 14.1 |

Other acquired factors include malignancy, dehydration, advanced age, obesity and vascular malformation. Hyperhomocysteinaemia (inherited or acquired) causes damage to the endothelial wall and increases clotting propensity — managed with vitamin B6, B12 and folic acid supplementation.

### Diagnosis

- **Clinical features** — unilateral leg swelling, pain, warmth, tenderness along deep veins, Homan's sign (unreliable)
- **D-dimer** — raised in DVT but non-specific (negative D-dimer effectively rules out DVT)
- **Compression ultrasonography** — first-line imaging, non-compressibility of vein confirms thrombus
- **CT venography** — for iliac/IVC thrombosis
- **Thrombophilia screen** — best delayed until 6 weeks after discontinuing anticoagulation
  - Includes: antithrombin, protein C, protein S levels; factor V Leiden; prothrombin gene mutation

### Management

**Anticoagulation:**
- **Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)** — initial treatment (e.g., enoxaparin)
- **Warfarin** — long-term oral anticoagulant, targets INR 2–4
- Duration: DVT typically treated for **3 months**, PE for 3–6 months
- Long-term anticoagulation for homozygous defects or recurrent events

**Monitoring:**
- Raised D-dimer after stopping anticoagulation is predictive of recurrent clot
- Patients tend to have same type of recurrence (DVT recurs as DVT, PE as PE)

**VTE Prophylaxis in ENT surgery:**
- Risk assessment on admission for all hospital inpatients
- LMWH prophylaxis for moderate-high risk patients
- Mechanical thromboprophylaxis (TED stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression)
- Early mobilization post-operatively

## PART E: Radionuclides in ENT — Imaging and Therapeutic Applications

### Definition

Radionuclides are unstable isotopes that emit radiation (gamma rays, positrons, or beta particles) as they decay. In medicine, they are used for both **diagnostic imaging** (nuclear medicine scans) and **therapeutic** purposes. The radiation emitted allows either visualization of physiological processes or targeted destruction of diseased tissue.

### Diagnostic Imaging Applications in ENT

**1. Technetium-99m (99mTc) Scintigraphy:**

| Application | Agent | Use |
|------------|-------|-----|
| Bone scan | 99mTc-MDP | Skull base osteomyelitis (SBO/MOE) — nearly 100% sensitivity in early disease |
| Thyroid scan | 99mTc-pertechnetate | Ectopic thyroid, thyroid nodule evaluation, retrosternal goitre |
| Salivary scan | 99mTc-pertechnetate | [[Sjogren Syndrome|Sjogren's syndrome]], salivary gland function |
| Bone metastasis | 99mTc-MDP | Staging for HN malignancies |

**2. Gallium-67 (67Ga) Scintigraphy:**
- Superior for **follow-up** of skull base osteomyelitis/MOE
- Images improve with clinical improvement, making it better than bone scan for monitoring treatment response
- Also used for inflammatory and neoplastic conditions

**3. Leucocyte Scintigraphy:**
- Uses labelled white blood cells (In-111 or 99mTc-HMPAO)
- Useful in diagnosis of osteomyelitis and active infection
- Complementary to gallium scan for MOE monitoring

**4. [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]] (Positron Emission Tomography):**
- Uses **18F-FDG** (fluorodeoxyglucose) — a positron-emitting radionuclide
- Principle: malignant cells have increased glucose metabolism, accumulating FDG
- Applications in ENT:
  - Staging of head and neck malignancies
  - Detection of unknown primary tumours
  - Post-treatment surveillance and recurrence detection
  - Assessment of treatment response
  - Detection of distant metastases

**5. MIBG (Metaiodobenzylguanidine) Scintigraphy:**
- Uses I-123 or I-131 labelled MIBG
- Specific for **paragangliomas/[[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]]s** — identifies multicentric disease
- Useful for pre-operative staging

### Therapeutic Applications in ENT

**1. Radioactive Iodine (I-131) Therapy:**
- **Differentiated thyroid cancer** — post-thyroidectomy ablation of thyroid remnant and treatment of metastases
- Mechanism: thyroid follicular cells actively take up iodine; I-131 emits beta particles causing local tissue destruction
- Most important therapeutic radionuclide in ENT practice

**2. Radionuclide-Based Brachytherapy:**
- Uses sealed radioactive sources (Ir-192, Cs-137) placed close to/within tumour
- Applications: oral cavity cancers, oropharyngeal cancers
- Allows high local dose with rapid dose fall-off, sparing surrounding tissues

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. ORN of temporal bone = exposed necrotic irradiated bone failing to heal over 3 months; tissue is hypoxic, hypocellular, hypovascular (Marx)
> 2. ORN classified as localized (conservative management, 1–4 year healing) or diffuse (requires radical debridement + vascularized flap)
> 3. Lignocaine max dose: 3 mg/kg without adrenaline, 7 mg/kg with adrenaline
> 4. LA toxicity presents with CNS symptoms first (perioral numbness, tinnitus, seizures) then cardiovascular collapse
> 5. **Intralipid 20% is the definitive rescue** for severe LA toxicity — acts as a "lipid sink"
> 6. Intranasal LA for [[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]]: topical cocaine/co-phenylcaine/oxymetazoline + infiltration with 1% lignocaine with adrenaline 1:80,000
> 7. TPN rarely needed in HN cancer (<1%); complications include line sepsis, hyperglycaemia, refeeding syndrome, hepatic steatosis
> 8. Virchow's Triad explains DVT: hypercoagulability + blood flow stasis + endothelial injury
> 9. Factor V Leiden homozygous = 80x relative risk for thrombosis; LMWH is initial DVT treatment
> 10. 99mTc-MDP bone scan has ~100% sensitivity for early skull base osteomyelitis
> 11. PET-CT (18F-FDG) is used for HN cancer staging, unknown primary detection, and recurrence surveillance
> 12. Radioactive iodine (I-131) is the most important therapeutic radionuclide — used for post-thyroidectomy ablation in differentiated thyroid cancer


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **ORN time lag**: ORN can present up to 23 years after radiotherapy — always consider in any patient with previous head/neck RT presenting with persistent ear discharge
> - **Cocaine awareness**: cocaine is both a local anaesthetic AND vasoconstrictor, but increasingly restricted; co-phenylcaine is an evidence-based alternative
> - **Refeeding syndrome**: potentially fatal when TPN started in malnourished patients — start feeding cautiously, monitor phosphate levels
> - **DVT vs PE recurrence**: patients tend to recur with the same type of event (DVT → DVT, PE → PE)
> - **Gallium > Bone scan for MOE follow-up**: gallium imaging improves with clinical improvement, making it the preferred modality for treatment monitoring
> - **Intralipid rescue**: should be available in all areas where local anaesthetics are administered in significant doses
