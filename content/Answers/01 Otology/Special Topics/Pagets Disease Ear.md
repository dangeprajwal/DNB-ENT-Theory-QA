# Otological Effects of Paget's Disease

**Question:** *Define Paget's disease of bone. Describe the otological effects of Paget's disease including pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis and management.*

Source: Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 90

---

## INTRODUCTION

Paget's disease of bone (PDB), originally termed **osteitis deformans** by Sir James Paget in 1877, is a progressive, focal disorder of bone remodelling and the **second most frequent metabolic bone disorder** worldwide. There is an increase in osteoclastic activity producing bone resorption, with reactive stimulation of osteoblasts producing increased bone deposition, resulting in bone that is architecturally unsound. This leads to bone pain, deformity and fragility. Affected individuals typically have enlargement of the skull with involvement of the pelvis, tibia or femur. The temporal bone is frequently involved when the skull is affected, producing significant otological sequelae.

---

## PART A: Epidemiology and Aetiology

### Epidemiology

- Second most common bone disease worldwide
- **Male predominance** — 3:2 male to female ratio
- More common in **Britain, North America, Australia and New Zealand**
- Rare in Scandinavia, Asia and non-white races
- UK prevalence in >55 year age group: **0.3%**
- Incidence rises steeply with age
- Incidence has been declining over recent decades
- Archaeological evidence suggests a northwest European origin

### Aetiology

The exact cause is unknown. Current evidence supports an interplay between **genetic and environmental factors**, possibly viral infection of bone cells.

**Genetic factors:**
- **SQSTM1 gene** — the first gene found to be affected in PDB; currently >25 mutations identified
- This gene encodes **p62 protein**, involved in regulating osteoclast function
- Patients carrying the abnormal gene develop more advanced disease
- Other candidate genes have also been identified

**Viral hypothesis:**
- Polyclonal antibodies reveal **paramyxovirus antigens** in pagetic osteoclasts, compatible with measles and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections
- Monoclonal antibody studies implicate measles, simian virus and human parainfluenza virus
- Electron microscopy shows **fingerprint pattern** in nuclei of pagetic osteoclasts, supporting viral infection
- However, findings are inconsistent — not all studies confirm paramyxovirus particles in pagetic bone, and no convincing viral particles have been isolated from pagetic osteosarcoma

## PART B: Pathophysiology

### Phases of Bone Disease

The basic abnormality is **increased bone turnover** in involved bones. PDB exists in monostotic (10–30%) and polyostotic forms.

**Most frequently affected bones** (decreasing order): pelvis, femur, skull, tibia, vertebrae, clavicle, humerus.

**Three phases of bone changes:**

| Phase | Characteristics |
|-------|----------------|
| Osteolytic | Increased osteoclastic resorption, early demineralization |
| Mixed | Simultaneous resorption and deposition, disordered architecture |
| Osteoblastic | Increased new bone formation, sclerotic changes |

**In the temporal bone, a fourth phase** has been described:
- **Lamellar remodelled phase** — osteoblastic pagetic bone undergoes remodelling into normal-appearing lamellar bone with distinct Haversian canal systems
- Particularly seen around the internal auditory canal, producing bossing of pagetic bone
- The endochondral bone is more resistant to resorption
- Different histological phases may coexist within a single temporal bone specimen

### Pattern of Temporal Bone Involvement

PDB involves the periosteal bone first. With increasingly aggressive disease:

1. **Periosteal layer** — involved in all cases (100%)
2. **Enchondral layer** — extension in ~85% (22/26 bones in Khetarpal and Schuknecht's study)
3. **Endosteal layer** — invasion in ~54% (14/26 bones)

The disease typically spreads from the **petrous apex laterally**, producing a washed-out appearance due to extensive demineralization.

## PART C: Otological Clinical Features

### Hearing Loss

The prevalence of hearing loss in PDB varies between **13% and 40%**. The relative risk compared to age/sex-matched controls is **1.6** (95% CI 1.3–1.9).

**Characteristics of hearing loss:**
- Typically **high-frequency sensorineural** in nature
- May also have a **low-frequency air-bone gap** (conductive component)
- **Rate of deterioration**: ~2 dB per annum in pagetic ears vs 0.5 dB per annum in controls
- PTA >40 dB in 55% of PDB patients vs 11% of controls

**Mechanisms of hearing loss (Khetarpal and Schuknecht's conclusions):**

The conductive AND sensorineural components are caused by **changes in bone density, mass and form that dampen the finely tuned motion mechanics of the middle and inner ears**. This is supported by a strong, statistically significant relationship between bone mineral density of the cochlear capsule and both high-frequency air-conduction thresholds and air-bone gap.

| Proposed Mechanism | Evidence |
|-------------------|----------|
| Otic capsule changes | Changes in bone density/mass dampen middle and inner ear mechanics |
| Stapes fixation | Found in 2/26 bones; fractured footplate in 1 specimen |
| IAC narrowing | Historical theory, now largely refuted — no correlation between IAC diameter and hearing thresholds |
| Cochlear vein occlusion | Occlusion of inferior cochlear vein in 78% → stria vascularis changes → fluid homeostasis abnormalities |
| VIIIth nerve compression | ABR normal in 56/64 ears — retrocochlear lesion unlikely |

**Important:** The conductive component does NOT improve surgically even if [[Otosclerosis|otosclerosis]] coexists.

### [[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]]

- Slightly more common in PDB than controls
- Relative rate: **1.5** (95% CI 1.1–2.2)

### Vestibular Disturbance

- Incidence: **20–30%** of PDB patients suffer vestibular dysfunction
- Relative rate compared to controls: **1.3** (95% CI 1.2–1.5)
- Symptoms have characteristic of **motion-provoked imbalance**
- Histological analysis shows loss of vestibular nerve fibres greater than normal for age, but not consistent hair cell loss in maculae or cristae

## PART D: Diagnosis

PDB is often found incidentally on X-ray or laboratory tests.

### Radiological Investigations

**Plain X-ray:**
- Early stages: osteolytic lesions
- Progressive disease: increased bone density, abnormal architecture, cortical thickening, bowing, overgrowth
- Microfractures in long bones

**CT scan (temporal bone):**
- Shows involvement of temporal bone
- Advanced cases: complete obliteration of otic capsule
- Disease spreads from petrous apex laterally
- Washed-out appearance from extensive demineralization
- IAC may show narrowing from irregular mineralized bone deposition

**Nuclear medicine — Technetium-99m bone scan:**
- Shows increased nuclide localization to pagetic sites
- Useful screening tool for disease extent
- More sensitive than plain X-ray for early disease

### Laboratory Investigations

| Test | Significance |
|------|-------------|
| Serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) | Elevated — marker of new bone formation (not resorption) |
| Bone-specific ALP | More specific marker |
| Urinary hydroxyproline/creatinine | Elevated — measure of collagen breakdown |
| Urinary/serum deoxypyridinoline | Measure of osteoclastic activity |
| N-telopeptide, C-telopeptide | Markers of bone resorption |
| Serum calcium/phosphorus | Usually normal; calcium may increase during bedrest |
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D | Part of baseline assessment |

## PART E: Management

### General Principles

Early PDB is not associated with significant otological complications. With advanced disease, otological complications occur with modestly increased rates compared to the normal population. Combined management with rheumatologists is recommended.

### Treatment Modalities

**1. Bisphosphonate Therapy (Mainstay):**

Bisphosphonates act to reduce bone turnover and normalize raised biochemical markers.

- **Zolendronic acid** — current recommended treatment: **single 5 mg intravenous dose**
- Reid et al. (Grade A evidence, double-blind RCT): single infusion of zoledronate produced greater biochemical disease control vs daily risedronate for 2 months
- 6.5-year follow-up: relapse rate — **0.7% with zolendronate vs 20% with risedronate**
- Potential otic toxicity with pamidronate infusions reported in one case (tinnitus, vertigo, hearing loss)

**Indications for treatment (Crisp criteria):**
- Pain at site of known PDB
- Early, potentially deforming disease
- Osteolytic lesions (especially weight-bearing bones)
- Skull disease
- Complications: progressive neurological syndrome, fissure fractures, immobilization hypercalcaemia, high-output cardiac failure
- Disease in patients aged <55 years
- Serum ALP and/or urinary hydroxyproline >2x upper normal limit
- Pre-operative reduction of hypervascularity

**2. Hearing Rehabilitation:**
- **Modern hearing aids** — useful for both conductive and sensorineural loss; preferred modality
- **[[Bone Conduction MEI Devices|BAHA]]** — case reports of successful use; performed in two stages due to bone vascularity (to allow osseointegration)
- **[[Cochlear Implant|Cochlear implantation]]** — case reports with reasonable results
- **Middle ear reconstruction** — generally poor results due to lack of consistent pathology for the conductive hearing loss

**3. Other:**
- Calcitonin and etidronate — historical use with stabilization or improvement of hearing in small studies
- Physiotherapy
- Analgesics for pain management

### Complications of Paget's Disease

| Complication | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Osteosarcoma | <0.1% of PDB; more common in polyostotic variant |
| Skull deformity | Frontal bossing |
| Platybasia | Descent of cranium on cervical spine → vertebrobasilar compromise |
| Cranial nerve involvement | VIIIth nerve most commonly affected |
| High-output cardiac failure | When >1/3 of skeleton is affected |
| Hypercalcaemia | During immobilization or bedrest |
| Pain, OA, fractures | Common; related to bone deformity |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Paget's disease (osteitis deformans) is the **2nd most common metabolic bone disorder** — characterized by increased osteoclastic activity with reactive osteoblastic new bone formation producing architecturally unsound bone
> 2. PDB shows **3:2 male predominance**, more common in Britain, North America, Australia/NZ; rare in Asia and non-white races
> 3. Aetiology involves **SQSTM1 gene** mutations (encoding p62 protein) and possible paramyxoviral infection (measles, RSV)
> 4. **Four phases in temporal bone**: osteolytic → mixed → osteoblastic → lamellar remodelled
> 5. Temporal bone involvement spreads from **petrous apex laterally**, involving periosteal layer first → enchondral → endosteal
> 6. Hearing loss prevalence 13–40% (RR 1.6); typically **high-frequency SNHL** with possible low-frequency air-bone gap
> 7. Hearing loss mechanism: changes in bone density/mass **dampen motion mechanics** of middle and inner ears (Khetarpal and Schuknecht)
> 8. ABR is normal in 88% of pagetic ears — retrocochlear lesion is NOT the usual cause of SNHL
> 9. **Serum alkaline phosphatase** is the key biochemical marker (elevated; reflects new bone formation)
> 10. **Zolendronic acid 5 mg IV single dose** is the current treatment of choice — 0.7% relapse at 6.5 years vs 20% with risedronate (Grade A evidence)
> 11. Middle ear surgery results are generally **poor** — conductive loss does not improve surgically even if otosclerosis coexists
> 12. Most devastating complication: **osteosarcoma** (<0.1% but significantly higher than non-affected individuals)


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **"Damped mechanics" concept**: The hearing loss in PDB is caused by altered bone density and mass dampening the precise motion mechanics of both middle and inner ears — it is NOT typically due to nerve compression despite historical theories
> - **Otosclerosis mimic**: PDB may produce a low-frequency conductive hearing loss resembling otosclerosis, but surgery does NOT help — always check serum ALP in suspected otosclerosis patients with skull involvement
> - **Zolendronate single-shot**: A single IV dose provides sustained remission up to 6.5 years — this is the current gold standard (Grade A)
> - **BAHA in PDB**: Requires two-stage procedure due to bone hypervascularity — allows time for osseointegration
> - **Inferior cochlear vein occlusion**: Found in 78% of pagetic temporal bones, leading to stria vascularis changes and fluid homeostasis disturbance — an important but underrecognized mechanism
> - **Declining incidence**: PDB incidence is declining over time in Western countries — the reason remains unclear but reduced viral exposure is hypothesized
