# Vestibular Implants & Temporal Bone Radiographic Projections

**Question 1:** *Discuss middle ear implants. Write a note on vestibular implant.*

**Question 2:** *Enumerate temporal bone projections in conventional imaging.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2: Chapter 95 (Middle Ear Implants), Chapter 97 (Imaging of the Temporal Bone), Chapter 48 (Vestibular Physiology — Future Research)

---

## Introduction

The evolution of implantable hearing devices has transformed the management of sensorineural and conductive hearing loss. Middle ear implants (MEIs) represent an established technology that bypasses the limitations of conventional hearing aids, while vestibular implants are an emerging frontier — analogous to [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]]s for the vestibular system. Separately, imaging of the temporal bone has evolved from conventional radiographic projections to modern cross-sectional techniques. Although CT and MRI have largely replaced plain films in clinical practice, knowledge of conventional temporal bone projections remains relevant for understanding anatomical landmarks, post-operative assessment and resource-limited settings.

---

## PART A: Middle Ear Implants — Overview

### Definition and Rationale

Middle ear implants are surgically implantable devices that directly stimulate the ossicular chain or round window membrane, providing amplification for patients who cannot benefit from or tolerate conventional hearing aids.

### Classification of MEI by Transduction Method

| Type | Mechanism | Devices |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Electromagnetic | Magnet on ossicle vibrated by EM coil | VSB (Med-El), Maxum |
| Piezoelectric | Crystal deformation generates vibration | Envoy Esteem, MET/Carina |

### Vibrant Soundbridge (VSB) — Med-El

The VSB is the most widely used MEI worldwide. It consists of:

- **External component:** Audio processor worn behind the ear
- **Internal component:** Vibrating Ossicular Prosthesis (VORP) with a floating mass transducer (FMT)

The FMT can be coupled to various sites:

- Long process of incus (standard coupling)
- Round window membrane (RW vibroplasty)
- Stapes superstructure
- Oval window

**Indications:**

- Moderate to severe SNHL with inability to use conventional hearing aids (ear canal issues, feedback problems, cosmetic concerns)
- Mixed and conductive hearing loss (RW vibroplasty)
- Congenital aural atresia
- Chronic ear disease with CHL after tympanomastoid surgery

**Contraindications:**

- Retrocochlear pathology
- Fluctuating hearing loss
- Active middle ear disease (for ossicular coupling)

### Envoy Esteem — Fully Implantable

- Entirely subcutaneous — no external components
- Piezoelectric sensor on incus body detects sound vibrations
- Piezoelectric driver on stapes head delivers amplified signal
- Requires intact ossicular chain with incudostapedial joint disarticulation
- Battery replacement surgery required every 4.5-9 years

### MET/Carina — Cochlear

- Semi-implantable (MET) or fully implantable (Carina)
- Electromagnetic transducer coupled to incus body
- Carina device malfunction/failure rate approximately 17.6%

### Advantages of MEI over Conventional Hearing Aids

- No occlusion effect
- No feedback
- Improved sound quality and speech discrimination
- Cosmetically superior
- Suitable for patients with chronic ear canal disease

### Disadvantages

- Surgical procedure required with associated risks
- MRI compatibility issues (varies by device)
- Battery limitations in fully implantable devices
- High cost
- Revision surgery may be needed

## PART B: Vestibular Implant

### Concept and Rationale

The vestibular implant (VI) is an emerging neuro-prosthetic device designed to restore vestibular function in patients with bilateral vestibular loss, analogous to how cochlear implants restore hearing. Scott-Brown's identifies the development and implementation of vestibular implants as a key area of future research, modelled on the success of cochlear implants over the past decades.

### Principle of Operation

The vestibular implant works on the same fundamental principle as the cochlear implant — electrical stimulation of neural elements to restore sensory function:

- **Motion sensors (gyroscopes/accelerometers):** Detect head angular velocity in three planes, analogous to the function of the three semicircular canals
- **Signal processor:** Converts motion data into modulated electrical signals
- **Electrode array:** Implanted near the ampullary nerves of one or more semicircular canals to deliver rate-modulated electrical pulses
- **Stimulation strategy:** Baseline tonic pulse rate is modulated up or down based on head movement direction and velocity, mimicking normal vestibular afferent firing patterns

### Types of Vestibular Implant

| Type | Description | Status |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Semicircular canal VI | Electrodes in ampullae of SCCs | Research phase |
| Combined CI + VI | Cochlear implant with vestibular electrodes | Experimental |
| Otolith-targeted VI | Stimulation of utricle/saccule | Early concept |

### Indications (Proposed)

- Bilateral vestibular hypofunction/areflexia (bilateral vestibular loss — BVL)
- Ototoxic vestibular damage (e.g., gentamicin bilateral vestibulotoxicity)
- Bilateral [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]] with complete vestibular loss
- Autoimmune inner ear disease with bilateral vestibular failure
- Post-meningitis vestibular loss
- Idiopathic bilateral vestibulopathy

### Current Research Status

Key research groups and milestones:

- **Johns Hopkins University (Della Santina):** First multichannel vestibular prosthesis; demonstrated restoration of VOR in animal models; human trials with multichannel device
- **University of Geneva/Maastricht (Perez Fornos, Kingma):** Human trials with vestibular implant prototype based on modified cochlear implant platform
- **University of Washington (Rubinstein):** Vestibular pacemaker concept for Meniere's disease

### Challenges

- Precise electrode placement near ampullary nerves without damaging vestibular end-organs
- Current spread causing cross-talk between canal electrodes
- Simultaneous preservation of residual hearing
- Calibration of stimulation parameters to match natural vestibular coding
- Long-term biocompatibility and electrode-nerve interface stability
- Otolith function (linear acceleration sensing) is more difficult to replicate than canal function

### Differences Between Vestibular and Cochlear Implants

| Feature | Cochlear Implant | Vestibular Implant |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Target nerve | Cochlear nerve | Ampullary nerves (3 canals) |
| Stimulus | Sound → electrical | Head motion → electrical |
| Electrode location | Scala tympani | Near ampullary nerves |
| Channels | 12-22 electrodes | 3-6 (per canal pair) |
| Baseline activity | None required | Tonic firing rate needed |
| Bilateral need | Often unilateral | Usually bilateral loss |
| Clinical status | Established since 1980s | Investigational |

## PART C: Temporal Bone Projections in Conventional Imaging

### Overview

Conventional radiographic projections of the temporal bone were the primary imaging method before the advent of CT scanning. Although largely superseded by high-resolution CT and MRI, these projections remain relevant for:

- Post-operative cochlear implant assessment (Stenver's view for electrode position)
- Resource-limited settings where CT is unavailable
- Understanding anatomical relationships and surgical landmarks
- Historical and examination purposes

Scott-Brown's notes that "a post-operative X-ray (Stenver's view) helps to confirm electrode insertion, identify potential tip foldover" in cochlear implant surgery. Modern temporal bone imaging uses multidetector CT with reconstruction in the Pöschl plane (parallel to superior canal) and Stenver plane (perpendicular to superior canal).

### Standard Temporal Bone Radiographic Projections

| Projection | Beam Direction | Structures Shown | Clinical Use |
|------------|---------------|------------------|--------------|
| **Law's lateral** | Lateral oblique, 15° caudal | Mastoid air cells, antrum, EAC | Mastoid disease, pneumatization |
| **Towne's (AP)** | AP, 30° caudal tilt | Petrous apex, IAC, arcuate eminence | IAC widening, petrous lesions |
| **Stenver's** | PA oblique, 12° cephalad | Petrous ridge, SCC, IAC, cochlea | CI electrode position, IAC tumours |
| **Schuller's** | Lateral, 25-30° caudal | Mastoid air cells, tegmen, sigmoid sinus | Mastoiditis, [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]] |
| **Chausse III** | Submentovertex modified | Middle ear, attic, aditus, ossicles | Attic cholesteatoma, ossicular integrity |
| **Transorbital (Guillen)** | AP through orbit | IAC, vestibule, cochlea | IAC widening, [[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]] |
| **Submentovertex (SMV/Hirtz)** | Axial base of skull | Foramen ovale, spinosum, jugular | Skull base foramina, [[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]]s |

### Detailed Description of Key Projections

**1. Law's Lateral View**

The patient's head is placed with the affected side against the film cassette. The X-ray beam enters from the opposite side at a 15° caudal angle. This provides a lateral view of the mastoid with the petrous bones superimposed. It demonstrates:

- Degree of mastoid pneumatization (pneumatic, diploic, sclerotic)
- Mastoid antrum outline
- External auditory canal
- Tegmen tympani
- Sigmoid sinus plate

**2. Towne's View (Modified)**

The X-ray beam is directed anteroposteriorly with 30° caudal tilt. The petrous pyramids are projected into the lower orbits. This demonstrates:

- Internal auditory canal (IAC) — widening suggests vestibular schwannoma (> 2mm difference between sides is significant)
- Petrous apex
- Arcuate eminence
- Dorsum sellae

**3. Stenver's Projection**

An oblique posteroanterior projection that profiles the petrous pyramid along its long axis. The affected ear is placed closest to the film at a 45° rotation with 12° cephalad beam angulation. This demonstrates:

- IAC en face (excellent for comparing both sides)
- Superior semicircular canal
- Vestibule and cochlea
- Petrous ridge

This remains the standard projection for **post-operative cochlear implant X-ray** to confirm electrode position and identify tip foldover.

**4. Schuller's View**

A true lateral projection with 25-30° caudal beam angulation. This profiles the mastoid and demonstrates:

- Mastoid air cell pattern
- Tegmen tympani position
- Sigmoid sinus plate position
- Antral outline and extent of disease

**5. Chausse III View**

A specialized modified submentovertex projection that opens up the middle ear cleft and epitympanum (attic). It demonstrates:

- Attic (epitympanic recess) — useful for detecting cholesteatoma
- Ossicular chain (malleus head and incus body)
- Aditus ad antrum
- Scutum (lateral wall of attic)
- Prussak's space

### Modern Imaging — CT Reconstruction Planes

Modern temporal bone CT imaging uses thin section (0.5-0.6mm) multidetector CT with volume acquisition, reconstructed in specific planes:

- **Axial plane:** Standard for assessment of EAC, middle ear, mastoid
- **Coronal plane:** Tegmen, facial nerve canal, oval/round windows
- **Pöschl plane:** Parallel to superior semicircular canal — for SSCD assessment
- **Stenver plane:** Perpendicular to superior canal — for SSCD assessment

Key anatomical variants to assess on CT include tegmen height, sigmoid sinus position, jugular bulb height, carotid canal course and facial nerve canal dehiscence.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Middle ear implants use electromagnetic (VSB, Maxum) or piezoelectric (Esteem, Carina) transduction to directly stimulate the ossicular chain or round window membrane.
> 2. The VSB floating mass transducer can be coupled to the incus, round window, stapes or oval window — making it versatile for both SNHL and mixed/conductive hearing loss.
> 3. The vestibular implant is modelled on cochlear implant technology and aims to restore VOR by electrically stimulating ampullary nerves with rate-modulated pulses encoding head motion.
> 4. Vestibular implants require tonic baseline stimulation (unlike cochlear implants) to mimic the resting discharge rate of vestibular afferents.
> 5. Stenver's view remains clinically relevant as the standard post-operative X-ray for cochlear implant electrode position verification.
> 6. Law's and Schuller's views demonstrate mastoid pneumatization and are used for assessment of mastoiditis and cholesteatoma.
> 7. Towne's view profiles the IAC — a > 2mm difference between sides suggests vestibular schwannoma.
> 8. Chausse III view opens the attic (epitympanum) and demonstrates ossicles, scutum and Prussak's space — useful for cholesteatoma assessment.
> 9. Modern CT uses Pöschl (parallel to SSC) and Stenver (perpendicular to SSC) reconstruction planes for superior semicircular canal dehiscence evaluation.
> 10. High-resolution CT (0.5-0.6mm) and MRI with non-EPI DWI sequences have largely replaced conventional radiography for temporal bone imaging.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **The Envoy Esteem is the only fully implantable hearing device** with no external components — but requires battery replacement surgery every 4.5-9 years and incudostapedial joint disarticulation.
> - **Vestibular implant electrode placement** targets the ampullary nerves, not the hair cells — making it feasible even when peripheral vestibular end-organs are destroyed.
> - **Current spread (cross-talk)** between closely spaced vestibular electrodes remains the primary engineering challenge, causing unintended stimulation of adjacent canals.
> - **Stenver's view for CI** is taken with the implanted ear closest to the film at 45° rotation — a tip foldover on post-op X-ray requires surgical revision.
> - **Sclerotic mastoid on Schuller's/Law's view** suggests chronic otitis media with poorly pneumatized mastoid — important for surgical planning.
> - **CT has replaced plain films** for pre-operative temporal bone assessment due to superior anatomical detail — but plain films remain useful for post-operative CI follow-up and resource-limited settings.
