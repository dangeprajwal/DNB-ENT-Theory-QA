## INTRODUCTION

Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV) is the commonest cause of peripheral vertigo. It was first described clinically by Barany in 1921, and later clearly defined by Dix and Hallpike in 1952, who described the provoking manoeuvres and coined the term based on its benign (non-cancerous) origin and momentary (paroxysmal) nature. BPPV results from the displacement of degenerative otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) from the utricular macula into the semicircular canals. Despite being a benign condition, unrecognized or poorly treated BPPV causes significant morbidity, with recurrent attacks of vertigo affecting daily activities and quality of life.

---

## PART A: PATHOPHYSIOLOGY - CANALOLITHIASIS vs CUPULOLITHIASIS

### Two Theories of BPPV

**1. Canalolithiasis (Free-floating debris theory)**

In canalolithiasis, degenerative otoconial debris floats freely within the endolymph of the semicircular canal. When the head is moved into a provocative position, the otoconia fall to the lowest part of the canal under the influence of gravity. This creates a hydrodynamic "plunger effect" where the otoconial mass acts like a piston, generating endolymph flow that deflects the cupula. This theory explains the characteristic features of BPPV nystagmus, including:

- **Latency** (delay in onset of nystagmus) as the otoconia take time to settle
- **Crescendo-decrescendo pattern** as the debris accelerates then decelerates
- **Fatigability** with repeated positioning as debris disperses

Most cases of BPPV are compatible with the canalolithiasis theory.

**2. Cupulolithiasis (Cupula-adherent debris theory)**

In cupulolithiasis, degenerative otoconia become adherent to the cupula, rendering it gravity-sensitive. This was supported by post-mortem findings of basophilic deposits on the cupula. Cupulolithiasis produces atypical features:

- **No latency** or very short latency
- **Persistent nystagmus** that does not fatigue as long as the provocative position is maintained
- **Apogeotropic nystagmus** (beating away from the ground) in horizontal canal variants

### Clinical Differentiation Between Canalolithiasis and Cupulolithiasis

| Feature | Canalolithiasis | Cupulolithiasis |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Latency | Present (up to 5-30 sec) | Absent or minimal |
| Duration | Transient (<60 sec) | Persistent while in position |
| Fatigability | Yes, with repetition | No |
| Nystagmus direction | Geotropic (toward ground) | Apogeotropic (away from ground) |
| Mechanism | Free-floating debris | Debris adherent to cupula |

Canalolithiasis may convert to cupulolithiasis when free-floating debris becomes attached to the cupula.

### Canal Involvement

- **Posterior semicircular canal (p-SCC):** Most common (93-96.9% of cases)
- **Horizontal semicircular canal (h-SCC):** 5-11.9% of cases
- **Anterior semicircular canal (a-SCC):** 2-21.2% of cases

The posterior canal's propensity for particle accumulation is related to the size of the common crus, its dependent position below the utricle when both supine and erect, and the fact that debris in the superior canal more readily falls back into the utricle.

## PART B: AETIOLOGY AND CLINICAL FEATURES

### Aetiology

BPPV is idiopathic in 34-86% of cases. Associated conditions include:

- **[[Vestibular Neuritis|Vestibular neuritis]]:** 9.8-15.3% of BPPV cases
- **Head trauma:** 14.5-18% (most common identifiable cause; can involve more than one canal)
- **[[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]]:** 2-30% (hydropically induced damage to maculae)
- **[[Otosclerosis|Otosclerosis]]:** Up to 51% association; may occur post-[[Stapes Surgery|stapedectomy]]
- **Migrainous vertigo:** Atypical features help differentiate

Risk factors for an acute episode include prolonged bed rest, bending forward with head down, general anaesthesia, and supine or head-reclined positions. Osteopenia and post-menopausal status in females are also associated.

### Clinical Features

The hallmark of BPPV is brief episodes of vertigo lasting seconds (not minutes or hours), provoked by specific head positions:

- Lying down or sitting up from lying
- Rolling over in bed
- Extending or flexing the neck (e.g., looking up at a shelf)
- Bending forward

Attacks occur in clusters with several episodes per day. Between attacks, patients may be symptom-free or experience a persistent sensation of imbalance described as "walking on pillows," which may be attributed to underlying damage of the otolith organs.

### Natural Course

Most symptoms subside within a few weeks. Up to 30% of patients have symptoms persisting for months. BPPV is recurrent in 12.5% and persistent in 10% of cases. Recurrence increases with length of follow-up and is more likely with secondary causes.

## PART C: DIAGNOSIS

### Dix-Hallpike Manoeuvre (Posterior Canal BPPV)

The Dix-Hallpike test is the gold standard for diagnosing posterior canal BPPV:

- Patient seated on couch with feet up
- Head turned 45 degrees toward the side being tested
- Patient brought down briskly with head hanging over the end of the couch, 30 degrees below horizontal, while maintaining the 45-degree turn
- The manoeuvre stimulates the LARP (Left Anterior, Right Posterior) canals during a right Dix-Hallpike

**Positive response characteristics:**

- **Latency:** Delay in onset, typically less than 5 seconds but may be up to 20-30 seconds
- **Adaptation:** Nystagmus declines and disappears within approximately 1 minute
- **Fatigability:** Reduced intensity with repeated positioning
- **Torsional (rotatory) nystagmus** is the predominant component
- **Secondary upbeating component** is often observed

A variant Hallpike manoeuvre can be performed when couch placement is awkward, with the patient reaching a similar end position without hanging off the couch.

### Roll Test (Horizontal Canal BPPV)

- Patient supine with head flexed 30 degrees
- Head quickly rolled to each side

**Geotropic nystagmus** (toward the tested ear) suggests canalolithiasis. Brisker nystagmus occurs with ampullopetal stimulation. **Apogeotropic nystagmus** (away from the tested ear) suggests cupulolithiasis.

### Anterior Canal BPPV

- The left Dix-Hallpike provokes right anterior canal BPPV (coplanar with right posterior canal)
- Produces **downbeating nystagmus** with or without a torsional component
- Less right-left specificity than posterior canal BPPV

### Frenzel Glasses

The classical descriptions of BPPV by Dix and Hallpike were made without Frenzel glasses. The torsional component of posterior canal BPPV nystagmus is less suppressed by visual fixation. No consistent increase in diagnostic efficacy with Frenzel glasses has been demonstrated despite their common use.

### Subjective BPPV

In some patients, positional vertigo occurs without detectable nystagmus. Treatment side is chosen based on symptom provocation. Recovery rates are lower: 60% with modified Epley compared to 90% with typical BPPV.

## PART D: HINTS EXAMINATION

The **HINTS** protocol (Head Impulse, Nystagmus characteristics, Test for Skew) is a bedside oculomotor examination used to differentiate peripheral from central causes of acute vestibular syndrome. It is particularly valuable in the acute setting.

### Components of HINTS

**1. Head Impulse Test (HIT)**

The patient fixates on a target while the examiner turns the head briskly in discrete steps of 10-15 degrees. A patient with unilateral vestibular loss will exhibit one or more catch-up saccades toward the target. This test is extremely useful in confirming that an acute vestibular syndrome is of peripheral origin (abnormal result in vestibular neuritis). The overall sensitivity of the head-impulse test is 34% for all canal paresis, but specificity is 100%, and all patients with canal paresis of 87% or greater had a positive result.

**2. Nystagmus Characteristics**

- **Peripheral:** Unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, suppressed by fixation
- **Central:** Bidirectional, purely vertical or torsional, not suppressed by fixation

**3. Test for Skew Deviation**

Vertical misalignment of the eyes (skew deviation) is indicative of a central lesion, particularly brainstem stroke.

### Clinical Significance

Combining the head-impulse test with other central oculomotor abnormalities (skew eye deviation, bidirectional nystagmus) is, in experienced hands, more sensitive than diffusion-weighted MRI for the diagnosis of acute vertebrobasilar stroke. However, clinicians should not forget other red flags suggestive of stroke.

## PART E: DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

### Central vs Peripheral Positional Vertigo

The most reliable distinguishing criterion is the direction of nystagmus in relation to the plane expected from the stimulated canal.

| Feature | Peripheral (BPPV) | Central Positional Vertigo |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Latency | Present | Absent |
| Duration | Transient (<60 sec) | Persistent |
| Fatigability | Yes | No |
| Nystagmus type | Torsional + vertical | Purely vertical/torsional |
| Course | Paroxysmal | Monophasic/persistent |
| Associated signs | None | Brainstem/cerebellar signs |

### Conditions in Differential Diagnosis

- Vestibular migraine
- Vertebrobasilar insufficiency
- Meniere's disease
- Vestibular neuritis / labyrinthitis
- Superior canal dehiscence syndrome
- Demyelinating lesions and CNS lesions (pontomedullary brainstem, vestibulocerebellum)
- Anxiety disorders and postural hypotension

### Red Flags Requiring Imaging

- Atypical nystagmus pattern
- Associated brainstem or cerebellar signs
- Failure to respond to repeated therapeutic manoeuvres

Central lesions causing positional vertigo are typically located in the pontomedullary brainstem or vestibulocerebellum (dorsolateral to the fourth ventricle, dorsal vermis).

## PART F: MANAGEMENT - REPOSITIONING MANOEUVRES

### 1. Epley's Canalith Repositioning Procedure (CRP) - Posterior Canal BPPV

Developed by Epley in 1992, this is based on the canalolithiasis theory and aims to move particles from the posterior canal to the utricle via the common crus. It is recommended by both the American Academy of Neurology and the American Academy of Otolaryngology.

**Steps (Left Posterior Canal):**

1. Patient seated, head turned 45 degrees to affected (left) side
2. Brought down rapidly with head still 45 degrees to affected side, extended over edge of couch
3. Head turned 90 degrees to opposite (right) side
4. Body rotated 90 degrees so patient lies on the right side
5. Head rotated further 90 degrees so patient looks obliquely downwards
6. Patient returns to sitting with head turned 45 degrees to unaffected side
7. Final position: sitting with head forward 20 degrees

Each position is maintained for 30 seconds. The manoeuvre is repeated until no nystagmus is provoked. It can follow directly from the Dix-Hallpike test.

**Efficacy:** Cochrane meta-analysis shows approximately 80% efficacy. Total resolution in 90% of patients.

**Modified Epley for self-treatment** achieves 64% success after 1 week compared to 23% with Brandt-Daroff exercises.

### 2. Semont's Liberatory Manoeuvre - Posterior Canal BPPV

- Patient placed on affected side from seated position, face turned up 45 degrees away from affected canal
- Quickly swung through sitting (no pause) to opposite side, face down 45 degrees
- Head position relative to shoulder is unchanged during the manoeuvre
- Maintained for 5 minutes, then slowly resume sitting
- 92% symptom resolution reported with single or multiple treatments
- Not conclusively superior to CRP; may be easier to perform

### 3. Brandt-Daroff Positional Exercises

Proposed in 1980, based on Schuknecht's cupulolithiasis hypothesis. These are mechanical self-treatment exercises involving rapid lateral head/body tilts from the sitting position:

- Rapid move to challenging position (lying on affected side, nose 45 degrees up)
- Remain 30 seconds until vertigo subsides
- Sit up 30 seconds
- Opposite side position for 30 seconds
- Sit up again
- Repeat for 15 minutes, three times daily

Initially reported high success rates, but controlled trials show only 23% resolution after 1 week. Involves dozens of self-induced vertigo attacks and is no longer first-line treatment. It is an alternative for patients with cervical problems who cannot tolerate Epley or Semont manoeuvres.

### 4. Horizontal Canal BPPV - Management Options

**Forced Prolonged Position on Healthy Side:**
- Lie on healthy side for 12 hours to allow debris to gravitate into the vestibule
- Total recovery within 3 days in 74.3% of treated patients

**270-Degree Barbecue Manoeuvre:**
- Three 90-degree step rotations (total 270 degrees) toward the unaffected ear from the supine position
- Achieves immediate results
- Rotation within half a second; positions maintained 30-60 seconds

**360-Degree Yaw Rotation:**
- 360-degree rotation in 90-degree steps at 30-second intervals
- 89% success rate in geotropic horizontal canal BPPV

**Gufoni Manoeuvre (Geotropic nystagmus):**
- Patient seated upright, rapidly tilted to unaffected side
- Swift 45-degree downward head turn (nose down)
- Few minutes later resume seated position

**Gufoni for Apogeotropic Nystagmus:**
- Lie on affected side, followed by upward head turn

A prospective RCT of 179 patients with geotropic h-BPPV found both barbecue rotation and Gufoni manoeuvre were superior to sham, with no significant difference between the two procedures.

### 5. Anterior Canal BPPV - Modified Epley

- From seated position to head-hanging position 30 degrees below horizontal
- Maintained 30 seconds, then chin-to-chest position for 30 seconds
- Then seated position for 30 seconds
- 84.6% success after single manoeuvre; 100% with repeated manoeuvres

### Post-Treatment Considerations

- Limited evidence supports postural restrictions (upright positioning and limited head movement for 24-48 hours)
- Mastoid oscillation has not been shown to consistently improve efficacy
- Head trauma BPPV responds less well to treatment than idiopathic BPPV
- Conversion of p-BPPV to a-BPPV or h-BPPV occurs in approximately 6% of treated patients

## PART G: SURGICAL TREATMENT (Refractory Cases)

### Posterior Semicircular Canal Occlusion

In a series of 5364 patients over 20 years, 53 who failed repositioning manoeuvres underwent p-SCC occlusion. All were cured of vertigo. Complications included permanent hearing loss in 9 patients, objective balance dysfunction on caloric testing in 10, subjective imbalance in 5, and later development of h-BPPV in the same ear in 2 patients.

### Singular Neurectomy

Gacek advocated the transcanal approach for chronic disabling vertigo of more than 1 year duration. In 242 patients over 3 decades, 96.8% achieved complete relief (negative Dix-Hallpike). Sensorineural hearing loss occurred in 3.7% (9 patients).

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. BPPV is the commonest cause of peripheral vertigo, resulting from displaced otoconia in the semicircular canals
> 2. Posterior canal is affected in 93-96.9% of cases; horizontal canal in 5-11.9%
> 3. Canalolithiasis (free-floating debris) is the predominant mechanism; cupulolithiasis (adherent debris) explains atypical cases
> 4. Canalolithiasis causes latency, fatigability, and geotropic nystagmus; cupulolithiasis causes persistent, apogeotropic nystagmus
> 5. Dix-Hallpike is the gold standard for posterior canal BPPV diagnosis; Roll test for horizontal canal BPPV
> 6. HINTS protocol (Head Impulse, Nystagmus characteristics, Test for Skew) differentiates peripheral from central causes of acute vertigo and is more sensitive than DWI-MRI for vertebrobasilar stroke
> 7. Epley CRP is the first-line treatment for posterior canal BPPV with approximately 80% efficacy (Cochrane meta-analysis)
> 8. Brandt-Daroff exercises have only 23% resolution at 1 week and are no longer first-line therapy
> 9. Barbecue and Gufoni manoeuvres are effective for horizontal canal BPPV with no significant difference between them
> 10. Red flags requiring imaging include atypical nystagmus, brainstem/cerebellar signs, and failure to respond to repeated manoeuvres
> 11. Posterior canal occlusion is reserved for refractory cases failing repeated repositioning manoeuvres
> 12. BPPV is idiopathic in 34-86%; recurrence occurs in 12.5% and is more likely with secondary causes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Torsional nystagmus is the critical diagnostic component of posterior canal BPPV and is less suppressed by visual fixation, hence Frenzel glasses are not strictly necessary for diagnosis
> 2. The Epley CRP can follow directly from the Dix-Hallpike test without repositioning the patient
> 3. Head trauma BPPV responds less well to treatment than idiopathic BPPV and may involve more than one canal simultaneously
> 4. In horizontal canal BPPV, forced prolonged position on the healthy side for 12 hours achieves 74.3% recovery within 3 days
> 5. Conversion from p-BPPV to h-BPPV or a-BPPV occurs in approximately 6% of treated cases and may require additional targeted repositioning
> 6. Subjective BPPV (positional vertigo without detectable nystagmus) has lower treatment success rates (60% vs 90%) with modified Epley
