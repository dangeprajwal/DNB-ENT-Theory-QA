# Meniere's Disease: Pathogenesis and Intratympanic Therapy

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the difference between Meniere's disease and Meniere's syndrome. Discuss the aetiopathogenesis of Meniere's disease. Discuss the role of intratympanic drug therapy for the treatment of Meniere's disease.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 63

---

## INTRODUCTION

Meniere's disease is an idiopathic inner ear disorder characterized by recurrent spontaneous vertigo accompanied by fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and aural fullness. The histopathological correlate is endolymphatic hydrops, first described independently by Hallpike and Cairns, and Yamakawa in 1938. Understanding the distinction between Meniere's disease and syndrome, along with the underlying pathogenesis, is essential for appropriate management, particularly the role of intratympanic drug therapy in refractory cases.

---

## PART A: MENIERE'S DISEASE VS MENIERE'S SYNDROME

### Key Distinction

Meniere's disease is defined as the idiopathic syndrome of endolymphatic hydrops where no underlying cause can be identified despite thorough investigation. The diagnosis is made by exclusion of all identifiable causes. In contrast, Meniere's syndrome refers to secondary endolymphatic hydrops where a similar clustering of symptoms occurs but with an identifiable underlying aetiology.

**Meniere's Disease (Idiopathic Endolymphatic Hydrops):**

- Idiopathic by definition
- Diagnosis made by exclusion of all identifiable causes
- Endolymphatic hydrops present on histopathology
- Requires systematic otovestibular history and thorough workup
- 2015 Barany Society guidelines provide diagnostic criteria (definite and probable)

**Meniere's Syndrome (Secondary Endolymphatic Hydrops):**

Causes include:

- **Infectious:** Syphilis, viral infections, bacterial meningitis with labyrinthitis
- **Traumatic:** Head trauma, temporal bone trauma
- **Autoimmune:** Cogan's syndrome, other autoimmune inner ear disorders
- **Inflammatory:** Viral, bacterial or spirochaetal inflammation
- **Congenital:** Inner ear malformations (embryopathic symptomatic endolymphatic hydrops)
- **Post-surgical:** Previous ear surgery

**Delayed Meniere's Disease:** According to 2015 guidelines, this term is preferred over "delayed hydrops" in cases where sensorineural hearing loss antedates the vertigo spells, typically following trauma or inflammation.

## PART B: AETIOPATHOGENESIS OF MENIERE'S DISEASE

### Endolymphatic Hydrops Theory

The pathophysiological basis of Meniere's disease remains centred on endolymphatic hydrops, representing distension of the endolymphatic compartment. Reissner's membrane accommodates large increases in endolymph volume by swelling outwards into the scala vestibuli before rupturing.

**Evidence supporting hydrops:**

- Endolymphatic hydrops was observed in all temporal bones studied in deceased patients with Meniere's disease
- Electrocochleography demonstrates large summating potential in 50-70% of patients, which can be reduced with administration of hyperosmotic substances
- Delayed MRI after intratympanic gadolinium visualizes hydrops in vivo and reveals bilateral hydrops quite frequently in unilateral Meniere's disease
- Anatomical variations in the temporal bone (identified on cone-beam CT) may predispose for blockage of the endolymphatic canal

**Controversies:**

- Endolymphatic hydrops was observed in a significant number of temporal bones from patients with hearing loss without classical Meniere's symptoms
- Merchant et al. observed clear histologic examples of membrane ruptures in patients without a history of vertigo
- Diuretics do not unequivocally lead to improvement of symptoms and/or hydrops
- Merchant et al. proposed that hydrops may be a marker for disordered inner ear homeostasis in which a yet unknown factor produces both clinical symptoms and hydrops, rather than being the direct cause

### Aetiological Factors

**Genetic Factors:**

Multiple candidate genes have been proposed, many linked to inner ear ion and water transportation:

- **AQP2:** Aquaporin water channel gene
- **KCNE1, KCNE3:** Potassium channel genes
- **ADD1:** Sodium-potassium pump activity regulator
- **COCH:** Cochlin protein production (extracellular matrix); also known as DFNA9, leads to progressive non-syndromic deafness and bilateral vestibular areflexia
- **HCFC1:** Host cell factor C1, involved in cell cycle control and transcriptional regulation during herpes simplex virus infection
- **HSPA1A:** Single nucleotide polymorphisms of the heat-shock protein 70 gene
- **PTPN22:** Encodes a lymphoid protein phosphatase
- **IL1:** Interleukin-1 gene

Familial cases constitute 5-15% of all Meniere's disease. These patients show higher prevalence of autoimmune diseases, longer spells of vertigo, and the familial form is more prone to bilateral presentation during a lifetime.

**Autoimmune Factor:**

In approximately one in three Meniere's disease patients there seems to be an autoimmune component. Proposed mechanisms include cross-reactions between similar antigens, innocent bystander damage due to cytokine release, acquired intolerance to inner ear antigens (previously covert from the immunological system and exposed by trauma or infection), and genetic factors involving extended major histocompatibility complex haplotypes.

**Viral Factor:**

Herpes simplex virus is most frequently implicated. Viral structures have been observed by transmission electron microscopy in vestibular ganglion cells. Vertigo control in 91% of Meniere's disease patients by means of acyclovir treatment supports the potential viral aetiology.

**Allergic Factor:**

Derebery observed vertigo control (Class A and B) in 47.9% (n=137) of patients accepting allergy treatment. Potential mechanisms include the endolymphatic sac being targeted by the allergic reaction, deposition of circulating immune complexes in the fenestrated blood vessels of the endolymphatic sac and stria vascularis, and viral antigen-allergic interaction with T-cell homing to the endolymphatic sac.

**Vascular Factor:**

Endolymphatic hydrops has been observed to impair blood flow autoregulation. The combination of endolymphatic hydrops and venous obstruction has been shown to induce vertigo attacks similar to Meniere's disease in an animal model.

## PART C: INTRATYMPANIC DRUG THERAPY

### Rationale for Intratympanic Route

The round window membrane is semipermeable and participates in absorption and secretion of perilymph, enabling direct drug delivery to the inner ear. This approach achieves higher intralabyrinthine concentrations with reduced systemic side effects. Aminoglycoside antibiotics applied to the middle ear cavity cross the round and oval window membranes into the perilymphatic spaces of the inner ear.

### Intratympanic Corticosteroids

**Mechanism:** Corticosteroids act on inner ear homeostasis via transmembrane water transporters called aquaporins, reducing endolymphatic hydrops.

**Evidence:** Based on a 2011 Cochrane analysis by Phillips and Westerberg, one randomized placebo-controlled double-blind trial was identified comparing intratympanic dexamethasone to saline over 24 months. Substantial vertigo control (Class A and B) was achieved in 100% (11/11) of the treatment group versus 57% (4/7) in the control group. The Cochrane analysis concluded that limited evidence supports effectiveness.

**Clinical Application:**

- **Indication:** Salvage treatment after failure of dietary restrictions and medical therapy for 6 months
- **Outcome:** 80-100% substantial long-term vertigo control in refractory cases
- **Hearing:** No significant hearing loss
- **Recommendation Grade:** A (repetitive intratympanic injections)

### Intratympanic Gentamicin

**Mechanism:** Aminoglycosides cause vestibulotoxicity through multiple pathways including excitotoxicity via NMDA receptor binding and production of reactive oxygen species. The antioxidant N-acetylcysteine may provide protection. The effect is partial chemolabyrinthectomy - destruction of vestibular hair cell function. The typical Meniere's disease vertigo attacks are not detected and do not provoke nystagmus after treatment.

**Historical Note:** First reported by Schuknecht in 1956.

**Evidence:** Based on a 2011 Cochrane analysis by Pullens et al., two randomized placebo-controlled trials demonstrated the beneficial effect. Meta-analysis by Huon et al. (2012) confirmed 87.5% Class A and B vertigo control, with sensorineural hearing loss in 0-38.7% of cases. A randomized controlled trial by Casani et al. (2012) showed intratympanic gentamicin achieved 93.5% (30/32) vertigo control at 2-year follow-up compared to 61% (17/28) with intratympanic dexamethasone.

**Clinical Application:**

- **Indication:** Refractory cases after intratympanic steroids; end-stage patients with drop attacks (Tumarkin crises)
- **Vertigo Control:** 87.5-93.5% (Class A and B)
- **Complication:** SNHL in 0-38.7% of cases
- **Protocol:** As-needed protocol recommended
- **Recommendation Grade:** A

**Patient Counselling:** The patient may develop some imbalance or visual-vestibular mismatch after treatment. The risk of hearing loss must be discussed.

### Transtympanic Low-Pressure Therapy (Meniett)

**Mechanism:** Based on reports of subjective improvement of acute vertigo attacks during pressure chamber exposure. The Meniett device produces repetitive 0.6-second pulses of low pressure (0-20 cm H2O) transmitted through a ventilation tube. Treatment consists of three to four cycles of 5-minute sequences.

**Evidence:** Cochrane review by Van Sonsbeek et al. (2015) concluded there is no evidence to demonstrate effectiveness.

### Treatment Algorithm

**1st Line (All patients):** Dietary modification with salt restriction, betahistine 16 mg TDS, diuretics if needed.

**2nd Line (After 6 months medical failure):** Intratympanic dexamethasone, repetitive injections. Grade A recommendation.

**3rd Line (Refractory after steroids):** Intratympanic gentamicin using as-needed protocol. Grade A recommendation.

**4th Line (Intact hearing, failed gentamicin):** Selective vestibular nerve section.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Meniere's disease = idiopathic endolymphatic hydrops; Meniere's syndrome = secondary causes (syphilis, trauma, autoimmune)
> 2. Endolymphatic hydrops found in 100% of temporal bones from confirmed Meniere's patients
> 3. Hydrops may be a marker for disordered homeostasis rather than the direct cause (Merchant hypothesis)
> 4. Multiple genes implicated: AQP2, KCNE1/3, COCH (DFNA9), HCFC1, PTPN22, IL1, ADD1, HSPA1A
> 5. Autoimmune factor present in approximately 1 in 3 patients
> 6. 91% vertigo control reported with acyclovir (viral theory); 47.9% with allergy treatment
> 7. 5-15% of cases are familial, more prone to bilateral presentation and autoimmune diseases
> 8. Round window membrane is semipermeable - enables intratympanic drug delivery to perilymph
> 9. IT dexamethasone: 80-100% vertigo control, no hearing loss, Grade A recommendation
> 10. IT gentamicin: 87.5-93.5% vertigo control, 0-38.7% SNHL risk, Grade A recommendation
> 11. Gentamicin causes partial chemolabyrinthectomy via NMDA excitotoxicity and reactive oxygen species
> 12. Meniett device: no evidence of effectiveness (Cochrane 2015)


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Meniere's disease is idiopathic by definition - all secondary causes must be excluded before the diagnosis is made
> - Delayed MRI with intratympanic gadolinium provides an in vivo surrogate for histopathologic confirmation of endolymphatic hydrops
> - Aquaporins (transmembrane water transporters) are the target of corticosteroid action in the inner ear
> - N-acetylcysteine may protect against gentamicin ototoxicity via its antioxidant properties
> - Always try intratympanic steroids before gentamicin - 6 months of conservative treatment is required before escalation
> - Drop attacks (Tumarkin crises) are a specific indication for intratympanic gentamicin
