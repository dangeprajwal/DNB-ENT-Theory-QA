# VEMP and [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's Disease]]

> [!question] Questions
> What is VEMP? What is its role in ENT practice? Describe clinical features, evaluation and management of Meniere's disease.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapters 62 & 63

---

## INTRODUCTION

Vestibular evoked myogenic potentials (VEMPs) are sound-evoked vestibulospinal reflexes that provide a clinical assessment of otolith organ function. They have emerged as valuable tools in the neuro-otological evaluation of patients with vestibular disorders. Meniere's disease is an idiopathic inner ear disorder characterized by recurrent spontaneous vertigo accompanied by fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and aural fullness, with endolymphatic hydrops as its histopathological correlate.

---

## PART A: VESTIBULAR EVOKED MYOGENIC POTENTIALS (VEMP)

### Definition and Mechanism

Loud sounds of brief duration (clicks) can activate the vestibular labyrinth while delivering minimal total energy to the ear. Using averaging techniques similar to brainstem auditory evoked potentials, an electromyographic (EMG) potential can be identified from neck or periorbital muscles. The response is a sound-evoked vestibulocollic (vestibulospinal) reflex. All available evidence indicates that the main inner ear structure activated by sound is the saccule, making VEMP essentially an otolith function test.

The technique can be easily set up in any clinical neurophysiology or audiology department with access to calibrated sound generators, averaging equipment and surface recording electrodes. Several devices are available commercially. A useful feature of this procedure is that it can investigate each ear separately.

### Types of VEMP

**Cervical VEMP (cVEMP):**

Cervical VEMPs are short-latency relaxation potentials recorded by surface electrodes placed on the skin over the ipsilateral sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle when it is tonically contracted. They are recorded in response to high-intensity narrow-band clicks or tone bursts. The main response is an ipsilateral biphasic waveform with a positive peak at approximately 13 ms (P13) and a negative deflection at approximately 23 ms (N23). The afferent fibres of this reflex travel in the inferior vestibular nerve, and the cVEMP primarily tests saccular function.

**Ocular VEMP (oVEMP):**

Short-latency ocular EMG potentials recorded with periorbital surface electrodes in response to air-conducted sound and bone vibration have been elicited, termed ocular VEMPs (oVEMPs). They are thought to represent a crossed excitatory utriculo-ocular response, hence a functional test of the superior vestibular nerve (as opposed to cVEMPs which test the inferior vestibular nerve). oVEMPs test utricular function.

### Effect of Hearing Loss on VEMP

- **Conductive hearing loss** interferes with or abolishes the response by obstructing clicks from reaching the labyrinth. Pre-test otoscopy and pure-tone audiogram are mandatory if the response is absent.
- **Sensorineural hearing loss** does not interfere with the cVEMP, as expected from a genuine vestibular response (vestibular-sparing).

### Role of VEMP in ENT Practice

**Primary clinical application - Superior semicircular canal dehiscence syndrome (SSCDS):**

The main practical diagnostic use is for the diagnosis of SSCDS, or more generally the Tullio phenomenon. These patients have lower threshold and higher amplitude potentials from the symptomatic ear only, even when the radiological abnormality is bilateral. Sensitivity and specificity of oVEMPs for SSCDS are reported as greater than 90%, and their use has been suggested as a screening tool prior to high-resolution CT.

**Other clinical applications:**

- **[[Vestibular Neuritis|Vestibular neuritis]] evaluation:** oVEMP and cVEMP can determine which branch of the vestibular nerve is affected. When the superior nerve is affected, oVEMP is abnormal and cVEMP is normal. When the inferior vestibular nerve is affected, the oVEMP is normal and the cVEMP is abnormal (increased asymmetry ratio and reduced or absent amplitude response).
- **Predicting secondary [[BPPV|BPPV]] after vestibular neuritis:** Patients with acute vestibular neuritis and absent caloric and cVEMP do not develop secondary BPPV. This suggests that absent cVEMP indicates loss of inferior vestibular nerve afferents which carry the macular fibres from the posterior semicircular canal; a deafferented posterior canal cannot produce BPPV symptoms.
- **[[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]] evaluation:** In patients with acoustic neuromas, an abnormal VEMP can occasionally be the only vestibular finding.
- **Inferior vestibular neuritis:** Selective loss of posterior semicircular canal function as shown by absent cVEMP is suggestive of selective inferior nerve vestibular neuritis.

**Limitations:**

The meaning of an isolated VEMP abnormality is unclear, particularly in patients older than 40 years of age. Ideally, absent VEMPs would imply a selective otolithic abnormality, but this does not always seem to be the case.

## PART B: MENIERE'S DISEASE

### Definition

Meniere's disease is an idiopathic inner ear disorder characterized by recurrent spontaneous vertigo accompanied by fluctuating or progressive sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus and aural fullness in the affected ear. The histopathological correlate is highly linked to endolymphatic hydrops, a finding which is not pathognomonic to Meniere's disease but more likely a marker for overall disordered inner-ear homeostasis. Meniere's disease is, by definition, the idiopathic type of endolymphatic hydrops. A similar clustering of symptoms caused by secondary causes (syphilis, trauma, autoimmune disorders) is termed Meniere's syndrome.

### Epidemiology

- **Prevalence:** 43.2 per 100,000 persons
- **Incidence:** 4.3 definite cases per 100,000 persons per year
- **Age of onset:** Second to sixth decade; only 1-7% in the paediatric population; 9% of patients experience onset at age 65 or more with a higher incidence of drop attacks
- **Sex:** No difference in female-to-male ratio
- **Familial:** 5-15% of cases are familial, with higher prevalence of autoimmune diseases, longer spells of vertigo, and more prone to bilateral presentation

### Clinical Features

**Classic Tetrad:**

- **Vertigo** - Recurrent spontaneous episodes lasting 20 minutes to 12 hours; most incapacitating symptom
- **Hearing loss** - Fluctuating or progressive low-frequency sensorineural type
- **Tinnitus** - Fluctuating, often low-pitched roaring quality
- **Aural fullness** - Fluctuating sensation of pressure in the affected ear

**Attack Phases:**

A typical attack of Meniere's disease can be defined by the direction of the spontaneous nystagmus. In the irritative phase, the nystagmus beats towards the affected ear in a horizontal or horizontal-torsional direction, usually lasting less than 1 hour. In the paretic phase, the nystagmus beats away from the affected ear and lasts hours to days. In the recovery phase, the nystagmus again beats towards the affected side as peripheral vestibular function recovers.

**Drop Attacks (Tumarkin Crises):**

Particularly in the later stages of Meniere's disease, drop attacks can develop. These may be related to acute otolithic dysfunction. The patient suddenly drops to the ground without losing consciousness and without associated vertigo. They indicate end-stage disease.

### Diagnostic Criteria (2015 Barany Society Guidelines)

| Category | Criteria |
|----------|----------|
| Definite Meniere's | Two or more spontaneous vertigo episodes (20 min-12 hrs) + audiometrically documented low-frequency SNHL + fluctuating aural symptoms |
| Probable Meniere's | Two or more vertigo/dizziness episodes (20 min-24 hrs) + fluctuating aural symptoms |

**Low-frequency SNHL definition:** Increases in pure-tone thresholds for bone-conducted sound that are higher (worse) in the affected ear than the contralateral ear by at least 30 dBHL at each of two contiguous frequencies below 2 kHz.

**Staging system (1995 AAO-HNS):**

| Stage | PTA (0.5-1-2-3 kHz) |
|-------|---------------------|
| 1 | 25 dB or less |
| 2 | 26-40 dB |
| 3 | 41-70 dB |
| 4 | More than 70 dB |

### Evaluation

**Clinical Assessment:**

- Systematic otovestibular history including family history and personal drug, trauma, surgery and disease history
- Complete head and neck examination including neuro-otological examination
- Otoscopy to rule out middle ear pathology

**Audiological Investigations:**

- Liminal and speech audiometry - essential to support the diagnosis of definite Meniere's disease; low-frequency SNHL is the hallmark finding
- Tympanometry to exclude middle ear pathology

**Electrophysiological Tests:**

- Electrocochleography (ECoG) - demonstrates large summating potential (increased SP/AP ratio) in 50-70% of Meniere's patients; can be reduced with administration of hyperosmotic substances
- Cochlear hydrops analysis masking procedure (CHAMP) - measures travelling wave velocity changes

**Imaging:**

- MRI posterior fossa with gadolinium - excludes CPA lesions, vestibular schwannoma, inner ear malformations, neurovascular conflicts and white matter lesions
- CT temporal bone - identifies anatomical variations that may predispose for blockage of the endolymphatic canal
- Delayed MRI after intratympanic gadolinium - visualizes endolymphatic hydrops in vivo; provides surrogate for histopathologic confirmation; reveals bilateral hydrops quite frequently in unilateral Meniere's disease

### Management

**1st Line: Dietary Modification and Medication**

Low-sodium and caffeine-free diet has a statistically significant effect on functional level. Patients compliant for longer than 6 months had larger improvement in vertigo frequency and higher rate of Class A/B vertigo outcome.

Betahistine dihydrochloride 16 mg thrice daily is a histamine H1 agonist and H3 antagonist with limited side-effect profile. High-dose betahistine up to 480 mg/day may be used for non-responders. Diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide, acetazolamide) may be added. Cochrane analysis found insufficient good evidence for both betahistine and diuretics, but they remain commonly used first-line agents.

**2nd Line: Intratympanic Corticosteroids (after 6 months medical failure)**

Mechanism involves inner ear homeostasis via aquaporins (transmembrane water transporters). Randomized placebo-controlled trial showed 100% (11/11) substantial vertigo control (Class A and B) in treatment group versus 57% (4/7) in control group at 24 months. Overall 80-100% substantial long-term vertigo control in refractory cases with no significant hearing loss. Recommendation grade A.

**3rd Line: Intratympanic Gentamicin (refractory after steroids)**

Causes partial chemolabyrinthectomy via vestibulotoxicity (destroys vestibular hair cells). Meta-analysis demonstrated 87.5% Class A and B vertigo control. RCT showed 93.5% (30/32) vertigo control at 2-year follow-up compared to 61% (17/28) with intratympanic dexamethasone. SNHL risk: 0-38.7% of cases. Also indicated for end-stage patients with drop attacks (Tumarkin crises). Recommendation grade A.

**4th Line: Selective Vestibular Nerve Section**

| Procedure | Vertigo Control | Hearing Preservation |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------|
| Endolymphatic sac surgery | 76-82% | 70-73% |
| Selective vestibular nerve section | 90-96% | 72-74% |
| Meniett device | No evidence (Cochrane 2015) | N/A |

Selective vestibular nerve section is considered an alternative to intratympanic gentamicin in cases of intact hearing or as salvage after gentamicin failure. Insufficient evidence supports endolymphatic sac surgery, transtympanic low-pressure therapy (Meniett) or tenotomy of the tensor tympani and stapedius muscles' tendons.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. VEMP is a sound-evoked vestibulospinal reflex testing otolith function; the main structure activated is the saccule
> 2. cVEMP tests saccular function via inferior vestibular nerve (P13/N23 waveform from SCM); oVEMP tests utricular function via superior vestibular nerve
> 3. Main clinical use of VEMP is diagnosis of superior semicircular canal dehiscence; oVEMP sensitivity and specificity greater than 90% for SSCDS
> 4. Conductive hearing loss interferes with VEMP; sensorineural hearing loss does not
> 5. Absent cVEMP after vestibular neuritis predicts absence of secondary BPPV (deafferented posterior canal)
> 6. Meniere's disease prevalence 43.2/100,000; incidence 4.3/100,000/year
> 7. Classic tetrad: vertigo (20 min-12 hrs), fluctuating low-frequency SNHL, tinnitus, aural fullness
> 8. Meniere's disease = idiopathic hydrops; Meniere's syndrome = secondary causes (syphilis, trauma, autoimmune)
> 9. ECoG shows increased summating potential in 50-70%; delayed MRI with gadolinium visualizes hydrops in vivo
> 10. First-line: dietary modification + betahistine 16 mg TDS with or without diuretics
> 11. IT dexamethasone: 80-100% vertigo control, no hearing loss, Grade A recommendation
> 12. IT gentamicin: 87.5-93.5% control, 0-38.7% SNHL risk, Grade A; also indicated for drop attacks


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Low-frequency SNHL is the hallmark audiological finding; demonstration of recovery on serial audiograms further supports the diagnosis
> - The 2015 Barany Society guidelines provide two categories: definite and probable Meniere's disease
> - Compliance with dietary modification for more than 6 months significantly improves vertigo outcomes
> - Always try intratympanic steroids before gentamicin to preserve hearing
> - Delayed MRI with intratympanic gadolinium is the in vivo biomarker for endolymphatic hydrops
> - Drop attacks (Tumarkin crises) indicate end-stage disease and are an indication for intratympanic gentamicin
