# Vestibular Migraine

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Discuss vestibular migraine (migrainous vertigo) including its classification, clinical features, diagnosis, and management.


Source: Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery, Volume 2, Chapter 67

---

## INTRODUCTION

Vestibular migraine (VM) is a subtype of migraine in which vestibular symptoms predominate. It represents the convergence of two common conditions: migraine and vestibular dysfunction. The understanding of the spectrum of migraine manifestations has increased rapidly, and vestibular migraine is now recognized as one of the most common causes of episodic vertigo.

Migraine is primarily an episodic disorder defined by the International Headache Society International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3 beta). There is no objective diagnostic test - diagnosis rests on the patient's subjective description of symptoms and the clinician's assessment that no other disorder better accounts for the clinical presentation. The lifetime prevalence of vestibular migraine is estimated at 0.98%, making it more common than [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]] (0.12-0.5%) but less common than [[BPPV|BPPV]].

## PART A: CLASSIFICATION AND DEFINITIONS

### Relationship Between Migraine and Vestibular Symptoms

There are six ways in which vestibular symptoms and migraine can coexist:

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Benign paroxysmal vertigo of childhood | Migraine precursor - vertigo without headache in young children |
| Migraine with brainstem aura | Vertigo as part of posterior circulation aura (formerly basilar-type migraine) |
| Vestibular migraine | Vestibular symptoms attributable to migrainous processes |
| Epidemiological association | VM associated with Meniere's disease, BPPV, episodic ataxia type II, motion sickness |
| Risk factor for slow compensation | Migraine may impair recovery from acute vestibular disorders |
| Secondary vestibular migraine | Underlying vestibular disorder triggers migraine episodes |

### Diagnostic Criteria

#### Migraine Headache (ICHD-3 beta)

- At least five attacks
- Headache duration 4-72 hours
- At least two of: unilateral, pulsating, moderate/severe, aggravation by routine physical activity
- During headache at least one of: nausea and/or vomiting, or photo- and phonophobia
- Not attributed to another disorder

#### Vestibular Migraine Diagnostic Criteria

| Criterion | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| A | At least five episodes with vestibular symptoms of moderate or severe intensity lasting 5 minutes to 72 hours |
| B | History of migraine according to IHS classification |
| C | Migraine features with more than 50% of attacks: headache (two of unilateral, throbbing, moderate-severe, aggravation by movement), photo- and phonophobia, or visual aura |
| D | Not better accounted for by another disorder |

#### Probable Vestibular Migraine

Criteria A and D are fulfilled but only one of B or C. Definite VM is a highly stable and valid diagnosis over 10 years.

#### Migraine with Brainstem Aura

For vertigo to be part of brainstem aura, it should:

- Conform to timing definitions for aura (symptoms develop gradually over 5 minutes, last 5-60 minutes)
- Have other posterior circulation symptoms: dysarthria, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], reduced hearing, diplopia, visual symptoms in both fields, ataxia, decreased consciousness, bilateral paraesthesias

## PART B: EPIDEMIOLOGY AND CLINICAL FEATURES

### Epidemiology

- Lifetime prevalence: 0.98%
- Female preponderance
- More common in women in mid-life with 1-year prevalence of 5%
- Only 20% of patients consulting doctors are diagnosed correctly
- Age-adjusted health-related quality of life scores consistently lower than dizziness-free controls
- More common than Meniere's disease but less common than BPPV

### Clinical Features

#### Characteristics of Vestibular Symptoms

- Episodes can be of variable duration (5 minutes to 72 hours)
- Very short-lived episodes (less than 5 minutes) are frequently reported but may not meet diagnostic criteria
- Symptoms can be constantly present in chronic forms but subject to fluctuation
- Even in chronic migraine, history elicits initial period of episodic symptoms

#### Associated Features

- Headache (need not be prominent but useful for distinguishing from other causes)
- Photophobia and phonophobia - key diagnostic symptoms that should be specifically enquired about
- Sensitivity to self and visual motion - may persist between acute episodes
- Motion sickness (strongly linked to migraine)
- Sensitivity to alcohol (hangover headaches)
- Family history (limited utility due to high prevalence of migraine)

#### Functional Impact

Visual motion intolerance can result in avoidance of everyday situations:

- Supermarkets
- Transportation systems
- Crowds
- Leisure activities
- Work environments

## PART C: PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND GENETICS

### Pathophysiology Theories

Vestibular migraine is considered a disorder of sensory modulation involving both neural and vascular tissue:

| Theory | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| Trigeminovascular reflex | Aminergic brainstem nuclei activating trigeminal afferents responsible for migraine pain |
| Cortical spreading depression | Waves of depression (Leao phenomenon) affecting brainstem structures including vestibular nuclei |
| Ion channel dysfunction | Ion channels within inner ear critical for neuronal excitation; serotonin influences neurovascular homeostasis in inner ear |
| Vasospasm | Vestibular branches of internal auditory artery causing labyrinthine ischaemia |

### Genetics

- Vestibular migraine is a familial disorder with documented pedigrees
- Rare forms have Mendelian inheritance with identified mutations:
  - Familial hemiplegic migraine Types 1-4: CACNA1A, ATP1A, SCN1A mutations
  - Episodic ataxias Types 1-7: KCNA1 and CACNA1A mutations
- Suggests migraine and manifestations are ionopathies
- Single genetic mutation for common VM remains elusive despite extensive genome-wide association studies

## PART D: CLINICAL ASSESSMENT AND DIAGNOSIS

### History Taking

Key elements to elicit:

- Presence of episodic vestibular symptoms (duration, frequency, triggers)
- Headache history (including what patient describes as normal headache)
- Photo- and phonophobia (easily missed - enquire specifically)
- Motion sickness history
- Family history of migraine
- Unilateral auditory symptoms (to exclude Meniere's disease)
- Red flags for secondary headache causes

### Pitfalls in Diagnosis

| Pitfall | Consideration |
|---------|---------------|
| Denial of migraine history | Enquire specifically about all headaches including normal ones |
| Vestibular trigger effect | Some patients have underlying vestibular disorder triggering migraine; treat the underlying cause |
| Other headache causes | Consider both primary and secondary headache disorders |

### Physical Examination

- Usually normal in interictal period
- Some patients show visual motion intolerance during eye movement examination
- In acute setting: positional nystagmus, spontaneous nystagmus, mixed central/peripheral patterns
- Interictal eye movement abnormalities should prompt consideration of episodic ataxia type 2 or underlying neurological disorder

### Investigations

#### Neuro-otological Tests

| Investigation | Findings in VM |
|--------------|----------------|
| Pure-tone audiometry | Usually normal; helps differentiate from Meniere's disease |
| Caloric testing | Canal paresis in approximately 25% |
| Rotation testing | Directional preponderance in approximately 50% |
| Posturography | Abnormal in approximately 33% |
| [[VEMP and Menieres Disease|VEMP]] | Abnormalities are non-specific and variable |

#### Other Investigations

- MRI scanning: Helpful in selected cases to exclude secondary neurological disorders
- May not be required if history is typical and examination normal

## PART E: MANAGEMENT

### General Principles

The most important aspect of management after correct diagnosis is clear explanation to the patient about the nature of the condition. Patients need to become expert in self-management as the condition is frequently present over long periods.

### Lifestyle Modifications

- Identify and avoid trigger factors where possible
- Maintain regular sleep patterns
- Regular exercise
- Regular meal times
- Large-scale exclusion diets have no convincing evidence of benefit

### Pharmacological Treatment

#### Acute Relief

| Category | Medications | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-------|
| Analgesics | Paracetamol, NSAIDs | Avoid analgesic-overuse headache |
| Triptans | 5HT1B/D agonists | Migraine-specific relief |
| Vestibular sedatives | Prochlorperazine, cyclizine, cinnarizine | For acute vertigo; not suitable long-term |
| Antiemetics | Domperidone | For nausea |

#### Preventive Medications

When symptoms are sufficiently intrusive to justify risk of adverse effects:

- Propranolol
- Topiramate
- Select according to comorbidities and individual preferences regarding adverse effect profiles
- Ensure adequate trial of medications before labelling them ineffective
- Starting doses may not be adequate for therapeutic purposes

### Vestibular Rehabilitation

- Can manage symptoms of VM, especially head and visual motion intolerance
- Also addresses underlying fixed vestibular deficits
- Progress can be slower than in other conditions
- Can be associated with increase in headache frequency
- May justify lowering threshold for starting prophylactic agents

### Psychological Management

- VM associated with higher rates of psychological symptoms than many other vestibular disorders
- Migraine anxiety-related dizziness (MARD) is a recognized entity
- Secondary anxiety and depression should be recognized and treated appropriately
- Medical and/or psychological interventions as indicated

### Other Treatments

- Acupuncture: Some evidence of benefit when combined with other treatments
- Nutritional supplements with low-level evidence:
  - Riboflavin
  - Coenzyme Q10
  - Magnesium

### Pitfalls in Management

- Inadequate medication trials before labelling them ineffective
- High dropout rates from physiotherapy due to symptom increases
- Unidentified secondary psychological disorders leading to poorer prognosis

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Vestibular migraine is a subtype of migraine where vestibular symptoms predominate, with lifetime prevalence of 0.98%
> 2. Diagnosis requires: at least 5 episodes of moderate-severe vestibular symptoms (5 min to 72 hours), migraine history, and migraine features with more than 50% of attacks
> 3. VM is more common than Meniere's disease but less common than BPPV
> 4. There are six relationships between migraine and vestibular symptoms including primary VM, secondary VM, and migraine precursor states
> 5. Photo- and phonophobia are key diagnostic symptoms that should be specifically enquired about
> 6. Physical examination in interictal period is usually normal
> 7. Pure-tone audiometry is usually normal but helps differentiate from Meniere's disease
> 8. Management includes lifestyle modifications, acute treatment (triptans, vestibular sedatives), and preventive medications (propranolol, topiramate)
> 9. Vestibular rehabilitation can manage symptoms but progress may be slower than other conditions
> 10. Secondary psychological disorders (anxiety, depression) are common and should be addressed
> 11. Migraine is considered an ionopathy based on genetic studies of rare familial forms
> 12. Patient education and self-management skills are essential for this chronic episodic condition


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Many patients deny migraine history when first asked - enquire specifically about all headaches including those the patient considers normal
> 2. Vestibular stimulation can trigger migraine headaches - some patients have an underlying vestibular disorder causing secondary VM that needs treatment
> 3. Visual motion intolerance (supermarkets, crowds) commonly persists between episodes and significantly impacts quality of life
> 4. Starting doses of preventive medications may not be therapeutic - ensure adequate dose escalation before declaring treatment failure
> 5. Vestibular rehabilitation may increase headache frequency, which may justify earlier initiation of migraine prophylaxis
> 6. Probable VM (meeting only some criteria) is less stable as a diagnosis than definite VM over long-term follow-up
