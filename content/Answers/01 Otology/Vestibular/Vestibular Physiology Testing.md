## INTRODUCTION

The vestibular system is one of the earliest phylogenetically developed sensory systems. It generates information for the central nervous system with a fourfold purpose: gaze stabilization, balanced locomotion, general orientation with respect to gravity, and autonomic function adjustment after alterations of body orientation. Understanding vestibular physiology, clinical testing methods, and the differential diagnosis of central versus peripheral vertigo is essential for the otologist.

## PART A: VESTIBULAR SYSTEM PHYSIOLOGY *(Addresses Q66)*

### Inputs and Outputs
The brain receives information from three main sensory systems for balance:
1. **Visual system** — provides spatial orientation cues
2. **Vestibular system** — detects angular and linear accelerations
3. **Proprioceptive system** — provides information about body position from joints, muscles, and skin

These inputs are weighted by the CNS according to circumstances (e.g., in darkness, reliance on vestibular and proprioceptive inputs increases).

### Peripheral Vestibular Apparatus

**A. Semicircular Canals (SCCs)**
- Three paired semicircular canals detect **rotational (angular) acceleration**
- Oriented in three planes to detect all rotational movements:
  - **Lateral (horizontal) canals:** Both parallel, in the lateral plane; angled ~30° above horizontal
  - **RALP plane:** Right Anterior + Left Posterior canals (approximately 45° to sagittal plane)
  - **LARP plane:** Left Anterior + Right Posterior canals
- **Mechanism:** During head rotation, endolymph lags behind (Newton's 1st law), deflecting the cupula, which bends hair cells
- The SCC system is designed such that cupular deflection is **proportional to head angular velocity** (not acceleration) — the canal acts as an integrator
- Time constant T1 ≈ 0.003 s — cupula reaches 67% of ultimate deflection in just 3 ms
- **Optimal frequency range:** 0.1-5 Hz (corresponds to natural head movements during walking/running)
- Sustained constant velocity is no longer detected after ~30 seconds

**B. Otolith Organs**
- **Utricle:** Relatively horizontal orientation — primarily detects horizontal linear accelerations and head tilts
- **Saccule:** Relatively vertical (sagittal) orientation — primarily detects vertical linear accelerations
- **Mechanism:** Otolithic membrane containing otoconia (CaCO3 crystals, density ~3x endolymph) shifts with linear acceleration or gravity, deflecting hair cells
- **Einstein's equivalence principle:** Otolith organs cannot distinguish between linear acceleration and tilt — the brain resolves this ambiguity using SCC input and an internal model in the vestibulocerebellum (flocculus and nodulus)

### Central Vestibular Connections

The vestibular information is processed in the **vestibular nuclear complex** (4 nuclei — superior, lateral, medial, inferior) and projects to produce three main reflexes:

| Reflex | Pathway | Function |
|--------|---------|----------|
| **Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex (VOR)** | Vestibular nerve → vestibular nuclei → oculomotor nuclei (III, IV, VI) | Gaze stabilization during head movement; generates compensatory eye movements equal and opposite to head movement; **3-neuron arc** with shortest latency (~16 ms) |
| **Vestibulospinal Reflex (VSR)** | Via lateral vestibulospinal tract (from lateral vestibular nucleus/Deiters') and medial vestibulospinal tract | Postural control; maintenance of upright posture; extensor muscle tone |
| **Vestibulocollic Reflex (VCR)** | Via medial vestibulospinal tract (from medial vestibular nucleus) | Head stabilization on trunk during body movements |

**Additional connections:**
- **Vestibulocerebellar connections:** Flocculus/nodulus — VOR calibration and velocity storage
- **Vestibulo-cortical connections:** Parietal cortex — conscious perception of spatial orientation and self-motion
- **Vestibulo-autonomic connections:** Suprachiasmatic nucleus (circadian rhythm), vagal centres (motion sickness)

### Push-Pull Principle
The SCCs operate as functional pairs (e.g., right and left horizontal canals). During rotation:
- The canal on the leading side is **excited** (cupula deflected towards utricle in horizontal canal)
- The contralateral canal is **inhibited** (cupula deflected away from utricle)
- This push-pull arrangement doubles the sensitivity and provides directional coding

## PART B: EWALD'S LAWS *(Addresses Q66)*

Ewald's laws describe the relationship between endolymph flow direction and the resulting vestibular response.

### First Law
**The nystagmus and vertigo induced by endolymph flow occur in the plane of the stimulated semicircular canal.**
- Each canal generates eye movements (VOR) in the plane of that canal
- Horizontal canal stimulation produces horizontal nystagmus
- Posterior canal stimulation produces torsional-vertical (upbeating) nystagmus
- Anterior canal stimulation produces torsional-vertical (downbeating) nystagmus

### Second Law
**In the horizontal (lateral) semicircular canal, ampullopetal flow (towards the ampulla/utricle) produces a STRONGER response than ampullofugal flow (away from the ampulla).**
- Ampullopetal flow = **excitatory** in horizontal canal → stronger nystagmus
- Ampullofugal flow = **inhibitory** in horizontal canal → weaker nystagmus
- Clinical significance: In horizontal canal BPPV, the ear with the stronger geotropic nystagmus is the affected ear (particles cause ampullopetal flow on that side)

### Third Law
**In the vertical (anterior and posterior) semicircular canals, the pattern is REVERSED — ampullofugal flow produces the stronger (excitatory) response.**
- Ampullofugal flow = excitatory in vertical canals
- Ampullopetal flow = inhibitory in vertical canals
- This explains the direction of nystagmus in posterior canal BPPV during Dix-Hallpike test

### Clinical Significance of Ewald's Laws
- Explain **Alexander's law:** Peripheral vestibular nystagmus increases when looking in the direction of the fast phase
- Help determine the **affected side** in BPPV
- Explain the **asymmetry of caloric responses** — warm stimulation (ampullopetal flow) produces a stronger response than cold stimulation
- The excitatory response always dominates — known as **Ewald's dominance**

## PART C: TYPES OF NYSTAGMUS *(Addresses Q66)*

Nystagmus is an involuntary rhythmic oscillation of the eyes. Direction is defined by the fast phase.

### Classification by Waveform
1. **Jerk (saw-tooth) nystagmus:** Alternating slow and fast phases; can be peripheral or central
2. **Pendular nystagmus:** Quasi-sinusoidal, no distinct fast phase; **always central** (but may be congenital or due to severe visual loss)

### Classification by Aetiology/Type

| Type | Features | Significance |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Spontaneous nystagmus** | Present in primary gaze; peripheral type beats away from lesion; enhances in dark/behind Frenzel glasses | Peripheral = acute vestibular imbalance (neuritis, Meniere's); Central = brainstem/cerebellar |
| **Gaze-evoked nystagmus** | Appears on eccentric gaze; fast phase towards direction of gaze | Central — cerebellar/brainstem (impaired neural integrator); also drug effect (alcohol, sedatives, anticonvulsants) |
| **Positional nystagmus** | Provoked by change in head position (Dix-Hallpike, roll test) | BPPV (peripheral); can also be central |
| **Optokinetic nystagmus (OKN)** | Elicited by moving visual stimulus (striped drum); slow phase = pursuit, fast phase = saccadic reset | Tests pursuit system + saccade system; asymmetry suggests central lesion |
| **Per-rotatory nystagmus** | During rotational stimulation; fast phase in direction of rotation | VOR assessment |
| **Post-rotatory nystagmus** | After sudden stop of rotation; fast phase opposite to prior rotation | Decay assessed by time constant |
| **Caloric nystagmus** | Induced by thermal irrigation of EAC | Assesses individual labyrinthine function (see Part E) |
| **Downbeat nystagmus** | Fast phase downward; present in primary gaze or downgaze | Craniocervical junction pathology (Chiari malformation, MS) |
| **Upbeat nystagmus** | Fast phase upward | Brainstem lesion (medulla, pons) |

### Central vs Peripheral Nystagmus Differentiation Table

| Feature | Peripheral | Central |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Plane** | Horizontal or horizontal-torsional | Any plane (vertical, torsional, multidirectional) |
| **Amplitude (acute)** | ++ | ++/+++ |
| **Amplitude (chronic)** | +/- (compensates) | ++/+++ (persists) |
| **Effect of fixation** | **Suppressed** by visual fixation | Variable — may not suppress, may even increase |
| **Effect of Frenzel/darkness** | **Appears or enhances** | Variable (can diminish) |
| **Slow-phase waveform** | **Rectilinear (linear velocity)** — constant velocity reflecting tonic imbalance | Exponential velocity (non-linear) — central integrator involvement |
| **Direction** | Unidirectional (beats away from lesion); follows Alexander's law | May change direction with gaze (direction-changing) |
| **Duration** | Transient (compensates within days-weeks) | Persistent |
| **Associated symptoms** | Severe vertigo, nausea, hearing loss possible | May have minimal vertigo; neurological symptoms |

## PART D: CENTRAL VS PERIPHERAL VERTIGO DIFFERENTIATION *(Addresses Q30)*

### History Differentiating Points

| Feature | Peripheral Vertigo | Central Vertigo |
|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Onset** | Sudden, paroxysmal | Can be gradual or sudden |
| **Duration of episodes** | Seconds (BPPV), minutes-hours (Meniere's), days (neuritis) | Variable; may be persistent |
| **Severity** | Severe rotatory vertigo | Often less severe, more vague instability |
| **Nausea/vomiting** | Prominent | Often mild or absent |
| **Hearing loss** | May be present (Meniere's, labyrinthitis) | Usually absent (unless AICA stroke) |
| **[[Tinnitus|Tinnitus]]** | May be present | Rare |
| **Headache** | Rare | Common (vestibular migraine, posterior fossa lesion) |
| **Positional component** | Common (BPPV) | Positional nystagmus may be atypical |
| **Neurological symptoms** | Absent | Diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, weakness, numbness |
| **Fall direction** | Towards lesion side | Variable |
| **Compensation** | Good (weeks) | Poor |

### Clinical Examination Differentiating Points

| Test | Peripheral | Central |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Nystagmus character** | Unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, suppresses with fixation | Direction-changing, vertical, pure torsional, persists with fixation |
| **Head Impulse Test (HIT)** | **Positive** (corrective saccade on turning towards affected side) — indicates labyrinthine loss | **Negative** (normal VOR) |
| **Gaze-evoked nystagmus** | Absent | Present |
| **Skew deviation** | Absent | Present (vertical misalignment) |
| **HINTS protocol** | Normal impulse, direction-fixed, no skew → Central; Abnormal impulse, direction-fixed, no skew → Peripheral | Head Impulse Normal + direction-changing + skew = Central |
| **Romberg's test** | Falls towards lesion | Variable/inconsistent |
| **Cerebellar signs** | Absent | Dysmetria, ataxia, intention tremor |
| **Cranial nerve deficits** | CN VIII involvement possible | Multiple CN involvement possible |
| **Smooth pursuit** | Normal | Abnormal (broken/cogwheel) |

## PART E: CALORIC TEST *(Addresses Q97)*

### Principle
Temperature changes in the EAC influence the activity of the vestibular labyrinth, primarily the horizontal SCC. The main mechanism is **convection current** formation in the horizontal SCC when placed vertically.

### Technique — Fitzgerald-Hallpike Bithermal Method
1. **Patient position:** Supine with head raised **30 degrees** above horizontal (places horizontal SCC vertically)
2. **Irrigation:** Water at **30°C** (cold) and **44°C** (warm) — i.e., 37 ± 7°C
3. **Sequence:** Standardized as Left cold → Right cold → Left warm → Right warm
4. **Duration:** Each irrigation lasts **40 seconds**
5. **Interval:** At least **5 minutes** between irrigations for thermal equilibration
6. **Recording:** EOG or VOG; measure slow-phase velocity or duration of nystagmus

### Response
- **COWS mnemonic:** Cold Opposite, Warm Same
  - Cold irrigation → nystagmus beating AWAY from irrigated ear (contralateral)
  - Warm irrigation → nystagmus beating TOWARDS irrigated ear (ipsilateral)
- Mechanism: Cold → endolymph cools and sinks (in vertical SCC) → ampullofugal flow → inhibitory; Warm → endolymph heats and rises → ampullopetal flow → excitatory

### Canal Paresis (CP) Formula — Jongkees Formula

**CP% = [(RW + RC) - (LW + LC)] / (RW + RC + LW + LC) × 100**

Where: RW = Right warm, RC = Right cold, LW = Left warm, LC = Left cold (measured as peak slow-phase velocity or duration)

- **Canal paresis >20-25%** is considered significant
- Indicates reduced function of one labyrinth

### Directional Preponderance (DP) Formula

**DP% = [(RW + LC) - (LW + RC)] / (RW + RC + LW + LC) × 100**

- **DP >20-30%** is significant
- Indicates asymmetry in VOR — less localizing than CP
- Can be peripheral or central

### Five Main Caloric Abnormalities
1. **Bilateral absence:** Aminoglycoside ototoxicity, post-meningitis (25% neurological, 25% ototoxic, 25% miscellaneous, 25% idiopathic)
2. **Unilateral canal paresis:** [[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]], [[Vestibular Neuritis|vestibular neuritis]], labyrinthine lesion
3. **Directional preponderance:** Less specific; peripheral or central lesion
4. **Abnormal VOR suppression:** Central disorder (cerebellar/brainstem); normally, fixation suppresses caloric nystagmus by ~50%
5. **Perverted nystagmus:** Vertical nystagmus in response to caloric (expected horizontal) — indicates posterior fossa disease

### Advantages and Limitations

| Advantages | Limitations |
|-----------|------------|
| Assesses individual ear function (unlike rotational tests) | Some patients intolerant (discomfort, vertigo) |
| Simple technique, widely available | Middle ear disease/perforation can affect results (use air caloric) |
| Good for detecting unilateral vestibular loss | Does not test high-frequency VOR function |
| Standardized, reproducible | Cannot detect bilateral partial loss well |

## PART F: VEMP — PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS *(Addresses Q30, Q97)*

### Overview
Vestibular Evoked Myogenic Potentials (VEMPs) are short-latency electromyographic responses evoked by loud sound or vibration, reflecting otolith organ function.

### Types of VEMP

| Feature | cVEMP (Cervical VEMP) | oVEMP (Ocular VEMP) |
|---------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Recording site** | Sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle | Contralateral inferior oblique muscle (beneath eye) |
| **Otolith organ tested** | **Saccule** | **Utricle** |
| **Reflex pathway** | **Vestibulocollic reflex (VCR):** Saccule → inferior vestibular nerve → vestibular nucleus → medial vestibulospinal tract → SCM motor neurons | **Vestibulo-ocular reflex:** Utricle → superior vestibular nerve → vestibular nucleus → oculomotor nucleus → inferior oblique |
| **Response type** | Inhibitory (biphasic p13-n23 wave) | Excitatory (biphasic n10-p15 wave) |
| **Stimulus** | Air-conducted clicks/tone bursts (500 Hz optimal) or bone-conducted vibration | Same stimuli; bone-conducted vibration at Fz also effective |
| **Normal latency** | p13 ~13 ms, n23 ~23 ms | n10 ~10 ms, p15 ~15 ms |
| **Crossed/Uncrossed** | Ipsilateral (uncrossed) | **Contralateral** (crossed) |

### Physiological Basis
- The saccular macula is closest to the stapes footplate and is preferentially stimulated by air-conducted sound (Tullio phenomenon basis)
- Sound stimulus → saccular hair cell activation → inferior vestibular nerve → descending pathway → ipsilateral SCM inhibition = cVEMP
- Sound stimulus → utricular hair cell activation → superior vestibular nerve → ascending pathway → contralateral inferior oblique excitation = oVEMP

### Clinical Applications of VEMP
1. **Superior semicircular canal dehiscence (SSCD):** Enhanced (low threshold, high amplitude) cVEMP and oVEMP
2. **[[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]]:** Elevated thresholds or absent VEMP
3. **Vestibular neuritis:** Absent cVEMP (inferior nerve) or oVEMP (superior nerve) depending on division affected
4. **Vestibular schwannoma:** Absent or delayed VEMP on affected side
5. **[[Otosclerosis|Otosclerosis]]:** Absent/reduced cVEMP due to stapes fixation (conductive component)
6. **Age-related decline monitoring**

## PART G: GERIATRIC DIZZINESS *(Addresses Q16)*

Dizziness is one of the most common complaints in the elderly, affecting approximately **30% of those over 65 years**. It is typically **multifactorial** rather than due to a single cause.

### Causes and Contributing Factors

**A. Peripheral Vestibular Causes:**
- **BPPV** — most common cause of vertigo in elderly; prevalence increases with age; posterior canal most common
- **Presbyvestibulopathy** — age-related decline in vestibular function; loss of hair cells and vestibular neurons
- **Meniere's disease** — may occur or recur in elderly
- **Bilateral vestibular failure** — aminoglycoside exposure, autoimmune, post-meningitis

**B. Central Causes:**
- **Cerebrovascular disease** — vertebrobasilar insufficiency, posterior circulation strokes
- **Vestibular migraine** — may present de novo in elderly
- **Cerebellar degeneration** — ataxia, imbalance
- **Multiple sclerosis** — though less common to present in old age

**C. Cardiovascular Causes:**
- **Orthostatic hypotension** — very common in elderly; exacerbated by medications, autonomic neuropathy
- **Cardiac arrhythmias** — presyncope/syncope presenting as dizziness
- **Carotid sinus hypersensitivity**
- **Aortic stenosis**

**D. Medication-Related:**
- Antihypertensives, sedatives, anxiolytics, antidepressants, aminoglycosides, anticonvulsants, diuretics
- **Polypharmacy** is a major contributing factor in elderly dizziness

**E. Sensory Deficit:**
- **Visual decline** — cataracts, macular degeneration, glaucoma → reduced visual input for balance
- **Peripheral neuropathy** — diabetes, B12 deficiency → impaired proprioception
- **Cervical spondylosis** — altered proprioceptive input from cervical spine

**F. Musculoskeletal Factors:**
- Sarcopenia, joint disease, deconditioning → increased fall risk
- Reduced postural reflexes

**G. Psychological Factors:**
- Anxiety, depression, fear of falling — can perpetuate imbalance and avoidance behaviour

## PART H: EVALUATION OF THE SEVERELY DIZZY PATIENT *(Addresses Q16)*

### Systematic Approach

**A. History (MOST Important)**
- Timing: Episodic vs continuous; duration of episodes
- Triggers: Positional, Valsalva, loud sounds, head movement
- Associated symptoms: Hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness, headache, neurological symptoms
- Medications review
- Cardiovascular history
- Falls history

**B. Bedside Clinical Examination**

| Assessment | Technique | Significance |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Otoscopy** | Standard examination | Exclude middle ear pathology, [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]], perforation |
| **Nystagmus assessment** | With and without Frenzel glasses; gaze in all directions | Central vs peripheral differentiation |
| **Head Impulse Test (HIT)** | Rapid passive head rotation; observe for corrective saccade | Positive = peripheral vestibular loss on side of rotation |
| **HINTS exam** (in acute vestibular syndrome) | Head Impulse + Nystagmus + Test of Skew | Differentiates stroke from vestibular neuritis; more sensitive than MRI in first 48 hours |
| **Dix-Hallpike test** | Rapid positioning; observe for nystagmus | BPPV diagnosis (posterior/anterior canal) |
| **Supine roll test** | Head turned left/right in supine position | Horizontal canal BPPV |
| **Romberg test** | Standing, feet together, eyes open then closed | Proprioceptive/vestibular loss |
| **Unterberger (Fukuda) stepping test** | March on spot with eyes closed | Deviation >45° towards affected side |
| **Cranial nerve examination** | Complete CN assessment | Exclude central pathology, CPA lesion |
| **Cardiovascular** | Lying/standing BP, pulse, carotid auscultation | Orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia |

**C. Investigations**

| Investigation | Indication |
|--------------|------------|
| **Pure tone audiometry** | All patients — baseline hearing assessment |
| **Tympanometry** | Middle ear assessment |
| **Caloric testing** | Assess individual labyrinthine function |
| **VEMP (cVEMP + oVEMP)** | Otolith function, SSCD, vestibular schwannoma |
| **Video HIT (vHIT)** | High-frequency VOR assessment of all 6 canals |
| **ABR** | Retrocochlear pathology (VS) |
| **MRI brain + IAM with gadolinium** | Exclude vestibular schwannoma, central pathology, stroke |
| **CT temporal bones** | SSCD, temporal bone fracture |
| **Blood tests** | FBC, ESR, TFTs, glucose, lipid profile, syphilis serology, autoimmune screen |
| **ECG / Holter monitor** | Cardiac arrhythmia |
| **Lying/standing BP** | Orthostatic hypotension |
| **EEG** | If epilepsy suspected |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> - The VOR is a **3-neuron arc** (Scarpa's ganglion → vestibular nuclei → oculomotor nuclei) with a latency of ~16 ms
> - The SCC system encodes **head angular velocity** (not acceleration) through the mechanics of endolymph-cupula interaction
> - **Ewald's 2nd law:** In horizontal canal, ampullopetal flow is excitatory (stronger); in vertical canals, ampullofugal flow is excitatory — this is reversed
> - **COWS** mnemonic for caloric: Cold Opposite, Warm Same
> - **Canal paresis** formula (Jongkees): >20-25% indicates unilateral labyrinthine hypofunction
> - **cVEMP** tests saccular function via vestibulocollic reflex; **oVEMP** tests utricular function via VOR
> - A **HINTS** examination (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) in acute vestibular syndrome is more sensitive than early MRI for detecting posterior circulation stroke
> - **Peripheral vestibular nystagmus:** unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, suppressed by fixation, linear slow-phase
> - **Central nystagmus:** direction-changing, any plane, not suppressed, exponential slow-phase
> - Geriatric dizziness is **multifactorial** — BPPV, orthostatic hypotension, medications, and sensory deficits are the most common causes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. **Velocity storage mechanism** extends the VOR time constant from the peripheral value (~5-7s) to ~15-20s centrally — important for understanding post-rotatory nystagmus duration
> 2. In the caloric test, place the head at **30° above horizontal** to make the lateral SCC vertical — this maximizes convection currents
> 3. **Caloric test in space** produces reduced but present nystagmus, proving a direct thermal effect on the labyrinth beyond just convection
> 4. **Absent bilateral caloric responses** has four equal causes: neurological 25%, ototoxic 25%, miscellaneous 25%, idiopathic 25%
> 5. The **human resonant frequency for locomotion** is approximately **2.00 Hz** — this falls within the optimal SCC frequency range (0.1-5 Hz)
> 6. In elderly patients, always measure **lying and standing blood pressure** before attributing dizziness to a vestibular cause
> 7. **Perverted caloric nystagmus** (vertical instead of expected horizontal) is a reliable sign of posterior fossa disease
