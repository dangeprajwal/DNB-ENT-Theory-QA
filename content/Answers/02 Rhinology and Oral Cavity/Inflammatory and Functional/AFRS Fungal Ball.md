# AFRS and Fungal Ball

> [!question] Questions
> Discuss the mycology of AFRS and the role of steroids before FESS in [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|nasal polyposis]]. Classify fungal infections of the nose and paranasal sinuses. Describe the diagnostic criteria and management of AFRS. Define and classify allergic fungal rhinosinusitis, its clinical features and management. Discuss the pathogenesis, imaging and surgical management of fungal ball of the paranasal sinuses.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 21 (Fungal Infections) and Chapter 96 (Fungal Rhinosinusitis)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Fungal rhinosinusitis (FRS) represents a spectrum of diseases affecting the paranasal sinuses caused by ubiquitous environmental fungi. Fungi can affect the paranasal sinuses through inhalation of spores, but rarely cause disease, often requiring predisposing factors. The spectrum of disease is largely dependent on the immunological competence of the host. Fungal disease affecting the sinuses can be defined as acute or chronic (lasting over 4 weeks) and as invasive or non-invasive. Invasive fungal disease is defined by the presence of fungal hyphae within mucosa, submucosa, bone, blood vessels and nerves, whereas non-invasive disease implies colonization of the surface of epithelial tissues without invasion. Among non-invasive forms, allergic fungal rhinosinusitis (AFRS) and fungal ball are the two major entities, each with distinct pathogenesis, clinical features and management strategies.

---

## PART A: CLASSIFICATION OF FUNGAL RHINOSINUSITIS

Fungal rhinosinusitis is classified into three invasive and two non-invasive subgroups:

| Type | Duration | Immune Status | Pathogen |
|------|----------|---------------|----------|
| **Acute invasive (AIFR)** | Days to weeks | Immunocompromised | Aspergillus; Mucor/Rhizopus |
| **Chronic invasive (CIFR)** | Months to years | Immunocompetent/mildly compromised | Aspergillus flavus/fumigatus |
| **Chronic granulomatous (CGIFR)** | Months to years | Immunocompetent | Aspergillus flavus |
| **Fungal ball** | Chronic | Immunocompetent | Aspergillus species |
| **AFRS** | Chronic | Atopic, immunocompetent | Dematiaceous species |

**Invasive forms** are characterized by the presence of fungal hyphae invading mucosa, submucosa, blood vessels and bone. Acute invasive FRS occurs in immunocompromised patients (diabetics with ketoacidosis, neutropenic patients) and carries mortality rates exceeding 50%. Chronic invasive FRS follows an indolent course over months to years and may present with orbital invasion and cranial nerve deficits. Chronic granulomatous invasive FRS is distinguished by its geographical distribution (North Africa, Middle East, Asia), Aspergillus flavus as the predominant pathogen, and the presence of non-caseating granulomas with Langhan's type giant cells.

**Non-invasive forms** include the fungal ball (dense accumulation of extramucosal fungal hyphae without tissue invasion) and AFRS (an allergic and immunological response to extramucosal fungal hyphae in atopic individuals).

## PART B: AFRS - DEFINITION AND DIAGNOSTIC CRITERIA

### Definition and Historical Background

AFRS is a non-invasive fungal disease first reported by Safirstein in 1976 in a patient with allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA). This condition shares similar histopathological features with ABPA. Unlike other forms of sinusitis, it has distinct clinical, histopathological and prognostic findings. AFRS is the most common form of FRS, accounting for 56-72% of patients with FRS.

AFRS is more common in younger individuals (mean age 21-33 years) compared to CRS. It is also more frequent in warm, humid environments and in individuals of lower socioeconomic status. Atopic individuals are predisposed, and there is a higher male to female ratio of 1.5-2.6 to 1.

### Bent and Kuhn Diagnostic Criteria

All five major criteria must be met for diagnosis. The presence of minor criteria supports the diagnosis:

| Major Criteria (All 5 Required) | Minor Criteria |
|--------------------------------|----------------|
| Type I hypersensitivity (history, skin test or in vitro) | Asthma |
| Nasal polyposis | Unilateral disease |
| Characteristic CT findings | Radiological bone erosion |
| Eosinophilic mucin without invasion | Fungal cultures |
| Positive fungal stain | Serum eosinophilia |
| | Charcot-Leyden crystals |

### DeShazo Revised Criteria

DeShazo et al. proposed a revised set of criteria reflecting the controversy surrounding the role of atopy in AFRS: (1) sinusitis confirmed on CT scanning; (2) presence of allergic mucin; (3) demonstration of fungal hyphae within the allergic mucin; (4) absence of fungal invasion; and (5) absence of diabetes or immunodeficiency states.

### 2004 Expert Panel Redefinition

In 2004, a panel of experts redefined AFRS as histological confirmation of eosinophilic mucin and the presence of type 1 hypersensitivity to fungi in CRS patients. This definition emphasised the importance of both histological and immunological confirmation.

## PART C: AFRS - MYCOLOGY AND PATHOGENESIS

### Mycology

The most common pathogens in AFRS are the **dematiaceous species**:

- Curvularia
- Bipolaris
- Alternaria
- Cladosporium
- Drechslera
- Helminthosporium

Aspergillus species are also implicated. Although Aspergillus was the presumed pathogen in the first few early case series, subsequent studies showed that dematiaceous fungi were more common. Culture of the mucin may or may not be positive for fungus; however, this does not rule out AFRS as the yield of culture is typically fairly low.

### Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of AFRS is not completely understood and remains subject to controversy. In AFRS, a Type I (and possibly Type III) hypersensitivity reaction to inhaled airborne fungi is believed to result in tissue inflammation, oedema and production of eosinophilic mucin. This consists of eosinophilic inflammatory cells and Charcot-Leyden crystals, which represent breakdown products of eosinophilic enzymes.

**ABPA Model (Manning and Holman):** Due to the histopathological similarity of AFRS to ABPA, a common pathophysiological mechanism was attributed. In ABPA, both elevated IgE and IgG to Aspergillus are present. Both Type I (specific IgE antibody-antigen interaction) and Type III (IgG antibody-antigen complex) reactions have been postulated. Manning and Holman studied eight AFRS patients with culture-positive Bipolaris who showed skin test positivity to Bipolaris as well as RAST IgE and ELISA IgG antibodies for Bipolaris. In contrast, only 1 in 10 controls had a positive skin test for Bipolaris.

**Local Hypersensitivity Theory (Collins et al.):** Some AFRS patients did not display signs of systemic allergy. Collins et al. proposed that AFRS is the result of a local Type I hypersensitivity rather than a systemic response, demonstrating fungal-specific IgE in sinus mucin. This may explain why the response was confined to the nose and paranasal sinuses without systemic involvement.

**Eosinophilic Theory (Ponikau et al.):** Ponikau et al. prospectively studied 210 CRS patients and found cultures positive for fungus in 96% of patients with CRS. When using DeShazo's criteria, 93% of surgical CRS patients had AFRS. They rejected the role of IgE-mediated reaction and proposed eosinophilic chemotaxis in response to extramucosal fungi as the hallmark of AFRS, introducing the term eosinophilic fungal rhinosinusitis (EFRS). This study has been criticized because AFRS is usually associated with copious allergic mucin, whereas the patients in Ponikau's study often only produced small pockets of mucin.

**Key distinction:** While fungal allergy may play a role in the pathogenesis of simple CRS, AFRS is a distinct entity with very strict criteria for diagnosis. More is needed for diagnosis than merely the existence of allergy to fungal elements in a patient with sinusitis.

## PART D: AFRS - CLINICAL FEATURES AND IMAGING

### Clinical Features

Patients presenting with AFRS are typically young, immunocompetent individuals with a history of atopy or asthma. They present often with unilateral, but sometimes bilateral, symptoms of nasal congestion, post-nasal drip, and a thick dark nasal discharge. There is often a history of recurrent symptoms following previous treatment or surgery. Typically, patients show an excellent response to oral corticosteroids but not antibiotics.

Patients may also present with signs and symptoms of bony erosion and involvement of the orbit, including proptosis and telecanthus. On examination, there is usually gross nasal polyposis with thick, highly viscous mucin, classically described as the consistency of peanut butter. Green or black casts consisting of allergic mucin may be present.

### Histopathology

Microscopic examination of the fungal mucin with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining typically shows the presence of Charcot-Leyden crystals and eosinophilic breakdown products, and often the eosinophils themselves form sheets. Fungi do not stain with H&E staining but can be seen on special stains (silver impregnation). Culture of the mucin may or may not be positive; this does not rule out AFRS.

### CT Imaging

CT imaging typically shows characteristic pansinusitis with hyperattenuation of the intrasinus contents (representing thick allergic mucin) surrounded by lower attenuation hyperplastic mucosa. The **"double density" sign** may be seen in affected sinuses, representing heterogeneous signal intensity due to the deposition of heavy metals within the mucin. The affected sinus is often expanded with erosion of the sinus walls. This erosion is not due to invasion by fungal elements but due to local inflammation and the expansive nature of mucin, which differentiates it from invasive fungal sinusitis.

### MRI

MRI, if interpreted in the absence of CT, can be misleading. On T2-weighted images, the high metal concentration within proteinaceous allergic fungal mucin may show up as a **signal void**, mimicking the appearance of a normally aerated sinus. On T1-weighted images the material is hypointense to isointense. Both T1- and T2-weighted images show peripheral enhancement.

## PART E: AFRS - MANAGEMENT AND ROLE OF STEROIDS

### Surgical Management

The mainstay of AFRS treatment remains surgery, although adjunctive medical management is critical for optimal outcomes. The aim of [[FESS Steps Complications|functional endoscopic sinus surgery]] (FESS) is to remove all antigenic fungal elements and to re-establish drainage of the affected sinuses. During the endoscopic procedure, all specimens including polyps and allergic mucin should be sent for microscopic examination and staining for fungi, and all should be sent for culture. In particular, invasion of vasculature or mucosa should be excluded.

### Post-Operative Medical Management (Gan et al. 2014 Evidence-Based Review)

**1. Oral Steroids (Grade B Evidence):**
Gan et al. recommend the use of oral steroids in the post-operative period. In 2010, Rupa et al. published a prospective, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of post-operative oral steroids in AFRS comparing oral prednisolone (n=12) and placebo (n=12). They concluded that post-operative oral steroid in a tapering dose produces significant subjective and objective improvement. Kuhn and Javer found that a minimum of 6 months of normal sinus mucosa while on steroids is necessary before steroids can be slowly discontinued. A 6-month post-operative regimen of tapering corticosteroid therapy was proposed, including high-dose intranasal corticosteroids. However, long-term use should be judicious and limited to short courses in the perioperative period and in acute exacerbations.

**2. Topical Nasal Steroids:**
Topical nasal steroids achieve effective drug concentration at the sinonasal mucosa with few systemic side effects. There is little evidence specifically for topical steroids post-operatively in AFRS. Standard topical nasal steroids include mometasone furoate, fluticasone propionate, budesonide and beclomethasone. Off-label use of budesonide administered as drops, atomized sprays or through low volume saline rinses has gained popularity.

**3. Oral Antifungals (Level C Evidence):**
Use restricted to patients with a poor post-operative response to steroids. Itraconazole at 200-400 mg daily in divided doses for up to 6 months shows reduction in symptoms, prevention of disease recurrence and reduced dependence on oral steroids. Oral itraconazole is also used as a steroid-sparing medication.

**4. Topical Antifungals:**
No high quality evidence regarding the use of topical antifungals in AFRS.

**5. [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|Immunotherapy]] (Level C Evidence):**
Bassichis et al. studied 82 patients and found a re-operation rate of 33% in those who did not receive immunotherapy compared to 11.1% in those who did. Folker et al. found improvements in mucosal endoscopic staging, quality of life and reliance on oral and systemic steroids. No evidence of unusual side effects or immune complex formation in AFRS patients receiving immunotherapy.

**6. Anti-IgE Antibody (Omalizumab):**
AFRS refractory to surgery and conventional medical treatment has been shown to be responsive to omalizumab.

**7. Leukotriene Modulators:**
No evidence that leukotriene modulators such as montelukast have a role in the treatment of AFRS.

### Role of Steroids Before FESS in Nasal Polyposis

A short pre-operative course of systemic corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg each morning for 5-10 days) reduces polyp size, mucosal inflammation and intraoperative bleeding. This improves surgical visualization and allows more complete surgery. In a prospective comparative study by Landsberg et al., AFRS patients who received oral steroids pre-operatively showed greater radiologic and endoscopic improvement compared to CRSwNP patients. Post-operatively, patients should be treated with nasal douching and intranasal or systemic corticosteroids, and compliance with this treatment influences the long-term efficacy of surgery. There is good evidence that post-operative use of topical nasal steroids reduces the rate of polyp recurrence.

## PART F: FUNGAL BALL

### Definition

DeShazo developed a clear definition for the diagnosis of fungal ball of the paranasal sinuses. A dense mass of hyphae is found in the affected sinus, which separates easily from the sinus mucosa, and macroscopically resembles a clay-like or cheesy material. Microscopically, there is no evidence of invasion of the mucosa, vessels or bone. There are typical radiological changes and non-specific inflammatory changes of the mucosa, with no predominance of eosinophils, no granuloma and no allergic mucin. It is distinct from saprophytic fungal infestation, which corresponds to fungal spores found on crusts and mucus in the nose.

### Epidemiology and Predisposing Factors

Fungal balls are seen more commonly in immunocompetent, middle-aged and elderly females. There is no evidence that pre-existing medical conditions or anatomical abnormalities predispose to fungal balls. However, an association between previous dental treatment and fungal balls has been suggested, with reports showing that between 56% and 84% of patients had previous dental treatment.

### Pathogenesis

Two theories have been proposed:

- **Aerogenic pathway:** Large amounts of inhaled fungal spores enter the sinuses through the natural ostium
- **Iatrogenic pathway:** Fungal colonization occurs via an oro-antral fistula created during endodontic treatment with overfilling of the dental canal. Commonly used endodontic sealers promote fungal growth: zinc oxide found in sealers promotes the growth of Aspergillus, whereas the second component (eugenol) may inhibit its growth. Over time, the inhibitory effect of eugenol fades, allowing fungal growth many years after the initial dental treatment. This explains why the maxillary sinus is the most commonly affected sinus and why many patients have a history of previous endodontic treatment.

### Clinical Features

Fungal balls most often affect one sinus, most commonly the maxillary sinus (94%), followed by the sphenoid sinus. Symptoms are non-specific and the patient may be completely asymptomatic, hence the reason why patients are often diagnosed late. Other symptoms include headache or facial pain, post-nasal drip and cacosmia. Fungal balls affecting the sphenoid sinus may present with retro-orbital pain at the vertex. Symptoms may be recurrent, coinciding with superimposed bacterial sinus infection, with relatively symptom-free periods between episodes.

Examination findings may be unremarkable, but careful endoscopic examination may reveal localized inflammatory changes at the natural ostium of the affected sinus, including small polyps or purulent mucopus.

### Pathology

Macroscopically the fungal ball consists of cheesy-like material, which is green, yellow, brown or black. The underlying mucosa may be normal or hypertrophic. Microscopically, the ball consists of densely packed hyphae and may have an onion-skin-like appearance due to alternating zones of dense and less-dense growth. The mucosa may show non-specific inflammation, but there is an absence of invasion by fungal elements or granulomatous reaction.

Aspergillus is the most commonly identified fungus. It can often be seen on haematoxylin-eosin slides. However, all samples should be stained with silver impregnation stains to identify fungi. Only culture will confirm the species of fungus, but culture typically has a low yield of up to 50%.

### Imaging

**CT scanning** is the imaging of choice for suspected fungal balls. Five CT features are commonly found:

- Heterogenous soft tissue density in a single unilateral sinus
- Absence of an air-fluid level
- Erosion of the inner wall of the sinus
- Sclerosis of the lateral sinus wall
- Presence of calcification

The presence of erosion of the inner wall and the presence of calcification have a positive predictive value of 94.6% and 93.2% respectively for the diagnosis of fungal balls. The calcification is due to deposition of calcium salts within the fungal ball.

**MRI** is not usually necessary but can differentiate mucosal swelling or mucous retention from a fungal ball. On T2-weighted imaging, fungal balls are hypointense compared to mucous or mucosal swelling which is hyperintense.

### Management

The treatment of fungal balls is surgical with no adjunctive medical treatment usually necessary. FESS is employed to create a wide opening of the affected sinus ostium. All fungal material should be removed, and biopsies taken from the underlying mucosa to rule out invasion. Examination with angled scopes is crucial to ensure complete surgical extirpation. Any dental filling material present in the sinus should also be removed. In immunocompetent patients, there is no role for the use of systemic antifungal agents. Subsequent regular surveillance in the clinic is necessary. Oral or topical antifungals are not necessary.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Fungal rhinosinusitis is classified into three invasive (AIFR, CIFR, CGIFR) and two non-invasive (fungal ball, AFRS) forms based on host immunity and tissue invasion.
> 2. AFRS diagnosis requires ALL five Bent and Kuhn major criteria: Type I hypersensitivity, nasal polyposis, characteristic CT findings, eosinophilic mucin without invasion, and positive fungal stain.
> 3. Dematiaceous species (Curvularia, Bipolaris, Alternaria, Cladosporium, Drechslera, Helminthosporium) are the commonest pathogens in AFRS, along with Aspergillus species.
> 4. The "double density" sign on CT is characteristic of AFRS, representing hyperattenuating allergic mucin surrounded by hypoattenuating hyperplastic mucosa.
> 5. T2 signal void on MRI in AFRS (due to heavy metal content of mucin) can mimic a normally aerated sinus and is a potential diagnostic pitfall.
> 6. Bone erosion in AFRS is caused by local inflammation and the expansive nature of mucin, NOT by fungal invasion.
> 7. AFRS management combines FESS (to remove antigenic fungal elements) with post-operative oral steroids (Grade B evidence from Rupa et al. RCT showing significant improvement with tapering prednisolone).
> 8. Immunotherapy for AFRS reduces re-operation rate from 33% to 11.1% (Bassichis et al.) and improves endoscopic staging and quality of life (Level C evidence).
> 9. Fungal ball is treated by FESS alone with wide ostium opening and complete fungal removal; there is no role for antifungals in immunocompetent patients.
> 10. Between 56% and 84% of fungal ball patients have a history of prior dental treatment; zinc oxide in endodontic sealers promotes Aspergillus growth while the inhibitory effect of eugenol fades over time.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **AFRS should be suspected** in any young atopic patient with unilateral pansinusitis, bone erosion, thick dark "peanut butter consistency" mucin and excellent response to oral steroids but not antibiotics.
> - **Fungal culture may be negative in AFRS** and does not rule out the diagnosis, as the yield of culture is typically low.
> - **Omalizumab (anti-IgE)** is a promising option for AFRS refractory to surgery and conventional treatment.
> - **Fungal ball calcification on CT** combined with inner wall erosion has a positive predictive value exceeding 93% for diagnosis.
> - **Pre-operative systemic steroids** (prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg for 5-10 days) improve surgical field in nasal polyposis by reducing polyp size and intraoperative bleeding, but must be used judiciously.
> - **AFRS has the highest recurrence rate** among CRS subtypes and requires long-term surveillance with a minimum of 6 months of normal sinus mucosa on steroids before tapering.
