## Introduction

Allergic rhinitis is characterized by inflammatory changes in the nasal mucosa caused by exposure to inhaled allergens and is an IgE-mediated disease. It affects 0.8–39.7% of the world population with a prevalence in Western Europe of 23%. It causes significant disability, is often poorly managed, and may co-exist with allergic conjunctivitis and asthma as part of the unified allergic airway concept. ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and Its Impact on Asthma) guidelines have replaced the older seasonal/perennial classification with a symptom-based system that better guides treatment decisions. Immunotherapy remains the only curative approach for allergic rhinitis.

---

## PART A: Definition, Classification and Pathophysiology

### Definition

Rhinitis is defined clinically as having two or more symptoms of anterior or posterior rhinorrhoea, sneezing, nasal blockage and/or itching of the nose during two or more consecutive days for more than one hour on most days. Allergic rhinitis is diagnosed when these symptoms are caused by allergen exposure leading to an IgE-mediated reaction.

### ARIA Classification

| Parameter | Intermittent | Persistent |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Duration | < 4 days/week OR < 4 consecutive weeks/year | > 4 days/week AND > 4 consecutive weeks/year |

| Severity | Criteria |
|----------|----------|
| Mild | Normal sleep, normal daily activities, normal work/school, no troublesome symptoms |
| Moderate–Severe | ONE or more of: abnormal sleep, impairment of daily activities/sport/leisure, problems at school/work, troublesome symptoms |

Approximately 75% of patients are classified as having persistent rhinitis and 25% intermittent. These definitions replaced the older terms "seasonal" and "perennial" and were introduced to relate the patient's symptoms, rather than an allergen, to a diagnosis.

### Immunological Pathway (Pathophysiology)

The immunological pathway in allergic rhinitis is an IgE-mediated Type I hypersensitivity reaction:

1. **Sensitization phase:** Initial allergen exposure leads to antigen processing by dendritic cells, presentation to T-helper cells, Th2 skewing, and production of allergen-specific IgE by B cells
2. **IgE binding:** Specific IgE binds to high-affinity FcεRI receptors on the surface of tissue mast cells in the nasal mucosa
3. **Early-phase response (minutes):** Re-exposure to the allergen causes cross-linking of IgE/FcεRI complexes, triggering mast cell degranulation with release of:
   - Preformed mediators: histamine, serine proteases, heparin
   - Newly synthesized eicosanoids: leukotriene C4 (LTC4), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), thromboxane, platelet-activating factor (PAF)
4. **Late-phase response (4–8 hours):** Recruitment of eosinophils, basophils and T cells; release of cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) perpetuating inflammation
5. **Clinical effects:** Histamine drives sneezing and itching (nerve irritation); mucosal integrity loss causes rhinorrhoea; vascular engorgement causes nasal blockage. Leukotrienes and prostaglandins are involved in longer-term blockage

### Natural History

- Genetic factors: having atopic parents increases risk 3–6 times
- Environmental exposure required — pollen allergy uncommon before age 2 (requires two seasons of exposure)
- **Atopic march:** Sequential development of eczema → allergic rhinitis → asthma in children
- **Hygiene hypothesis:** Reduced exposure to infections reduces Th1 response, leading to Th2 overdrive and excessive IgE production; farm children and those with helminth infections are protected
- Adult-onset asthma is 3x more likely in allergic rhinitis patients
- Remission may occur over time; symptoms generally become less severe with age

### Local Allergic Rhinitis (LAR)

A localized nasal allergic response without detectable serum specific IgE or positive skin tests, diagnosed by positive nasal allergen provocation test (NAPT). LAR accounts for approximately 15% of all rhinitis and involves local IgE production within the nasal mucosa with a Th2 cytokine pattern.

## PART B: Diagnosis and Allergy Testing

### Clinical Assessment

The history is crucial. Key aspects:
- When symptoms started, how long they last, seasonal pattern
- Two or more of: rhinorrhoea, sneezing, blockage, itching
- Associated symptoms: itchy eyes, pharyngeal itch, hearing loss (OME), eczema, asthma, hyposmia
- Family history of atopy
- Red flags (NOT typical allergic rhinitis): unilateral symptoms, mucopurulent discharge, [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]], pain, anosmia

### Skin Prick Test (SPT)

SPT is the preferred diagnostic test. The procedure involves:

1. Clean forearm skin with 70% alcohol
2. Place drops of standardized allergen extracts (1:10 or 1:20 w/v) 2 cm apart on volar forearm
3. Use a separate lancet for each allergen to introduce extract into the skin
4. Include negative control (saline) and positive control (histamine)
5. Read at 15–20 minutes

**Interpretation:** Wheal ≥ 3 mm greater than negative control with flare = positive (sensitization). Precise measurement = (D + d) / 2 where D = largest diameter and d = perpendicular diameter.

| Factor | Effect on SPT |
|--------|--------------|
| Antihistamines | Stop 3–7 days before |
| H2 blockers | Stop 48 hours before |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Stop 2 weeks before |
| Omalizumab | Suppresses for ≥ 6 months |
| Age | Less reactive in elderly |
| Back vs forearm | Back more reactive |

**UK main 4 allergens:** Grass pollen, house dust mite, cat, dog. Approximately 25% of adults have positive SPT but only 10–15% are symptomatic.

### RAST (Specific IgE Measurement)

Blood test measuring allergen-specific IgE. Reserved for patients where SPT is unavailable or inappropriate. Positive result: IgE ≥ 0.35 kU/L. Must be correlated with clinical symptoms.

### Nasal Allergen Provocation Test (NAPT)

Gold standard of allergy diagnosis but infrequently required. Useful when history suggests allergy but SPT negative (suspected LAR). Risk of anaphylaxis — used only in specialist centres.

## PART C: Pharmacological Treatment (ARIA Algorithm)

### ARIA Treatment Algorithm

- **Mild intermittent:** Oral H1 blocker OR intranasal H1 blocker AND/OR decongestant OR LTRA
- **Moderate–severe intermittent:** Same as above OR intranasal corticosteroid OR LTRA (or cromone)
- **Mild persistent:** Intranasal corticosteroid, H1 blocker, or LTRA; review at 2–4 weeks
- **Moderate–severe persistent:** Intranasal corticosteroid first-line; if failure — increase INCS dose, add ipratropium for rhinorrhoea, add decongestant or short oral steroid course for blockage; consider immunotherapy

### Pharmacological Agents

| Drug Class | Itch/Sneezing | Discharge | Blockage | Smell |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|----------|-------|
| Sodium cromoglicate | + | + | +/- | - |
| Oral antihistamines | +++ | ++ | +/- | - |
| Ipratropium bromide | - | +++ | - | - |
| Topical decongestants | - | - | +++ | - |
| Topical corticosteroids | +++ | +++ | ++ | + |
| Oral corticosteroids | +++ | +++ | +++ | ++ |
| Antileukotrienes | - | ++ | + | +/- |

**Antihistamines:** Second-generation (loratadine, cetirizine) are non-sedating, safe for long-term use, rapid onset (<1 hour), once-daily dosing. More effective when used regularly rather than on demand. Topical azelastine provides rapid nasal symptom control but has bitter taste and requires twice-daily dosing.

**Intranasal corticosteroids (INCS):** Most effective single treatment for allergic rhinoconjunctivitis. Reduce all symptoms including ocular symptoms. Slow onset — 2 weeks for full benefit. No mucosal atrophy even with long-term use. Low systemic bioavailability. Side effects: epistaxis (from incorrect spray technique). First-line for nasal block.

**LTRAs (Montelukast):** Effective as loratadine for nasal symptoms but less effective than INCS. Licensed for allergic rhinitis associated with asthma.

**Decongestants:** Topical (xylometazoline) — maximum 7–10 days to avoid rhinitis medicamentosa. Systemic (pseudoephedrine) — side effects include insomnia, tachycardia, tremor.

**Ipratropium bromide:** Effective for watery rhinorrhoea. Useful addition when INCS alone does not control rhinorrhoea.

**Oral corticosteroids:** Prednisolone 20–40 mg/day for short courses only. Depot injectable steroids NOT recommended.

## PART D: Immunotherapy — SCIT and SLIT

### Definition and Principle

Immunotherapy is the only curative approach for allergic rhinitis. It involves repeated exposure to increasing doses of allergen to induce tolerance. It reduces symptoms, offers long-lasting benefit even after stopping treatment, and prevents progression of allergic disease.

### Mechanism of Action

- Inhibition of mast cell and basophil degranulation
- Upregulation of blocking IgG4 antibodies
- Inhibition of IgE-facilitated antigen presentation
- Skewing to regulatory T-cell (T-reg) and B-reg phenotypes
- Reduction of tissue mast cells and eosinophils

### Types: SCIT vs SLIT

| Feature | SCIT | SLIT |
|---------|------|------|
| Route | Subcutaneous injection | Sublingual drops/tablets |
| Updosing | 2–4 months gradually | May start at maximum dose |
| Maintenance | Every 4–6 weeks x 3 years | Daily x 3 years |
| Supervision | Observe 1 hour post-injection | First dose supervised; home use thereafter |
| Side effects | Injection site redness/itching; rare anaphylaxis | Oral itching; very rare systemic reaction |
| Efficacy | Similar | Similar |
| Onset | Earlier than SLIT | Slightly delayed |

### Patient Selection

- Severe symptoms unresponsive to standard pharmacotherapy
- Unacceptable medication side effects
- Up to 40% of UK GP patients have poor symptom control
- Confirmed IgE-mediated response (SPT or specific IgE)
- Main allergens: HDM, grass pollen, cat, dog, tree pollen
- Treatment continued for 3 years for lasting tolerance

### Efficacy Data

- SCIT for grass pollen: symptom scores 32% lower, medication scores 41% lower than placebo
- Reduces risk of developing asthma in older children with allergic rhinitis
- Prevents development of new allergies
- **Priming concept:** Exposure to one allergen can cause asymptomatic eosinophil infiltration, "priming" the patient to react to a different allergen. This explains why immunotherapy for one allergen may benefit responses to others.

### Anti-IgE Therapy (Omalizumab)

Monoclonal antibody binding circulating IgE, preventing mast cell degranulation. Reduces all nasal symptoms and improves asthma control. Monthly injection, expensive. Risk of anaphylaxis. Currently recommended only for severe allergic asthma with or without rhinitis.

## PART E: Allergic Rhinitis in Children

- Most prevalent allergic disease in children with significant impact on quality of life
- May exacerbate coexisting asthma
- Difficult to differentiate from frequent acute infective rhinitis — suspect allergy if episodes last >2 weeks with itching
- Blocked nose with normal mucosa may suggest adenoid enlargement
- SPT can be performed in young children (on the back if afraid of lancet)

### Treatment in Children

- Treatment algorithm similar to adults
- **First-line:** Oral non-sedating antihistamines
- **Second-line:** Intranasal corticosteroids — fluticasone (from age 4), mometasone and triamcinolone (from age 6); low systemic bioavailability
- **Beclometasone NOT recommended** (associated with growth retardation)
- **LTRAs:** Trial if nasal spray not tolerated or steroid load excessive
- **SCIT:** Generally not recommended under 5 years
- **SLIT:** May be used increasingly; efficacy in young children still uncertain
- Monitor growth if child already using steroids for asthma/eczema

## PART F: Drug-Induced Rhinitis and Pregnancy Rhinitis

### Drug-Induced Rhinitis

Medications that cause rhinitis:

| Mechanism | Drugs |
|-----------|-------|
| Rhinorrhoea (predominantly) | Aspirin, NSAIDs |
| Nasal blockage | ACE inhibitors, methyldopa, oral contraceptive pill, beta-blockers, psychotropic agents |
| Rhinitis medicamentosa | Topical vasoconstrictors (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, naphazoline) — chronic use >7–10 days causes rebound vasodilation |

Aspirin/NSAIDs may cause isolated rhinorrhoea or be part of AERD complex (asthma + polyposis + aspirin sensitivity). Rhinitis medicamentosa from decongestant overuse should be excluded before considering inferior turbinate surgery.

### Pregnancy-Induced Rhinitis (PIR)

- Cumulative incidence: 22%
- Caused by high oestrogen levels — oestrogen and progesterone increase H1 receptor expression on nasal epithelial cells and induce eosinophil migration/degranulation
- May occur in women without prior rhinitis history
- Symptoms: nasal congestion, manifests in 2nd/3rd trimester, lasting ≥ 6 weeks
- Smokers have 69% higher relative risk
- Neither asthma nor pre-existing rhinitis are risk factors for PIR

**Treatment:**
- Topical nasal steroids with low bioavailability (relatively safe)
- Hypertonic saline douches preferred
- Occasional topical decongestants if needed
- Self-limiting — resolves rapidly after childbirth
- Depot steroids NOT recommended

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. ARIA classifies allergic rhinitis as intermittent vs persistent (not seasonal/perennial) and mild vs moderate–severe based on impact on sleep, daily activities and work/school.
> 2. The immunological pathway is a Type I IgE-mediated hypersensitivity — mast cell degranulation releases histamine (sneezing/itching), leukotrienes (blockage) and prostaglandins.
> 3. SPT is the preferred diagnostic test — positive result is wheal ≥ 3 mm above negative control at 15–20 minutes; UK main 4 allergens are grass, HDM, cat, dog.
> 4. Local allergic rhinitis (LAR) accounts for 15% of rhinitis — positive NAPT but negative SPT and serum IgE.
> 5. Intranasal corticosteroids are the most effective single treatment for allergic rhinoconjunctivitis, addressing all symptoms including ocular symptoms.
> 6. Antihistamines are first-line for itch/sneezing but have minimal effect on nasal blockage — more effective when used regularly rather than on demand.
> 7. Immunotherapy (SCIT/SLIT) is the only curative approach — 3-year treatment induces lasting tolerance by upregulating blocking IgG4 antibodies and T-regulatory cells.
> 8. SCIT and SLIT have similar efficacy; SCIT requires hospital observation (1 hour post-injection) while SLIT can be administered at home after supervised first dose.
> 9. Drug-induced rhinitis includes rhinitis medicamentosa (topical decongestant overuse >7–10 days) and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD).
> 10. Pregnancy rhinitis affects 22%, is caused by oestrogen-mediated H1 receptor upregulation, and is self-limiting — resolves rapidly after delivery.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **The "priming" concept** explains why immunotherapy for one allergen benefits responses to others — allergen exposure causes asymptomatic eosinophil infiltration and pro-inflammatory mucosal changes that lower the threshold for reacting to other allergens.
> - **25% of adults are SPT-positive but only 10–15% are symptomatic** — a positive test shows atopy, not necessarily clinical allergy; results must be correlated with history.
> - **Montelukast is licensed for AR associated with asthma** — it is as effective as loratadine for nasal symptoms but less effective than INCS; significant inter-patient variation in responsiveness.
> - **Beclometasone should NOT be used in children** — associated with growth retardation; fluticasone, mometasone and triamcinolone have low systemic bioavailability and are preferred.
> - **SCIT is not recommended for children under 5** — SLIT may be used but efficacy in young children remains uncertain.
> - **Omalizumab (anti-IgE)** reduces all nasal symptoms but is currently recommended only for severe allergic asthma with or without rhinitis due to cost and anaphylaxis risk.
