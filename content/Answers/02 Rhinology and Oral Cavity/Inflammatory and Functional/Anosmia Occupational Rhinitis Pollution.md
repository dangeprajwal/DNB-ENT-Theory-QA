# Anosmia and Effects of Pollution On the Respiratory Tract

**Question:** *Discuss the classification, causes, evaluation, and management of anosmia. Describe the effects of air pollution and occupational exposure on the nose and respiratory tract.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 110 (Abnormalities of Smell) & Chapter 93 (Occupational Rhinitis)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The sense of smell allows discrimination between thousands of volatile compounds, providing information regarding safety (spoiled food, gas leaks), aesthetics, and communication. Combined with gustatory and somatosensory stimuli, it determines the flavours of foods and beverages. Among patients presenting with olfactory problems, over 68% experience altered quality of life, 46% describe changes in appetite or body weight, and 56% report influences on daily living or psychological well-being. There are at least 2.7 million (1.4%) adults in the United States with olfactory dysfunction. Notably, most patients who complain of decreased "taste" actually have an unrecognized impairment of smell.

---

## PART A: OLFACTORY ANATOMY AND PHYSIOLOGY

### Olfactory Neuroepithelium

The olfactory neuroepithelium exists within a small region (~2 cm^2) in the upper nasal recesses lining the cribriform plate and sectors of the superior turbinate, middle turbinate, and septum. The olfactory cleft is approximately 1 mm wide, situated 7 cm deep to the nostril. Only 10-15% of inspired air reaches the olfactory neuroepithelium.

### Four Neural Systems Within the Human Nose

1. **Main olfactory system (CN I):** Mediates common odour sensations
2. **Accessory olfactory system (vomeronasal):** Non-functional in humans
3. **Trigeminal somatosensory system (CN V):** Mediates irritation, burning, cooling, tickling; induces protective reflexes
4. **Nervus terminalis (CN 0):** GnRH-immunoreactive; possible endocrine association

### Cell Types of the Neuroepithelium

- Bipolar sensory receptor neuron (CNS origin; receptor-containing cilia)
- Supporting (sustentacular) cell
- Duct cell of Bowman's glands (secretes mucus)
- Microvillar cell
- Horizontal (dark) basal cells (stem cells)
- Globose (light) basal cells (multipotent)

### Key Facts

- Approximately **6 million** receptor cell axons coalesce into 30-50 fascicles (olfactory fila) traversing the cribriform plate
- Olfactory receptor gene family: ~1000 genes (~1% of all expressed genes)
- Surface area of cilia exceeds **20 cm^2** in humans
- Olfactory receptor cells can serve as conduits for viruses from nasal cavity to brain

## PART B: CLASSIFICATION OF OLFACTORY DISORDERS

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Anosmia | Inability to detect qualitative olfactory sensations |
| Partial anosmia | Ability to perceive some, but not all, odours |
| Hyposmia/microsmia | Decreased sensitivity to odours |
| Hyperosmia | Increased sensitivity to common odours |
| Dysosmia (cacosmia/parosmia) | Distorted smell perception to stimulation |
| Phantosmia | Smell perception without stimulus (olfactory hallucination) |
| Olfactory agnosia | Inability to recognize a smell despite intact processing |
| Presbyosmia | Age-related decline in smell function |

Olfactory loss can be subdivided into:

- **(a) Conductive (transport):** Obstruction of nasal passages (inflammation, polyps)
- **(b) Sensorineural:** Damage to olfactory neuroepithelium, central tracts, or connections (viruses, toxins, tumours)

## PART C: CAUSES OF ANOSMIA

### Three Commonest Causes of Permanent Olfactory Loss

1. **Prior upper respiratory infections** (most common cause)
2. **Head trauma** (coup/contrecoup injury; prevalence ~15%)
3. **Nasal and paranasal sinus disease** ([[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|chronic rhinosinusitis]], polyposis)

### Other Major Categories

**Neurodegenerative diseases:**
- Alzheimer's disease and idiopathic Parkinson's disease - olfactory dysfunction may be the **first clinical sign**
- Multiple sclerosis - olfactory loss proportional to number of demyelinating lesions in olfactory-processing regions
- Schizophrenia - significant deficits in olfactory threshold and odour identification

**Endocrine:**
- Kallmann's syndrome (anosmia + hypogonadotropic hypogonadism due to olfactory bulb aplasia)
- Hypothyroidism, diabetes, Addison's disease, pregnancy

**Mass lesions:**
- Olfactory groove meningiomas, frontal lobe gliomas, suprasellar meningiomas
- Foster-Kennedy syndrome: ipsilateral anosmia + ipsilateral optic atrophy + contralateral papilloedema

**Granulomatous diseases:** Sarcoidosis, GPA (Wegener's), SLE

**Congenital:** Septo-optic dysplasia (de Morsier's syndrome)

**Age-related:**
- Under 65 years: ~1% have major difficulty
- 65-80 years: ~50% exhibit some decrement
- Over 80 years: ~75% affected

## PART D: CLINICAL EVALUATION

### History

Key questions include onset (sudden vs gradual), duration, pattern, precipitating events (URI, trauma, chemical exposure), medications, smoking history, associated symptoms, and comorbidities.

### Physical Examination

- Nasal endoscopy (flexible and rigid) for polyps, congestion, deviated septum, masses
- Assessment of olfactory meatal area
- Neurological evaluation: CN II, V, VII; visual acuity, fields, optic disc
- Nasal tickle test (CN V assessment)

### Quantitative Olfactory Testing

**UPSIT (University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test):**
- 40-item microencapsulated "scratch and sniff" test
- Self-administered in 10-15 minutes
- Classifies function into: normosmia, mild/moderate/severe microsmia, anosmia, probable malingering
- Chance performance = 10/40 (very low scores suggest malingering)
- Test-retest reliability: r = 0.94
- Available in >30 languages

### Imaging

- **CT:** Most useful and cost-effective for sinonasal inflammatory disorders; evaluates bony structures, cribriform plate, olfactory cleft
- **MRI:** Evaluates soft tissue, olfactory bulbs, tracts, and cortical parenchyma
- Plain radiographs: rarely useful

## PART E: TREATMENT OF SMELL DISORDERS

### Conductive Anosmia

- **Systemic steroids** temporarily reverse conductive impairment in 83% of patients (useful diagnostic tool); topical steroids effective in only 25%
- Topical nasal steroids often fail as steroid does not reach the upper nasal passages; **Moffett's (head-down) position** increases efficacy
- Proper allergy management (antihistamines)
- Antibiotics for suspected bacterial infection
- Surgery for: large medically-refractory polyps; suspected malignant neoplasm

### Sensorineural Anosmia

- Prognosis for long-standing total loss from URI or head trauma is poor
- Majority who recover after trauma do so within **12 weeks**
- CNS tumours impinging on olfactory structures can often be resected with improvement
- Anti-epileptic/anti-migraine medications if seizures or migraine suspected
- Immunomodulatory therapy for multiple sclerosis
- No proven efficacy of zinc or vitamin A treatments in absence of frank deficiencies

### Supportive Measures for Complete Anosmia

- Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors must be installed
- Electric appliances preferred over gas
- Food expiration dates must be scrutinized
- Balanced diet to prevent malnutrition; flavour enhancers (MSG, food colouring, stock)

### Prognosis

- Anosmic patients: 56.7% show some change over time, but only **11.3% regain normal** age-related function
- Microsmic patients: 42.9% show change; **23.3% regain normal** function
- Predictors of recovery: age, initial severity, and duration of dysfunction

## PART F: EFFECTS OF AIR POLLUTION AND OCCUPATIONAL EXPOSURE

### Occupational Rhinitis (OR) - Definition

OR is an inflammatory disease of the nose characterized by intermittent or persistent symptoms (congestion, sneezing, rhinorrhoea, itching) and/or variable nasal airflow limitation arising from causes attributable to a particular work environment. Symptoms usually manifest on weekdays and abate during weekends and holidays. Over 200 chemicals and organic dusts have been implicated.

### Pathogenesis

Inhaled materials impact on the nasal mucosa based on their aerodynamic equivalent diameter (AED):

- AED >9 μm: ~80% deposit in the nose
- AED 2-9 μm: ~50% deposit
- AED <2 μm: ~40% deposit; remainder passes to lower airways
- Highly soluble materials (ammonia, SO2, formaldehyde) deposit in the upper airway

### Classification

| Type | Mechanism | Onset |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Allergic OR | IgE-mediated (Type 1); late phase 4-6 hours | Latency 2 months - 18 years |
| Irritant OR | Neurogenic inflammation via substance P from C-fibres | Immediate |
| RUDS | Acute high-dose irritant exposure | Persistent after exposure |
| Corrosive | Excessive concentration of soluble gases | Immediate tissue damage |

### High-Risk Occupations

Furriers (highest risk), livestock breeders, bakers, farmers, food-processing workers, wood workers, detergent manufacturers, animal laboratory workers, hairdressers, and healthcare workers.

### Air Pollutants Causing Olfactory Dysfunction

Reported agents include: acetone, formaldehyde, benzene, hydrogen sulphide, cadmium, lead, nickel, nitrous gases, paint solvents, chromium, chlorine, trichloroethylene, cement, carbon disulphide.

- Anosmia/hyposmia reported after single high-dose exposure to sulphuric acid, hydrogen selenide, phosphorus oxychloride
- Chronic low-level exposure to benzene, cadmium, carbon disulphide, formaldehyde also causes olfactory dysfunction
- Tobacco smoking: diminished olfactory ability proportional to cumulative smoking dose; cessation leads to improvement

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Three commonest causes of permanent anosmia: upper respiratory infections, head trauma, and nasal/sinus disease
> 2. Olfactory dysfunction may be the first clinical sign of Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease
> 3. UPSIT is the most widely used olfactory test; scores below chance (10/40) suggest malingering
> 4. Systemic steroids reverse conductive anosmia in 83% vs only 25% for topical steroids - a useful diagnostic tool
> 5. Foster-Kennedy syndrome: ipsilateral anosmia + ipsilateral optic atrophy + contralateral papilloedema
> 6. Only 10-15% of inspired air reaches the olfactory neuroepithelium; minor nasal architectural changes can cause significant olfactory impairment
> 7. Excessive turbinate removal can paradoxically worsen olfaction by shunting air away from the olfactory cleft
> 8. Occupational rhinitis precedes occupational asthma, especially with high molecular weight compounds
> 9. Over 200 chemicals implicated in occupational rhinitis; >80% of particles with AED >9 μm deposit in the nose
> 10. Kallmann's syndrome: anosmia + hypogonadotropic hypogonadism due to olfactory bulb aplasia (X-linked or autosomal recessive)


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Most patients who complain of "taste" loss actually have unrecognized olfactory impairment - retronasal olfaction drives flavour perception
> - Moffett's (head-down) position improves efficacy of topical nasal steroids for olfactory cleft disease
> - Age-related olfactory loss should never be attributed simply to age - cumulative damage is often the culprit
> - Olfactory nerve cells can serve as conduits for viruses into the brain (historical basis for zinc sulphate cauterization of olfactory epithelium during polio epidemics)
> - In PD patients under 60: UPSIT score of 31 has 91% sensitivity and 88% specificity for diagnosis
