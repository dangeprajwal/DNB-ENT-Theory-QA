## Introduction

Atrophic rhinitis, rhinosporidiosis, and midline destructive conditions represent a diverse group of nasal pathologies united by their potential to cause progressive nasal mucosal damage and structural destruction. Primary atrophic rhinitis is characterized by progressive atrophy of the nasal mucosa and underlying turbinate bone, leading to paradoxical nasal obstruction and the hallmark foul-smelling crusts known as ozaena. Rhinosporidiosis is an infectious granulomatous disease caused by an aquatic protozoan, endemic to South Asia and presenting as friable vascular polyps. Midline destructive conditions encompass a spectrum of autoimmune, inflammatory, and neoplastic diseases that destroy the nasal framework, often requiring multidisciplinary management and subsequent nasal reconstruction. Understanding these conditions is essential for the otorhinolaryngologist, as they may be the first clinician to encounter these presentations.

## Part A: Primary Atrophic Rhinitis -- Hypotheses and Aetiology

Primary atrophic rhinitis is a condition that occurs predominantly in women and is characterized by progressive atrophy of the nasal mucosa and underlying bone of the turbinates. This leads to the formation of thick crusts, which leave a constant foul smell (ozaena) in the nose. The nasal cavities are enlarged and there is a paradoxical sensation of nasal congestion despite spacious cavities. The precise aetiology remains unclear, and multiple hypotheses have been proposed.

### Hypotheses for Primary Atrophic Rhinitis

1. **Infective theory:** Klebsiella ozaenae is the most commonly implicated organism. Other bacteria such as Coccobacillus foetidus, Bacillus mucosus, and Diphtheroids have also been isolated. However, whether bacteria represent the primary cause or a secondary colonization of already damaged mucosa remains debated.

2. **Hereditary/genetic theory:** Higher incidence in certain families, ethnic groups, and geographical regions (South Asia, Middle East, Mediterranean) suggests a genetic predisposition. An autosomal dominant pattern with incomplete penetrance has been proposed.

3. **Nutritional deficiency theory:** Deficiency of iron and fat-soluble vitamins (particularly Vitamin A and D) has been linked to squamous metaplasia of the nasal mucosa. The condition is more common in populations with nutritional deficiencies, supporting this theory.

4. **Endocrine theory:** The marked female predominance and onset around puberty suggest hormonal influence. Oestrogen affects nasal mucosal vascularity, and hormonal changes during puberty and menstruation may trigger or worsen the condition.

5. **Autoimmune theory:** Progressive destruction of nasal mucosa resembling autoimmune processes has been observed. Chronic inflammatory infiltrates with features suggestive of immune-mediated tissue damage support this hypothesis.

6. **Vascular/endarteritis theory:** This is the most widely accepted pathogenesis mechanism. Progressive endarteritis of terminal arterioles supplying the nasal mucosa leads to chronic ischaemia, resulting in mucosal atrophy, glandular destruction, and squamous metaplasia. The ischaemia explains the irreversible nature of established disease.

7. **Neurogenic/autonomic theory:** Sympathetic predominance causing vasoconstriction and reduced blood supply to the nasal mucosa has been proposed. Autonomic imbalance may contribute to both reduced vascularity and diminished glandular secretion.

### Primary vs Secondary Atrophic Rhinitis

Primary atrophic rhinitis is distinct from secondary atrophic rhinitis, which develops directly as a result of identifiable causes.

| Feature                | Primary                  | Secondary                  |
|------------------------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| Aetiology              | Unknown/multifactorial   | Identifiable cause         |
| Age of onset           | Young females, puberty   | Any age                    |
| Common causes          | Klebsiella (debated)     | Surgery, granulomas, XRT   |
| Bilateral involvement  | Usually bilateral        | Often unilateral           |
| Progression            | Chronic progressive      | Related to primary cause   |

Secondary causes include:
- Granulomatous nasal infections (TB, syphilis, rhinoscleroma, leprosy)
- [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|Chronic rhinosinusitis]]
- Excessive nasal surgery (particularly radical turbinectomy -- described as a "nasal crime" by Huizing)
- Trauma and irradiation
- Empty nose syndrome after total inferior turbinate resection

## Part B: Atrophic Rhinitis -- Clinical Features, Diagnosis and Management

### Clinical Features

- **Ozaena** (foul-smelling crusts) -- the hallmark symptom; thick greenish-black crusts with a characteristic putrid odour
- **Paradoxical nasal obstruction** -- despite roomy nasal cavities, patients feel blocked due to crusting over large cavities and loss of normal mucosal sensation
- **[[Epistaxis Management|Epistaxis]]** -- from crusting trauma and mucosal friability
- **Merciful anosmia** (hyposmia/anosmia) -- the patient is characteristically unaware of the foul smell due to olfactory mucosal atrophy and adaptation
- **Nasal examination findings:** roomy cavities, pale/atrophic mucosa, absent or atrophic turbinates, greenish-black crusts adherent to the mucosa
- Pharyngeal and laryngeal dryness from chronic mouth breathing

### Investigations

- **Nasal endoscopy:** atrophic mucosa, spacious cavities, crusting, absent turbinate landmarks
- **CT PNS:** atrophic turbinates, widened nasal cavity, lateral nasal wall resorption
- **Culture:** may isolate Klebsiella ozaenae (not always positive)
- **Biopsy:** if needed to exclude granulomatous diseases, malignancy; shows squamous metaplasia replacing ciliated columnar epithelium
- **Blood tests:** iron studies, Vitamin A levels; ANCA and ACE to exclude vasculitis and sarcoidosis

### Management

**1. Conservative Management (Mainstay of Treatment):**

- **Nasal douching/irrigation:** alkaline solutions (saline, sodium bicarbonate) to loosen and remove crusts; 25% glucose in glycerine drops to promote mucosal moisture
- **Nasal hygiene:** regular gentle mechanical removal of crusts under endoscopic guidance
- **Topical antibiotics:** mupirocin ointment, chloramphenicol drops
- **Systemic antibiotics:** targeted against Klebsiella ozaenae; aminoglycosides (streptomycin), ciprofloxacin, rifampicin for 6-12 weeks course
- **Secretion stimulants:** potassium iodide (to increase nasal glandular secretion)
- **Vitamin supplements:** Vitamin A, iron supplementation for deficiency states
- **Oestrogen nasal spray:** to stimulate mucosal regeneration and glandular function
- **Placental extract:** submucosal injection to promote vascularity

**2. Surgical Management:**

- **Young's operation (1967):** complete closure of the nostril(s) using mucosal flaps to allow mucosal recovery. The temporary closure (6 months to years) creates a warm, moist environment that promotes natural regeneration of mucosal glands and reversal of squamous metaplasia. Modified Young's operation involves partial closure.
- **Submucosal implantation:** injection of Teflon paste, cartilage, or bone chips submucosally to reduce nasal cavity size and improve airflow dynamics
- **Sternberg procedure:** insertion of cartilage or bone grafts to medialize the lateral nasal wall
- **Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) studies** have demonstrated that radical turbinectomy causes aerodynamic changes akin to atrophic rhinitis, while conservative one-third reduction restores near-normal airflow patterns

## Part C: Rhinosporidiosis

### Epidemiology

- **Endemic regions:** South Asia (India -- particularly Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Sri Lanka), Africa, and South America
- **Association with stagnant water:** bathing in ponds, rivers, and lakes in endemic areas is the primary risk factor
- **Demographics:** more common in males; predominantly rural populations; all ages affected with peak incidence in the 2nd-3rd decade of life
- **Occupation:** agricultural workers and those with regular contact with stagnant water bodies

### Etiopathogenesis

Rhinosporidiosis is an infectious disease caused by an aquatic protozoan previously considered to be a fungus. The aetiological agent, **Rhinosporidium seeberi**, is now classified under the DRIP clade (Dermocystidium, Rosette agent, Ichthyophonus, Psorospermium) -- a group of aquatic protoctistiae closely related to fish pathogens.

**Mode of infection:** Direct contact of traumatized nasal epithelium with contaminated stagnant water containing the infective endospores. Airborne transmission via dust containing dried spores has also been proposed.

**Life cycle in host tissue:**
- Smallest form: endospore (6-10 micrometres)
- Endospore enters traumatized epithelium and undergoes progressive maturation
- Intermediate stage: trophocyte
- Mature stage: sporangium (100-350 micrometres) -- the largest pathogenic organism visible under light microscopy
- Sporangium ruptures, releasing thousands of endospores into surrounding tissue
- Released endospores cause auto-inoculation and local spread/recurrence

**Key point:** Rhinosporidium seeberi cannot be cultured on artificial media. Diagnosis is always histopathological.

### Clinical Features

- **Nasal cavity** is the most common site of involvement (approximately 70%), followed by the nasopharynx and ocular conjunctiva
- Other rare sites include oropharynx, larynx, trachea, skin, and genitalia
- Presents as a **friable, sessile or pedunculated polypoidal mass**
- **"Strawberry-like" appearance:** reddish-pink surface studded with whitish-yellow dots representing mature sporangia visible beneath the surface epithelium
- **Highly vascular** -- bleeds easily on touch
- Symptoms include unilateral nasal obstruction, recurrent epistaxis, rhinorrhoea, and nasal mass sensation
- Large lesions may cause nasal deformity and extension into the nasopharynx

### Diagnosis

| Investigation          | Finding                        |
|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Clinical appearance    | Strawberry-like friable polyp  |
| Histopathology (gold   | Sporangia (100-350 um) with    |
| standard)              | endospores in subepithelial    |
|                        | stroma                         |
| Special stains         | GMS, PAS, Mucicarmine positive |
| KOH mount              | Shows sporangia                |
| Culture                | Cannot be cultured             |

### Management

- **Surgical excision** is the treatment of choice -- wide excision with a cuff of normal tissue
- **Cautery of the base** with electrocautery or diathermy is essential to destroy residual endospores and prevent recurrence
- Avoid excessive manipulation during surgery to minimize endospore spillage
- **Dapsone** (diaminodiphenylsulfone, an anti-leprotic drug) -- used as adjunct to surgery; arrests maturation of sporangia and promotes stromal fibrosis; helps prevent recurrence
- **Recurrence rate** is high (10-70%) primarily due to endospore spillage during surgical excision
- **Prevention:** avoid bathing in stagnant water in endemic areas; health education in endemic communities

## Part D: Midline Destructive Conditions of the Head and Neck

A granuloma is an organized collection of macrophages (epithelioid cells) that may fuse to form multinucleated giant cells. This configuration is encountered in multiple infective, inflammatory, and neoplastic conditions of the nose and sinuses. The midline destructive conditions are a group of diseases that cause progressive destruction of the nasal framework and midfacial structures.

### Differential Diagnosis of Midline Conditions

| Condition              | ANCA Pattern         | Key Diagnostic Feature       |
|------------------------|----------------------|------------------------------|
| GPA (Wegener's)        | c-ANCA (PR3)         | Triad: ENT + lung + kidney   |
| EGPA (Churg-Strauss)   | p-ANCA (MPO) 40-75%  | Asthma (99%) + eosinophilia  |
| CIMDL (cocaine)        | p-ANCA (HNE) ~90%    | Palatal perforation, cocaine |
| Sarcoidosis            | ANCA negative         | Non-caseating granuloma      |
| ENKTCL (NK/T-cell)     | ANCA negative         | CD56+, EBV+, skin destruct. |
| Eosinophilic granuloma | ANCA negative         | Punched-out lytic bone       |

### 1. Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA / Wegener's)

GPA is a systemic condition characterized by granulomatous inflammation of the respiratory tract and necrotizing vasculitis affecting small- to medium-sized vessels with focal glomerulonephritis. It classically involves a triad of upper airway, lung, and renal disease. Limited or localized forms occur in up to 30% of cases.

- **Epidemiology:** mean age at diagnosis 40-50 years; equal sex distribution; predominantly Caucasian (>90%); incidence 10-15 per million per year
- **Aetiology:** autoimmune -- strong association with ANCA. c-ANCA binds to and activates neutrophils, releasing oxygen radicals and lytic enzymes that damage vascular endothelium. Staphylococcus aureus nasal carriage associated with more frequent relapses.
- **Sinonasal involvement:** >80% of cases; symptoms include nasal obstruction, crusting, discharge, bleeding, facial pain; destruction of septum, turbinates, and sinuses with characteristic nasal collapse (saddle nose deformity)
- **Diagnosis:** c-ANCA (PR3) sensitivity 91%, specificity 99% in generalized active disease; sensitivity falls to 60% in localized disease. CT shows mucosal thickening, bone destruction, and new bone formation ("tramlining" on MRI). Histology shows granulomatous inflammation, vasculitis, and necrosis.
- **Treatment:** remission induction with cyclophosphamide + high-dose steroids (93% remission rate). Rituximab (anti-CD20) is as effective as cyclophosphamide and may be superior in relapsing disease (RAVE trial). Maintenance with methotrexate or azathioprine. Untreated, mean survival is 5 months.

### 2. Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA / Churg-Strauss)

- **Asthma** occurs in approximately 99% of patients, characteristically late-onset, preceding vasculitis by an average of 8 years
- **Sinonasal symptoms** in up to 93%; nasal polyps in up to 75%
- **p-ANCA (MPO)** positive in 40-75% of cases
- **Cardiac involvement** is the major cause of mortality (granulomatous myocarditis)
- **Three phases:** prodromal (asthma + URI), eosinophilic (peripheral eosinophilia, pulmonary infiltrates), and vasculitic (systemic involvement)
- **Treatment:** high-dose systemic steroids; cyclophosphamide for organ/life-threatening disease; overall remission rates 91%

### 3. Cocaine-Induced Midline Destructive Lesion (CIMDL)

- Chronic intranasal cocaine use causes mucosal inflammation; septal perforation in approximately 5% of chronic users; more aggressive midfacial destruction can occur
- **ANCA:** nearly 90% have p-ANCA against human neutrophil elastase (HNE-ANCA) -- this is specific for CIMDL and not found in GPA. Over 50% also have positive PR3-ANCA, making differentiation from localized GPA difficult.
- **Key differentiating feature:** palatal perforation is more common in CIMDL than GPA, where it occurs only rarely
- **Treatment:** no role for immunosuppression; must stop cocaine; conservative management with nasal douching and antibiotics; surgical correction only after 6-12 months cocaine-free

### 4. Sarcoidosis

- Systemic granulomatous condition of unknown aetiology; sinonasal involvement in 1-4% of cases
- Nasal mucosa shows a characteristic granular "strawberry skin" appearance with tiny pale granulomas against hypertrophic erythematous mucosa
- **Lupus pernio:** cutaneous manifestation with purplish thickening of nasal skin; indicates chronic systemic sarcoid
- **Diagnosis:** non-caseating granulomas on biopsy; serum ACE elevated in up to 85% (non-specific); chest staging I-IV
- **Treatment:** systemic steroids; methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine (response <50%); TNF-alpha inhibitors (infliximab); surgery has limited role; septal surgery should be avoided due to increased perforation risk

### 5. Extranodal NK/T-Cell Lymphoma (ENKTCL)

- Previously known as "lethal midline granuloma"; a rare non-Hodgkin's lymphoma causing midface destruction
- **EBV-driven**; most common in South-East Asia and South America
- Progressive destruction including the overlying skin (unlike GPA, which spares the midfacial skin)
- **Immunohistochemistry:** CD56+, CD2+, cytoplasmic CD3+; angiocentric growth pattern
- **Treatment:** dramatic response to [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] for localized disease; role of chemotherapy not established; 5-year survival 38-45%

### 6. Eosinophilic Granuloma (Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis)

- Clonal proliferation of Langerhans cells; now regarded as a neoplastic condition with variable clinical course
- 85% detected in first three decades; 60% in young children; males affected twice as frequently
- Skull bones most common site; punched-out lytic lesions on imaging with bevelled margins
- **Treatment:** solitary lesions -- curettage/excision + radiotherapy; multifocal disease -- etoposide and steroids for 12+ months; alpha-interferon and bone marrow transplantation for refractory cases

## Part E: Nasal Plastic Reconstruction Options After Midline Destruction

Reconstruction of the nose poses unique challenges due to the complex three-dimensional contours, variable skin quality, and the need to restore both function and aesthetics. The nose has five convex and four concave aesthetic subunits (tip, dorsum, paired sidewalls, paired ala-nostril sills, soft triangles, and columella). The fundamental principle is to recreate missing structures as closely as possible, replacing like with like.

### Reconstruction Options

**1. Non-Surgical Options:**

- **Septal button (Silastic obturator):** for septal perforations; simple, well-tolerated; covers inflamed perforation margins and reduces crusting and epistaxis; particularly useful in GPA where surgical repair is not recommended due to active disease risk. Success reported in 72.8% of cases remaining in place.
- **Nasal prosthesis (epithesis):** external nasal prosthesis for patients with extensive midfacial destruction; can be retained by adhesive, magnets, or osseointegrated implants; useful when surgical reconstruction is contraindicated

**2. Augmentation Rhinoplasty (for Saddle Nose Deformity):**

- Autografts: costal (rib) cartilage, iliac crest bone, calvarial bone
- Alloplastic: silicone, Medpor (porous polyethylene)
- **In GPA:** should be deferred until disease has been in remission for at least one year; a series of 13 patients in remission reported 92% overall success rate from dorsal augmentation without reactivation of disease

**3. Paramedian Forehead Flap:**

- The **workhorse** of nasal reconstruction and consistently gives the best results in major nasal reconstruction
- Axial pattern flap based on the ipsilateral supratrochlear artery
- Improved modification of the midline forehead flap; positioned to one side of the midline
- Provides excellent colour and texture match with nasal skin
- Can be used for subunit repair, heminasal reconstruction, or total nasal reconstruction
- Two-stage procedure: pedicle divided after flap develops sufficient vascularity at recipient site
- Donor site often heals well, even when left to heal by secondary intention
- Can be combined with composite conchal graft for internal lining and skeletal support

**4. Nasolabial Flap:**

- Suitable for alar subunit reconstruction; based on perforating branches of facial and angular arteries
- Can be superiorly or inferiorly based; pedicle divided at second procedure or raised as an island flap with 180-degree rotation for primary closure
- The "note flap" is a variant (superiorly based nasolabial flap) that does not require a second stage

**5. Local Flaps for Smaller Defects:**

- **Bilobed flap (Zitelli's):** ideal for defects of lower third of nose, 0.5-1.5 cm diameter; each lobe rotates no more than 50 degrees
- **Dorsal nasal flap:** skin and soft tissue elevated off periosteum/perichondrium and slid down to cover lower third defects
- **Glabellar rotation flap:** useful for upper third of nose and medial canthus defects
- **Cheek advancement flap:** with Z-plasty and/or back cut to extend reach

**6. [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|Free Flap]] Reconstruction:**

- For extensive full-thickness defects not amenable to regional flaps
- Radial forearm free flap (thin, pliable, but bulky fat), dorsalis pedis free flap (thin but significant donor site morbidity), retroauricular free flap (small vessels, precarious anastomosis)
- Combination of forehead flap (cover) + free cartilage graft from septum/concha/rib (framework) + mucosal or composite graft lining is the standard approach for total nasal reconstruction

**7. Cartilage and Bone Grafts (Skeletal Support):**

- Essential for structural integrity; should be placed at the same time as reconstruction
- Septal cartilage, conchal cartilage, or rib cartilage provide scaffolding
- Alar margin must always be supported with a cartilage strut graft to prevent notching and contracture
- Composite auricular grafts provide both cartilage support and skin lining

### Principles of Nasal Reconstruction

- Replace like with like; restore all three layers (lining, scaffold, cover)
- Defects involving more than half a subunit should be repaired by replacing the entire subunit
- Avoid tension; approximate vascularized tissue to grafts to prevent dead space
- Excess tissue will contract and "bunch up" -- replace the exact amount of tissue lost
- Without septal support, any reconstruction using grafts or flaps is very difficult
- The history of nasal reconstruction dates back more than 3000 years to Sushrutha (approximately 600 BC) who described the cheek flap; the Indian method using forehead flap remains the basis for most reconstructive procedures today

> 1. Primary atrophic rhinitis is characterized by progressive mucosal and bony atrophy with foul-smelling crusts (ozaena), predominantly in women, with endarteritis of terminal arterioles being the most widely accepted pathogenesis.
> 2. Klebsiella ozaenae is the most commonly implicated organism in primary atrophic rhinitis, though whether it is cause or consequence remains debated.

> 4. Radical turbinectomy causes aerodynamic changes akin to atrophic rhinitis on CFD studies and should be considered a "nasal crime" -- mucosal-preserving techniques are essential.
> 5. Rhinosporidium seeberi is now classified as an aquatic protozoan (not a fungus), closely related to fish pathogens in the DRIP clade.
> 6. Rhinosporidiosis presents as a "strawberry-like" friable vascular polyp; diagnosis is always histopathological (sporangia with endospores) as the organism cannot be cultured.
> 7. Dapsone arrests sporangial maturation and promotes stromal fibrosis, and is used as adjunct to surgery to prevent rhinosporidiosis recurrence.
> 8. c-ANCA (PR3) has 91% sensitivity and 99% specificity for generalized GPA; sensitivity falls to 60% in localized disease.
> 9. HNE-ANCA (not PR3-ANCA) is the specific antibody in CIMDL, and palatal perforation occurs more commonly in CIMDL than GPA -- two key distinguishing features.
> 10. Augmentation rhinoplasty for GPA saddle nose deformity should be deferred until disease has been in remission for at least one year, with 92% success rate without disease reactivation reported.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **"Merciful anosmia"** is a characteristic feature of ozaena -- patients are completely unaware of their foul smell due to progressive olfactory mucosal atrophy and central adaptation.
> - **Secondary atrophic rhinitis** after excessive turbinate surgery highlights the critical importance of mucosal-preserving techniques; CFD studies confirm that one-third turbinate reduction restores near-normal aerodynamics while radical resection replicates atrophic rhinitis physiology.
> - **Rhinosporidiosis recurrence** (10-70%) is predominantly due to endospore spillage during excision -- electrocautery of the base and wide excision margins are essential surgical principles.
> - **CIMDL causes palatal perforation more commonly than GPA** -- this is a clinically useful differentiating feature when evaluating midline destructive disease in a patient who may not admit to cocaine use.
> - **Sarcoidosis nasal mucosa** has a characteristic "strawberry skin" appearance that may be confused with the "strawberry-like" polyps of rhinosporidiosis -- biopsy resolves the diagnosis (non-caseating granuloma vs. sporangia with endospores).
> - **The paramedian forehead flap** based on the supratrochlear artery remains the gold standard for major nasal reconstruction; when combined with a composite conchal graft for lining and support, it consistently produces the best results with low donor site morbidity.
