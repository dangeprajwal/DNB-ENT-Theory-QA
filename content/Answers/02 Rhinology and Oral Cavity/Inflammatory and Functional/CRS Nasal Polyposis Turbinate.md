## Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) is phenotypically divided into CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and CRS without polyps (CRSsNP) based on endoscopic findings. CRS represents a significant disease burden worldwide, affecting at least 11% of the population with substantial economic impact. In the UK, sinonasal disease accounts for at least 120,000 outpatient visits per year with over 40,000 nose and sinus operations performed annually. The UK Sinonasal Audit revealed a surgical revision rate of up to 20% within 5 years. Nasal polyps represent the end-stage local manifestation of chronic inflammatory disease of the sinonasal tract and their exact aetiology remains elusive despite extensive research.

## PART A: Definitions and Classification of CRS

### EPOS Diagnostic Criteria for CRS

Inflammation of the nose and paranasal sinuses associated with **two or more symptoms** (lasting ≥12 weeks), one of which should be nasal blockage/obstruction OR nasal discharge:
- ± facial pain/pressure
- ± reduction or loss of smell

AND either:
- Endoscopic signs: polyps, mucopurulent discharge from middle meatus, or oedema/mucosal obstruction primarily in the middle meatus

AND/OR:
- CT changes: mucosal changes within the osteomeatal complex and/or sinuses

### Phenotypic Classification

| Phenotype | Pathophysiology | Key Features |
|-----------|----------------|--------------|
| CRSwNP | TH2-skewed eosinophilic inflammation; high IL-5 and ECP | Oedematous stroma, pseudocyst formation, albumin deposition |
| CRSsNP | TH1 milieu; increased IFN-γ | Fibrosis, basement membrane thickening, goblet cell hyperplasia, mononuclear infiltration |
| AERD (Samter's triad) | COX inhibition → leukotriene overproduction | Asthma + polyposis + aspirin sensitivity; ~15% of CRSwNPs |
| [[AFRS Fungal Ball|AFRS]] | Type 1 hypersensitivity to fungal antigens | Unilateral/bilateral polyps, eosinophilic mucin, characteristic CT (double density) |

### Type 2 CRS (CRSwNP Endotype)

Approximately 80% of nasal polyps in Western countries are characterized by:
- Robust T-helper 2 response
- Eosinophilic infiltration
- Decreased T-regulatory function
- Abundance of IL-5 cytokine
- High tissue IgE levels (often polyclonal, independent of serum IgE)
- Functional mucosal IgE inducing mast cell degranulation independent of serum IgE

Note: Asian studies have identified a predominantly TH1/TH17 pattern within polyp tissue, highlighting heterogeneity. In cystic fibrosis, patients exhibit neutrophilia rather than eosinophilia.

### Classification of Nasal Polyps

| Type | Subtype | Examples |
|------|---------|---------|
| Localized (CRSwNP-localized) | Reactive polyp | Secondary to prior inflammation, sinus ostia obstruction |
| | Neoplastic | [[Schneiderian Papilloma|Inverted papilloma]], sinonasal malignancy |
| Diffuse (CRSwNP-diffuse) | Host factors | Poor OMC function, AERD |
| | Atopy | [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|Allergic rhinitis]]-associated |
| | External agent | Staphylococcal superantigen |
| | Combined host/external | AFRS and derivatives |
| Systemic | | Cystic fibrosis, EGPA (Churg-Strauss) |

## PART B: Nasal Polyposis — Pathogenesis & Associated Diseases

### Pathogenesis

Nasal polyps are oedematous grape-like protrusions most often originating around the osteomeatal complex on the lateral wall. Histologically they consist of loose connective tissue, inflammatory cells and fluid, covered by pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium. Polyps form when oedematous connective tissue stroma ruptures and herniates through the basement membrane.

**Proposed mechanisms driving polyp formation:**

1. **Bacterial superantigens:** Non-invasive S. aureus secretes exotoxins causing localized eosinophilic inflammation. Superantigens can activate up to 20% of the body's T cells (vs 0.001–0.0001% for regular antigens)
2. **Fungal antigens:** T-cell mediated hypersensitivity to fungi; peripheral blood mononuclear cells of CRS patients react to Alternaria extracts
3. **Allergic allergens:** 25.6% of allergic patients have nasal polyps vs 3.9% in controls
4. **Biofilms:** Bacteria enclosed within self-developed polysaccharide matrix — inaccessible to antimicrobials; associated with worse postoperative outcome
5. **Osteitis:** May act as a marker of severity; occurs more often in revision cases

### Associated Diseases

| Disease | Polyp Prevalence | Key Features |
|---------|-----------------|--------------|
| Asthma | 30–71% of NP patients have asthma | Same eosinophilic basis; late-onset asthma → polyps in 9–13 years |
| AERD (Samter's Triad) | 30–60% of AERD patients | Non-atopic; COX inhibition → leukotriene overproduction; highest recurrence rates (100% at 10 years) |
| AFRS | 80% of fungal sinusitis patients | Bent-Kuhn criteria: Type I hypersensitivity, polyps, characteristic CT, eosinophilic mucin, positive fungal stain |
| Cystic fibrosis | Up to 86% of CF patients | Autosomal recessive; neutrophil-driven IL-8 inflammation; Pseudomonas/S. aureus; hypoplastic frontal/sphenoid sinuses |
| PCD (Kartagener's) | ~40% with PCD; 27% with Kartagener's | Absent mucociliary clearance; bronchiectasis + CRS + situs inversus |
| EGPA (Churg-Strauss) | ~50% of EGPA patients | Systemic small-vessel vasculitis; asthma + eosinophilia + nasal polyps; ANCA positivity |

## PART C: Diagnosis and Staging

### Diagnostic Investigations

- **Rigid nasal endoscopy** — gold standard for clinical assessment; identifies polyps, discharge, crusting
- **CT scanning** — mandatory before surgical intervention; coronal sections for assessing extent and anatomy
- **MRI** — helpful to differentiate from tumour or intracranial extension
- **Allergy testing** — SPT or RAST
- **Nasal biopsy** — exclusion of neoplasia, granulomas, eosinophilia, fungal hyphae
- **Blood tests** — FBC (eosinophilia), ANCA (GPA), ACE (sarcoidosis)
- **Olfactory testing** — psychophysical (threshold, discrimination) and OERPs
- **SNOT-22** — validated patient-reported outcome measure

### Lund-Mackay CT Staging System

| Sinus | Score |
|-------|-------|
| Maxillary | 0 (normal), 1 (partial opacification), 2 (complete) |
| Anterior ethmoid | 0, 1, 2 |
| Posterior ethmoid | 0, 1, 2 |
| Sphenoid | 0, 1, 2 |
| Frontal | 0, 1, 2 |
| Osteomeatal complex | 0 (not occluded), 2 (occluded) |
| **Maximum per side** | **12** |
| **Maximum total** | **24** |

### Endoscopic Staging of Nasal Polyps

- Grade 0: No polyps
- Grade 1: Polyps in middle meatus only
- Grade 2: Polyps extending beyond middle meatus

## PART D: Medical Management of CRS

### Pharmacological Agents

| Agent | Role in CRS |
|-------|------------|
| Intranasal corticosteroids (INCS) | First-line; reduce polyp size, improve airflow, decrease recurrence. Drops more effective than sprays for olfaction |
| Systemic corticosteroids | Prednisolone 0.5 mg/kg for 5–10 days for large polyps; short courses only |
| Nasal saline douching | Symptomatic benefit; use sterilized/distilled water |
| Macrolide antibiotics | 3-month course (e.g. clarithromycin) — increases mucociliary transport, accelerates neutrophil apoptosis; cardiac toxicity risk (QT prolongation) |
| Antihistamines | Only helpful if allergy present |
| Leukotriene inhibitors | May help patients with coexisting asthma and/or aspirin sensitivity |

**INCS side effects:** Local irritation, [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]]; rare systemic effects (adrenal suppression, Cushing's syndrome from absorption). Salivary cortisol monitoring useful for long-term users. Drops should not be used >2 months at one time without specialist supervision.

### Biological Agents in CRS

For refractory Type 2 CRS/CRSwNP not responding to standard medical and surgical therapy:
- **Dupilumab** (anti-IL-4Rα) — blocks IL-4 and IL-13 signalling; reduces polyp size and improves symptoms
- **Omalizumab** (anti-IgE) — reduces IgE-mediated inflammation
- **Mepolizumab/Benralizumab** (anti-IL-5/IL-5Rα) — targets eosinophilic inflammation

Indications for biologicals: Severe CRSwNP with Type 2 inflammation, failed maximal medical therapy, previous surgery with recurrence, significant comorbid asthma. These agents were emerging at the time of the textbook's publication and have since gained regulatory approval for CRSwNP.

## PART E: Surgical Management of CRS

### Indications for Surgery

- Failed maximal medical treatment (minimum 3 months INCS + nasal douching ± systemic steroids)
- Need for histological diagnosis (unilateral polyps — malignancy until proved otherwise)
- Complications of CRS (orbital, intracranial)

### FESS for CRSwNP

[[FESS Steps Complications|Functional endoscopic sinus surgery]] aims to improve sinus ventilation and drainage and remove polyps. The extent of surgery varies with disease extent. FESS permits better view of the surgical field with more precise clearance than conventional surgery. Benefits are greatest for patients with polyps and nasal obstruction. Coexistent asthma, allergic rhinitis or aspirin intolerance does not decrease the benefit.

**Post-operative management:**
- Nasal douching
- INCS or systemic corticosteroids
- Compliance with post-operative treatment influences long-term efficacy
- Good evidence that post-operative topical nasal steroids reduce polyp recurrence

### Recurrence Rates

| Subgroup | Polyp-free at 5 years | Polyp-free at 10 years |
|----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Control (no asthma, no AERD) | 84% | 78% |
| Asthma | 55% | 45% |
| AERD | 10% | 0% |

## PART F: Inferior Turbinate — Causes & Surgical Management

### Causes of Bilateral Inferior Turbinate Hypertrophy

1. **Allergic rhinitis** — gross congestion, pale purplish mucosa
2. **Non-allergic perennial rhinitis** — degenerative change and fibrosis in lamina propria
3. **Rhinitis medicamentosa** — chronic vasoconstrictor abuse (must exclude first)
4. **Chronic rhinosinusitis** — secondary to underlying sinus disease
5. **Compensatory hypertrophy** — opposite a deviated septum
6. **Sarcoidosis** — unusual but can cause severe enlargement
7. **Pregnancy rhinitis** — hormonal-mediated

### Hol & Huizing Classification of Inferior Turbinate Enlargement

1. Compensatory hypertrophy (opposite deviated septum)
2. Protruded turbinate (extends medially more than expected)
3. Hyperplasia of turbinate head (obstructs nasal valve)
4. Hyperplasia of whole turbinate (chronic rhinitis)
5. Hyperplasia of turbinate tail (chronic sinusitis/post-nasal discharge)

### Important Principle

- First-line treatment for enlarged inferior turbinates is ALWAYS medication (topical nasal steroids for minimum 3 months)
- Specialist rhinologists operate on inferior turbinates infrequently
- If underlying CRS is corrected by FESS ± septal surgery, the turbinate often responds positively without turbinate reduction
- Examine BEFORE and AFTER topical decongestant — chronically congested turbinates do not reduce in size

### Surgical Techniques

| Category | Technique | Key Features |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Mucosal preservation | Lateralization (outfracture) | Lowest risk; least airway improvement; useful adjunct to FESS |
| Mucosal preservation | Submucosal diathermy (SMD) | Monopolar needle, 70W; short-term results good, long-term variable |
| Technology-specific | Radiofrequency ablation | Submucosal tissue reduction via controlled thermal energy |
| Technology-specific | Laser (CO2, diode, KTP) | Tissue reduction by coagulation |
| Technology-specific | Coblation | Lower temperature tissue reduction |
| Mucosal reduction | Microdebrider turbinoplasty | Safe and effective in experienced hands; reduces medial mucosal bulk |
| Excisional | Partial inferior turbinectomy | Anterior subtotal may carry reduced post-op bleeding risk |
| Excisional | Total inferior turbinectomy | Risk of severe haemorrhage (3–9%); risk of empty nose syndrome |

### Complications of Turbinate Surgery

- **Haemorrhage** — severe in 3–9% of turbinectomies; inferior turbinate artery runs in bony tunnels preventing constriction
- **Crusting** — especially after excessive diathermy
- **Adhesions** — synechiae to septum
- **Empty nose syndrome** — rare but debilitating; from excessive mucosal/turbinate removal
- **Atrophic rhinitis** — from total turbinectomy
- **Nasolacrimal duct injury** — anatomical proximity

## PART G: Congenital Conditions and CRS

### Anatomical Variations Predisposing to CRS

- Haller cells (infra-orbital ethmoid cells) — narrow the OMC
- Concha bullosa — pneumatized middle turbinate
- Paradoxically curved middle turbinate
- Uncinate process attachment variations
- Deviated nasal septum with spur

### Congenital/Systemic Conditions Associated with CRS

| Condition | CRS Mechanism |
|-----------|--------------|
| Cystic fibrosis | CFTR mutation → thick tenacious mucus → impaired clearance; neutrophilic IL-8; S. aureus/Pseudomonas; polyps in up to 86% |
| Primary ciliary dyskinesia | Absent/abnormal ciliary beating → mucus retention → recurrent infection; polyps in ~40% |
| Kartagener's syndrome | PCD + bronchiectasis + situs inversus; polyps in 27% |
| Young's syndrome | Obstructive azoospermia + bronchiectasis + sinus disease; rare |
| EGPA (Churg-Strauss) | Systemic vasculitis; asthma + eosinophilia + polyps (~50%) |
| Immunodeficiency states | Hypogammaglobulinaemia, HIV |

### Evaluating CF in a Child with CRS

1. **Suspect CF:** Diffuse bilateral nasal polyposis in a child with total sinus opacification AND hypoplastic frontal/sphenoid sinuses
2. **Sweat test** — gold standard screening (elevated chloride >60 mmol/L)
3. **Genetic testing** — CFTR gene mutations on chromosome 7
4. **CT findings:** Hypoplasia of frontal/sphenoid sinuses, demineralization and medial displacement of uncinate process
5. **Nasal endoscopy:** Bilateral polyposis with thick rhinorrhoea, facial deformities (hypertelorism)
6. **Microbiology:** Sinus cultures — S. aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa
7. **Note:** Mucocele formation not uncommon in CF — presence of mucoceles in a child should raise suspicion

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. CRS is defined by ≥12 weeks of symptoms (blockage/discharge + pain/hyposmia) with endoscopic or CT confirmation; divided into CRSwNP and CRSsNP.
> 2. CRSwNP is predominantly TH2/eosinophilic (80% in Western countries) with high IL-5 and tissue IgE; CRSsNP is TH1-driven with fibrosis.
> 3. Nasal polyps form when oedematous stroma ruptures and herniates through the basement membrane — proposed mechanisms include superantigens, fungi, allergy and biofilms.
> 4. AERD (Samter's Triad) has the worst prognosis — 100% recurrence at 10 years post-FESS; COX inhibition shunts arachidonic acid to leukotriene pathway.
> 5. Lund-Mackay CT scoring: 0–2 per sinus (5 sinuses + OMC), maximum 12 per side, 24 total.
> 6. First-line medical therapy for CRSwNP is INCS; nasal drops more effective than sprays for olfaction but use limited to 2 months without specialist supervision.
> 7. Surgery (FESS) is reserved for failed maximal medical therapy; post-operative INCS reduces recurrence rate.
> 8. Inferior turbinate surgery should preserve mucosa — first-line is always medical therapy; exclude rhinitis medicamentosa before considering surgery.
> 9. CF should be suspected in any child with diffuse bilateral polyposis + total sinus opacification + hypoplastic sinuses — confirm with sweat test.
> 10. Unilateral nasal polyps are tumours until proved otherwise — always biopsy.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **80% of CRSwNP is TH2-driven** but this represents a gross oversimplification — Asian populations show TH1/TH17 patterns, and CF patients show neutrophilia, highlighting disease heterogeneity.
> - **Biologicals (dupilumab, omalizumab, mepolizumab)** represent a paradigm shift for refractory Type 2 CRSwNP but are reserved for severe disease failing maximal medical and surgical therapy.
> - **Superantigens activate 20% of T cells** vs 0.001% for regular antigens — explaining the intense eosinophilic response in S. aureus-associated CRSwNP.
> - **Specialist rhinologists operate on inferior turbinates infrequently** — correcting underlying CRS/septal deviation often resolves turbinate hypertrophy without separate turbinate surgery.
> - **The "polyp-free" rates at 10 years** dramatically illustrate the impact of comorbidities: 78% for controls, 45% for asthma, 0% for AERD — counsel patients accordingly.
> - **A unilateral polyp in an older patient or anyone exposed to wood dust** should always raise suspicion for malignancy — biopsy is mandatory.
