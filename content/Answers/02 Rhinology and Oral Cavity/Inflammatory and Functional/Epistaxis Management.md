## Introduction

Epistaxis is defined as bleeding from the nose and represents one of otolaryngology's commonest and most difficult emergencies. It has a bimodal age distribution — common in childhood, less common in early adult life, then peaks in the sixth decade. Between 7–14% of adults experience epistaxis at some time but only 6% are seen by otorhinolaryngologists. In Scotland, epistaxis is the commonest reason for adult admission to an ENT ward. The annual admission rate in the northern UK is 30 per 100,000 per annum. The simplest treatment — pinching the ala nasi — is called the Hippocratic technique. Fewer than 10% of hospitalized patients require a general anaesthetic procedure. After head and neck cancer, epistaxis is a prominent cause of mortality in ENT patients.

## PART A: Surgical Anatomy of the Nasal Vascular System

### Arterial Supply

The nose receives blood supply from both external and internal carotid arteries, which anastomose extensively within the lateral wall, septum and across the midline.

**External Carotid Artery contributions:**

| Branch | Area Supplied |
|--------|--------------|
| Superior labial artery (facial) | Anterior septum, vestibule |
| Ascending palatine artery | Small area of nasal cavity |
| Greater palatine artery | Anteroinferior nasal floor & septum |
| Sphenopalatine artery (maxillary) | Main supply — posterior septum & lateral wall |

The **sphenopalatine artery (SPA)** is the most important supply to the nasal cavity. It enters through the sphenopalatine foramen and immediately divides into:
- **Posterior septal branch** — runs medially across the sphenoid face, then anteroinferiorly in the mucoperiochondrium; terminal branches anastomose in Little's area
- **Posterior lateral branches** — give rise to inferior and middle turbinate arteries which run in bony tunnels within the turbinates (Burnham, 1935)

**Internal Carotid Artery contributions:**
- **Anterior ethmoidal artery** — larger vessel; runs under superior oblique muscle to anterior ethmoidal canal; supplies nasal roof, olfactory cleft, superior turbinate; absent in up to 14% of individuals
- **Posterior ethmoidal artery** — smaller; present in only 80% of individuals; enters posterior ethmoidal foramen 5 mm anterior to optic canal; supplies posterosuperior nasal cavity

The view that the middle turbinate marks a watershed between internal and external carotid circulations is erroneous — the anastomotic network allows varying directions of flow.

### Key Clinical Areas

**Little's Area (Kiesselbach's Plexus):**
An arterial plexus on the anterior septum — the most frequent site of epistaxis. Identified by James Little (1879) and Kiesselbach (1880). Receives contributions from superior labial, greater palatine, anterior ethmoidal and terminal branches of posterior septal artery.

**Woodruff's Plexus:**
A plexus of vessels lying inferior to the posterior end of the inferior turbinate. Originally considered a frequent site of posterior epistaxis. However, it has been confirmed as a venous plexus and is thus very unlikely to be important in epistaxis.

**Retro-columellar vein:**
Runs 2 mm behind and parallel to the columella — a common cause of venous epistaxis in children.

### Surgical Anatomy of the Sphenopalatine Foramen

The SPF is the portal for the major arterial supply of the nasal cavity. It is formed by a U-shaped notch in the vertical portion of the palatine bone, closed posterosuperiorly by the sphenoid bone. It transmits the SPA, vein and nasopalatine nerve (maxillary division of trigeminal). The **crista ethmoidalis** — a small bony projection anterior to the foramen in 96% of cases — is the key surgical landmark for identifying the foramen during ESPAL.

### Venous Drainage

Veins follow the arteries within the mucosa. The lateral wall drains through the SPF into the pterygoid venous plexus and to the internal jugular vein. Anteriorly, drainage is via superior labial and greater palatine veins to the facial vein and external jugular system.

## PART B: Classification of Epistaxis

| Classification | Categories |
|---------------|------------|
| Age-based | Childhood (<16 years) vs Adult (>16 years) |
| Cause | Primary (idiopathic, 70–80%) vs Secondary (proven cause) |
| Location | Anterior (anterior to piriform aperture) vs Posterior (posterior to piriform aperture — subdivided into lateral wall, septal, floor) |
| Severity | Recurrent (minor, non-life-threatening) vs Acute severe (one-time, requires hospitalization) |

In posterior epistaxis, the septum is the principal source — 70% of posterior bleeds are septal in origin, 24% from the lateral wall (McGarry study of 50 patients).

The severity of epistaxis is inversely proportionate to its frequency — recurrent bleeds tend to be minor while acute severe bleeds are often a one-time event.

### Aetiological Factors in Adult Primary Epistaxis

| Factor | Association |
|--------|------------|
| Weather/chronobiology | Proven — peaks in autumn/winter; circadian biphasic pattern (morning and late evening) |
| NSAIDs | Proven — anti-platelet aggregation effect via altered platelet membrane physiology |
| Alcohol | Proven — prolongs bleeding time despite normal platelet counts |
| Hypertension | No proven causal relationship |
| Septal deviation | No clear case-control evidence of association |

## PART C: Management of Adult Primary Epistaxis

### Management Algorithm

1. **Resuscitation** → IV access, baseline blood tests, coagulation studies only if positive history
2. **Initial examination** → headlight, suction, vasoconstrictor solutions (lignocaine + pseudoephedrine)
3. **If vessel located** → **Direct therapy** (bipolar cautery, chemical cautery, silver nitrate)
4. **If vessel NOT located** → Endoscopy → if still not found → **Indirect therapy** (packing)
5. **If continued bleeding** → Posterior packs, septal surgery, ligation (ESPAL)
6. **If still uncontrolled** → Angiography and embolization

### Direct Therapies

Direct (bleeding point-specific) therapies are logically and theoretically superior to indirect treatments. Over 90% of anterior epistaxis can be controlled with silver nitrate cautery or bipolar. The use of packing for primary anterior epistaxis is unwarranted and should be strongly discouraged.

**Endoscopic control:** Identifies the source of posterior epistaxis in over 80% of cases. Success rates for immediate control are consistently in the 90% range. Monopolar diathermy should NOT be used in the nasal cavity (reports of blindness due to current propagation).

### Indirect Therapies

**Nasal packing:** Ribbon gauze impregnated with petroleum jelly or BIPP, left for 24–72 hours. Re-bleeding with packs in situ occurs in up to 40% of cases. Complications include sinusitis, [[Septal Perforation Repair|septal perforation]], alar necrosis, hypoxia, myocardial infarction. Modern alternatives: Merocel tampons, Kaltostat, balloon catheters (Brighton, Epistat).

**Hot water irrigation:** Irrigation at 50°C — similar success to anterior packing and balloon tamponade (Stangerup). Mechanism unclear — may involve reflex vasodilatation.

**Tranexamic acid:** Reduces severity and risk of re-bleeding at 1.5 g TDS. Does not increase thrombosis risk. Best reserved as adjuvant therapy.

### Surgical Management

**ESPAL (Endonasal Endoscopic Sphenopalatine Artery Ligation):**
- **Principle:** Controls bleed as close as possible to nasal source — SPA is the most logical target
- **Steps:**
  1. Under general or local anaesthetic
  2. Incision made approximately 8 mm anterior to and under cover of the posterior end of the middle turbinate, carried down to bone
  3. Mucosal flap elevated posteriorly until fibroneurovascular sleeve arising from the sphenopalatine foramen is identified
  4. Crista ethmoidalis used as landmark to locate the foramen
  5. Main vessel identified, ligated using haemostatic clips and divided, or coagulated using bipolar diathermy
- **Success rate:** Approaches 100% in some series
- **Complications:** Re-bleeding (anastomoses), infection, nasal adhesions — generally less common than other procedures

**Internal Maxillary Artery Ligation (IMAL):**
Trans-antral via sublabial approach or combined anterior and medial (endoscopic) technique. Posterior wall of antrum entered, branches of IMA clipped within pterygopalatine fossa. Controls haemorrhage in 89% of cases. Complications: sinusitis, infra-orbital nerve damage, oro-antral fistula.

**External Carotid Artery Ligation (ECAL):**
Via skin crease or sternomastoid incision. Carotid bifurcation identified, external carotid ligated in continuity. Reserved for extreme cases or by non-rhinologists. Success in 14/15 patients in one study.

**Anterior/Posterior Ethmoidal Artery Ligation (EAL):**
Via medial canthal incision to anterior lacrimal crest, periosteal elevation, clip and divide anterior ethmoidal artery. Posterior ethmoidal artery located ~12 mm behind. Best reserved as adjunct to other procedures given minimal contribution to nasal blood supply.

**Ligation hierarchy:** SPA → IMA → ECA → Ethmoidal arteries

**Embolization:**
Transfemoral Seldinger angiography; particles (PVA, tungsten, steel microcoils) used to embolize internal maxillary circulation; ipsilateral facial artery also embolized. Controls severe epistaxis in 82–97% of cases. Complications: skin necrosis, paraesthesia, CVA, groin haematoma. Reserved for failed ligations.

## PART D: Secondary Epistaxis

### Warfarin-related Epistaxis
Warfarin patients constitute 9–17% of epistaxis admissions. Direct therapies seldom work — packing usually required. Bleeding often from multiple sites. After resuscitation, gentle anterior packs inserted; haematology team consulted. Fibrin glue useful as haemostatic dressing for large areas of oozing.

### New Oral Anticoagulants (TSOACs)
Rivaroxaban, dabigatran — reversal is often difficult and incomplete. Liaison with haematology essential before any steps to stop or reverse agents. Liaison with cardiologists required to avoid catastrophic complications (e.g. stent occlusion).

### Hereditary Haemorrhagic Telangiectasia (HHT/Osler-Weber-Rendu)
Autosomal dominant condition affecting blood vessels in skin, mucous membranes and viscera. Penetrance reaches 97% by age 50. Genetic abnormality on chromosome 9q (HHT1) and 12q (HHT2). Recurrent epistaxis in 93% of cases. Management includes: packing, cautery, antifibrinolytics, oestrogens, laser photocoagulation (NdYAG, Argon, KTP532), septal dermoplasty, ligation, embolization, and as last resort — Young's operation (permanent closure of nostrils).

### Traumatic Epistaxis
Post-traumatic haemorrhage does not conform to normal patterns. Ethmoidal artery damage from fronto-ethmoidal fractures may require open approach ligation. Catastrophic bleeding can occur from delayed rupture of internal carotid artery pseudo-aneurysm — persistent/unexpectedly severe bleeding post-head injury warrants CT scanning and angiography.

### Post-surgical Epistaxis
Severe haemorrhage occurs in 3–9% of inferior turbinectomies. Bipolar to the main branch of the inferior turbinate artery at the posterosuperior aspect usually secures haemostasis. ICA damage during posterior ethmoid/sphenoid surgery is usually fatal.

## PART E: Epistaxis in CKD Patients

Chronic kidney disease patients are at increased risk of epistaxis due to:
- Uraemic platelet dysfunction (impaired platelet aggregation)
- Acquired von Willebrand disease
- Concurrent anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy
- Hypertension (commonly coexistent)

**Bedside consultation approach:**
1. **Resuscitation:** Secure IV access, check haemoglobin, renal function, coagulation profile (PT, aPTT, INR), platelet count and bleeding time
2. **History:** Duration, frequency, severity, medication review (anticoagulants, antiplatelets, dialysis status), previous epistaxis episodes
3. **Examination:** Anterior rhinoscopy with headlight; if bleeding point identified — silver nitrate cautery or bipolar; if not — nasal endoscopy
4. **Specific considerations:** Liaise with nephrology; correct uraemic bleeding with desmopressin (DDAVP), cryoprecipitate or conjugated oestrogens; avoid nasal packing if possible (mucosal damage); consider tranexamic acid as adjunct
5. **Definitive management:** If recurrent/refractory — ESPAL preferred over packing strategies

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The sphenopalatine artery is the most important supply to the nasal cavity — the key target for surgical ligation (ESPAL).
> 2. Little's area (Kiesselbach's plexus) on the anterior septum is the most frequent site of epistaxis in both anterior and posterior bleeds (70% of posterior bleeds are septal).
> 3. Woodruff's plexus is a venous plexus and is very unlikely to be important in epistaxis.
> 4. Primary epistaxis (70–80% of cases) is idiopathic; proven associations include NSAIDs, alcohol and weather but NOT hypertension or septal deviation.
> 5. Direct bleeding point-specific approaches are superior to indirect nasal packing strategies.
> 6. Endoscopic identification of posterior bleeding points succeeds in over 80% of cases with 90%+ control rates.
> 7. ESPAL is the ligation of choice — the crista ethmoidalis (present in 96%) is the key surgical landmark for locating the sphenopalatine foramen.
> 8. Ligation hierarchy: SPA → IMA → ECA → Ethmoidal arteries — ligate as close as possible to the bleeding source.
> 9. Embolization (82–97% success) is reserved for failed ligations; complications include skin necrosis, CVA and groin haematoma.
> 10. HHT (Osler-Weber-Rendu) causes recurrent epistaxis in 93% of cases — management ranges from cautery/laser to Young's operation for severe cases.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **The Hippocratic technique** (pinching the ala nasi) remains the first-line treatment — yet only 43% of A&E staff could correctly demonstrate it in one study.
> - **Severity is inversely proportional to frequency** — recurrent bleeds are usually minor; acute severe epistaxis is often a one-time event with high morbidity.
> - **Packing for anterior epistaxis is unwarranted** — over 90% of anterior bleeds can be controlled with simple cautery.
> - **Compensatory anastomotic flow** via facial arteries explains re-bleeding events after ligation or embolization.
> - **Anterior ethmoidal artery retraction** into the orbit during sinus surgery can cause tension haematoma — a surgical emergency requiring immediate decompression.
> - **All epistaxis patients should be haemostased within 24 hours of admission** — patients should not be left on a ward with blood oozing from packs while on-call teams change shift.
