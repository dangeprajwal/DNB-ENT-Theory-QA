# Olfactory Pathway, Theories of Olfaction, Rhinomanometry and UPSIT

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - A) Describe the olfactory pathway and theories of olfaction. B) Discuss rhinomanometry. Add a note on UPSIT.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 110 (Abnormalities of Smell) & Chapter 90 (Measurement of the Nasal Airway)

## INTRODUCTION

The olfactory system is unique among the senses in that information from sensory receptors is sent directly into cortical regions without synapsing in the thalamus. Despite its primordial importance, the olfactory pathway is poorly understood and has been historically underappreciated. Among patients with olfactory dysfunction, over 68% experience altered quality of life, 46% describe changes in appetite and 56% report influences on daily living.

---

## PART A: OLFACTORY PATHWAY

### Olfactory Neuroepithelium

The olfactory neuroepithelium occupies approximately 2 cm^2 in the upper nasal recesses lining the cribriform plate, sectors of the superior turbinate, middle turbinate and septum. The olfactory cleft is approximately 1 mm wide, situated 7 cm deep to the nostril. Only 10-15% of inspired air reaches the olfactory neuroepithelium.

**Cell types within the neuroepithelium:**
- Bipolar sensory receptor neuron (bearing receptor-containing cilia)
- Supporting (sustentacular) cell
- Duct cell of Bowman's glands (secretes mucus)
- Microvillar cell
- Horizontal (dark) basal cells (stem cells)
- Globose (light) basal cells (multipotent)

### Signal Transduction

Before neural transduction begins, odorants must: (a) enter the nose during active sniffing or passive diffusion, (b) pass through the olfactory cleft, and (c) move from air phase into the aqueous mucus phase. Odorant binding proteins in mucus transport molecules to receptors.

Signal transduction involves a complex cascade:
1. Odorant binds to 7-transmembrane domain receptor on cilia
2. Activation of olfactory-specific G-protein (Golf)
3. Stimulation of adenylyl cyclase III
4. Increase in intracellular cAMP
5. Opening of cyclic nucleotide-gated ion channels
6. Ca^2+ and Na^+ influx leading to depolarization
7. Action potential generated in the receptor neuron

### Olfactory Nerve (CN I) and Olfactory Bulb

Approximately **6 million** receptor cell axons coalesce into 30-50 fascicles (olfactory fila) which traverse the cribriform plate and pia mater to synapse with second-order neurons within the glomeruli of the olfactory bulb. The olfactory bulbs are complex structures located on the ventral surface of the frontal lobes directly over the cribriform plate.

**Key points about the olfactory bulb:**
- First synapse occurs within spherical glomeruli
- A given receptor projects to only one glomerulus
- Second-order neurons: mitral cells and tufted cells
- Neurotransmitter: glutamate (excitatory); dopamine (modulatory)
- Contains interneurons: periglomerular cells and granule cells (GABAergic/dopaminergic)

### Central Projections

The olfactory system is **unique** among sensory systems in that information is sent directly and primarily ipsilaterally into cortical regions without synapsing in the thalamus. However, some cortical projections from primary to secondary (orbitofrontal) cortex do relay through the thalamus, and there are contralateral projections via the anterior commissure from the AON.

Higher order brain regions targeted by mitral and tufted cells (from rostral to caudal):
1. Piriform cortex
2. Olfactory tubercle
3. Entorhinal area
4. Amygdaloid cortex
5. Corticomedial nuclear group of the amygdala

**Third-order projections** (reciprocal): mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus, posterior and medial hypothalamus, hippocampus, and orbitofrontal cortex.

**Centrifugal (feedback) fibres** project from the olfactory cortex back to the olfactory bulb to modify and control olfactory input. Structures involved include the AON, piriform cortex, lateral entorhinal cortex, amygdala, raphe nuclei, locus ceruleus, and hypothalamus.

### Clinical Correlations

- Lesions anterior to the olfactory trigone (including neuroepithelium, fila, bulb and tract) cause **total loss of smell** on the affected side
- Lesions posterior to the olfactory trigone do **not** typically cause complete loss
- Areas that result in smell perception when stimulated: pre-piriform and intermediate piriform cortices

## PART B: THEORIES OF OLFACTION

### Odorant Receptor Theory (Molecular Biology)

The most widely accepted theory based on the discovery of the olfactory receptor gene family by Buck and Axel (1991 Nobel Prize):

- ~1000 olfactory receptor genes (pseudogenes included), representing ~1% of all expressed genes
- Each receptor neuron expresses only **one type** of odorant receptor gene (one neuron-one receptor rule)
- Each receptor can bind multiple odorants; each odorant activates multiple receptors
- The **combinatorial code** of activated receptors creates a unique pattern for each smell
- All neurons expressing the same receptor converge on the same glomerulus in the olfactory bulb
- This creates a spatial "odour map" in the olfactory bulb

### Stereochemical Theory (Amoore, 1963)

Proposed that odour quality depends on molecular shape and size:
- Seven primary odours: camphoraceous, musky, floral, pepperminty, ethereal, pungent, putrid
- Molecules of similar shape produce similar odours
- Specific receptor sites on cilia have complementary shapes (lock-and-key mechanism)

### Vibrational Theory

Proposes that olfactory receptors detect molecular vibrations rather than shape:
- Different functional groups have characteristic vibrational frequencies
- Electron tunnelling mechanism proposed

### Additional Neural Systems in the Human Nose

1. **Main olfactory system (CN I):** Mediates common odour sensations
2. **Trigeminal somatosensory system (CN V):** Mediates irritation, burning, cooling, tickling; induces protective reflexes (sneezing, lacrimation)
3. **Nervus terminalis (CN 0):** GnRH-immunoreactive; possible endocrine function
4. **Vomeronasal organ:** Non-functional in humans; no centrally-projecting nerve and no accessory olfactory bulb present

## PART C: RHINOMANOMETRY

### Definition and Principle

Rhinomanometry provides a **functional** measure of nasal patency by measuring nasal resistance to airflow. Nasal resistance is calculated from two measurements: nasal airflow and trans-nasal pressure, both measured by differential pressure transducers (manometers).

### Formula

R = δP / V

Where:
- R = resistance to airflow (Pa/cm^3 per second or cm H2O/L per second)
- δP = trans-nasal pressure (Pa or cm H2O)
- V = nasal airflow (cm^3/s or L/s)

### Types of Rhinomanometry

| Type | Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Active anterior | Pressure tube taped to one nostril; measures one side at a time | Recommended by standardization committees; most commonly used | Cannot measure total resistance directly |
| Active posterior | Pressure tube held in mouth | Measures total nasal resistance directly | ~10% subjects cannot obtain airway around soft palate |
| Passive | External airflow from fan/pump directed through nose | Separates upper and lower airways; useful for research | Not a natural physiological measurement |

### Standardized Measurement

- Unilateral nasal airflow measured at a sample pressure point of **150 Pa**
- Bilateral nasal airflow measured at **75 Pa**
- Asian populations: lower sample pressures of 100 and 50 Pa accepted (smaller body size, smaller lung volumes)
- Total nasal resistance: 1/R(total) = 1/r(left) + 1/r(right)

### Normal Values

- Mean total nasal resistance in adults: **0.23 Pa/cm^3/s** (range 0.15-0.39)
- Upper limit of normal: 0.3 Pa/cm^3/s
- Cole considers >0.25 Pa/cm^3/s as obstructed
- Maximum in infants: ~1.2 Pa/cm^3/s; declines to adult value by 16-18 years
- Females generally have lower resistance than males

### Nasal Cycle and Measurement Variability

- Unilateral resistance varies up to 4-fold due to the nasal cycle (0.36-1.36 Pa/cm^3/s)
- Total nasal resistance is more stable due to reciprocal relationship between the two sides
- Single measurement is unreliable; multiple measurements with mask repositioning are needed
- Decongestion eliminates physiological variation and allows assessment of anatomical factors

### Acoustic Rhinometry

Provides an **anatomical** measurement of nasal patency (cross-sectional area):
- Acoustic pulse generated from speaker transmitted along tube into nose
- Reflected sound detected by microphone and processed into area-distance graph
- Correlates well with CT cross-sectional area measurements
- Accuracy unreliable in posterior nose when congested
- Subject to noise and breathing artefacts

### Peak Nasal Inspiratory Flow (PNIF)

- Simple, effort-dependent measure of nasal conductance using a face mask and peak flow meter
- Normal adult: >100 L/min
- Less sensitive than rhinomanometry or acoustic rhinometry for small changes
- Inspiratory measurements may cause nasal alar collapse and flow limitation
- Useful for monitoring response to treatment and nasal challenge testing

## PART D: NOTE ON UPSIT

### University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT)

- 40-item microencapsulated "scratch and sniff" forced-choice odour identification test
- Self-administered in 10-15 minutes
- Patient scratches odour strip with pencil and selects one of four options (forced-choice)
- Available in >30 languages

### Classification Based on UPSIT Score

| Category | Score Range |
|----------|-----------|
| Normosmia | 34-40 (age/sex dependent) |
| Mild microsmia | 26-33 |
| Moderate microsmia | 19-25 |
| Severe microsmia | 11-18 |
| Anosmia | 6-10 |
| Probable malingering | <6 |

### Key Properties

- Chance performance = 10/40 (forced-choice among 4 options)
- Scores very low (below chance) suggest **malingering**
- Test-retest reliability: r = 0.94
- Useful in medicolegal assessment for compensation claims
- Provides normative data adjusted for age and sex
- Systemic steroids temporarily reverse conductive impairment in 83% (useful diagnostic tool); topical steroids effective in only 25%

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Olfactory system is unique: sensory information reaches cortex directly without thalamic relay (ipsilateral projections)
> 2. ~6 million receptor cell axons coalesce into 30-50 olfactory fila traversing the cribriform plate
> 3. ~1000 olfactory receptor genes (~1% of all expressed genes); combinatorial code creates unique odour identity
> 4. Lesions anterior to the olfactory trigone cause total ipsilateral anosmia; posterior lesions do not cause complete loss
> 5. Rhinomanometry measures nasal resistance (functional); acoustic rhinometry measures cross-sectional area (anatomical)
> 6. Normal total nasal resistance: 0.23 Pa/cm^3/s; upper limit of normal 0.3 Pa/cm^3/s
> 7. Active anterior rhinomanometry at 150 Pa sample pressure is the standardized method
> 8. UPSIT: 40-item scratch-and-sniff test; chance score = 10/40; below-chance scores suggest malingering
> 9. Unilateral resistance varies up to 4-fold with the nasal cycle; total resistance is much more stable
> 10. Only 10-15% of inspired air reaches the olfactory neuroepithelium; minor nasal changes can significantly affect olfaction

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The olfactory vector route (nerve cells as conduits for viruses) was historically recognized as a polio entry point, leading to zinc sulphate cauterization programs
> - Golf (olfactory-specific G protein) distinguishes olfactory signal transduction from other G-protein coupled pathways
> - Decongesting the nose before rhinomanometry eliminates physiological variation and allows pure anatomical assessment
> - Asian populations require lower sample pressures (100/50 Pa) for rhinomanometry due to smaller lung volumes
> - UPSIT below-chance scores are more reliable for detecting malingering than clinical assessment alone
