# Orbital Complications of [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|Chronic Rhinosinusitis]]

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the orbital complications of CRS.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 101 (Complications of Rhinosinusitis)

---

## INTRODUCTION

It is estimated that 3% of sinusitis cases will progress to orbital cellulitis, with 60-85% of orbital cellulitis cases being secondary to sinusitis. In the pre-antibiotic era, death or blindness occurred in up to 20% of patients. Orbital cellulitis is by far the most frequent acute complication of ethmoid sinusitis, owing to the paper-thin lamina papyracea separating the ethmoid sinuses from the orbit. Orbital cellulitis is far more common in children (50% under 6 years) and young adults (76-85% under 20 years). The rhinosinusitis itself may be occult or asymptomatic, particularly in children where the complication is often the first presentation.

---

## PART A: CHANDLER CLASSIFICATION

The Chandler classification grades the severity of orbital complications and guides overall management. It is important to note that the disease does not necessarily progress in chronological order - a stage 2 can progress directly to stage 5 bypassing the intermediate steps.

| Stage | Classification | Description |
|-------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | Preseptal cellulitis (50%) | Inflammation does not extend beyond the orbital septum |
| 2 | Postseptal cellulitis / orbital cellulitis without abscess (35%) | Inflammation extends into the tissues of the orbit beyond the orbital septum |
| 3 | Subperiosteal abscess (15%) | Abscess formation deep to the periosteum of the orbital bones, typically at the lamina papyracea |
| 4 | Orbital abscess (less than 1%) | Abscess formation within the orbit which has breached the periosteum |
| 5 | Cavernous sinus thrombosis | Inflammatory process has extended into the cavernous sinus which thromboses and may progress to abscess |

---

## PART B: PATHOGENESIS AND CLINICAL FEATURES

### Stage 1: Preseptal Cellulitis

Swelling around the eye is the earliest sign. Oedema results from congestion of veins draining the eyelid and can be present when the infection is still confined to the sinus. Cellulitis from untreated sinusitis represents local spread of inflammatory elements to the lid. The inflammation does not extend beyond the orbital septum (where the medial orbital periosteal reflection attaches to the medial eyelid at the tarsal plate).

### Stage 2: Postseptal Cellulitis (Orbital Cellulitis)

Confined to the orbit but has extended through the orbital septum, potentially with intraconal involvement. Typically results from transmitted pressure from the sinus to orbital veins with leakage of inflammatory elements into the orbit.

**Clinical features:** Eyelid swelling, chemosis, proptosis, potentially with impaired extraocular muscle function and diplopia. It is difficult to clinically differentiate postseptal cellulitis from subperiosteal abscess; CT scanning is mandatory.

### Stage 3: Subperiosteal Abscess

In ethmoid sinusitis, orbital abscesses are typically medial to the globe. Superiorly located orbital subperiosteal abscesses with severe frontoethmoiditis may indicate increased potential for intracranial suppuration and should be aggressively evaluated. Subperiosteal abscess secondary to [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]]itis typically forms superiorly with anteroinferior globe displacement.

### Stage 4: Orbital Abscess

Abscess formation within the orbit that has breached the periosteum. Multiple rim-enhancing orbital abscesses may be seen on CT with surrounding cellulitis and resultant proptosis.

### Stage 5: Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis

Infection may spread through veins from the paranasal sinuses and orbit to the cavernous sinuses as thrombophlebitis or by septic emboli. Bacteria themselves are pro-thrombotic; the thrombus provides conditions for bacterial growth and shields bacteria from antibiotics. The ethmoid and sphenoid sinuses are the most common source.

**Clinical features:** Chemosis, periorbital oedema, proptosis, progressive ophthalmoplegia, visual impairment, headache, and trigeminal paraesthesia. Initially signs may be unilateral; bilateral disease propagates via the intercavernous sinuses. Published mortality is high at between 14% and 79%, and up to half of patients experience residual cranial nerve palsies and blindness. Visual loss may be irreversible if retinal ischaemic time exceeds 100 minutes.

## PART C: INVESTIGATIONS

### Clinical Examination

- Nasal endoscopy to determine site and extent of disease
- Formal ophthalmological assessment: degree of chemosis, range of eye movements, degree of proptosis, relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD), visual acuity (Snellen chart), colour vision (Ishihara plates), optic disc inspection
- Monitoring: at least twice daily; if increasing concern, 4-6 hourly monitoring of eye movements, visual acuity and colour vision with regular temperature and pulse

### Radiological Investigations

**Contrast-enhanced CT** is the first-line imaging:

- High-resolution triplanar (axial, coronal and sagittal) scanning
- Superior demonstration of bony anatomy and orbital/sinus pathology
- CT may miss up to 50% of early intracranial complications

**MRI:** Outstanding soft tissue detail without radiation exposure; low threshold advised if clinical suspicion of intracranial involvement. CT and MR venography are complementary for diagnosing cavernous sinus thrombosis.

**Radiological findings by stage:**

- **Stage 1:** Eyelid swelling and thickening of preseptal tissues; CT cannot distinguish between oedema, preseptal cellulitis and allergy
- **Stage 2:** Induration of extraconal, intraconal and retrobulbar fat without abscess
- **Stage 3:** Typical lenticular rim-enhancing collection adjacent to the lamina papyracea with a fat plane between it and the displaced medial rectus muscle; lateral deviation and proptosis of the globe
- **Stage 4:** Multiple rim-enhancing orbital abscesses with surrounding cellulitis
- **Stage 5:** Early CT scans may appear normal; thickening and increased signal intensity in extraocular muscles on T2-weighted MRI

### Other Investigations

- Blood cultures
- Sinonasal/abscess pus culture (ideally from initial nasal endoscopy)
- Full blood count and inflammatory markers
- Urinalysis for diabetes mellitus (immunocompromised state)
- Regular temperature monitoring

### Risk Factors for Simultaneous Orbital and Intracranial Complications

- Male sex
- Age 7 years and older
- Failure to improve after appropriate therapy
- Changes in neurological status
- Frontal sinus opacification on CT
- Superior or lateral position of orbital abscess
- Need for surgical intervention to drain orbital abscess

## PART D: MANAGEMENT

### Medical Management

Almost all patients require admission for observation and treatment. Unless abscess is demonstrated radiologically, non-surgical management is first choice. Exception: when vision is affected by pressure on the optic nerve from surrounding inflammation without abscess formation.

**Antibiotics:** Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics to cover likely organisms including S. pneumoniae, S. anginosus and other Streptococcus species, H. influenzae, S. aureus, Moraxella catarrhalis, and anaerobic bacteria (Prevotella, Porphyromonas, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus). An initial regime of intravenous cephalosporin with metronidazole is an appropriate first choice until culture-specific sensitivities are known. Polymicrobial and anaerobic isolates are more common in patients older than 15 years and in those with intracranial complications.

**Decongestants:** Xylometazoline may aid resolution by reducing mucosal oedema (evidence inconclusive).

**Systemic corticosteroids:** Evidence base is lacking; not recommended unless evidence of pituitary insufficiency in cavernous sinus thrombosis.

**Monitoring protocol:** If no significant clinical improvement in the first 24 hours of medical treatment, surgical intervention should be considered. If at any point there is clinical deterioration, urgent repeat imaging with a view to emergency surgical intervention is required.

**Duration:** IV antibiotics continued until clinical improvement is well established, then switch to oral. 14 days of culture-directed oral antibiotics is often appropriate.

### Surgical Management

**Indications for surgery:**

- Demonstrable abscess on CT (subperiosteal or orbital)
- No improvement within 24 hours of medical management
- Any clinical deterioration during treatment
- Vision-threatening signs (reduced visual acuity, colour vision impairment, RAPD)
- Patients with proptosis but normal eye movements and visual acuity are likely to require surgery

**Endoscopic approach (preferred for medial abscesses):**

- Endoscopic ethmoidectomy with removal of lamina papyracea and pernasal drainage of the orbital abscess
- Advantages: no facial scarring, endonasal ventilation of sinuses via osteomeatal complex, less post-operative orbital oedema, shorter hospital stay, no wound complications
- Surgeon must be prepared to convert to external drainage if required

**External approach (Lynch-Howarth):**

- Advised when the surgeon is not very familiar with endoscopic sinus surgery
- Advised for abscesses not situated medially within the orbit adjacent to the lamina papyracea
- Conventional Lynch-Howarth approach for external ethmoidectomy with assistant maintaining clear operative field
- Essential for superiorly or laterally placed abscesses (especially from frontal sinusitis)

**Drainage:** Maintained with surgical drains either into the nasal cavity (fixed to septum) or externally through the medial canthal incision.

### Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis Management

- Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics for at least 2 weeks beyond clinical resolution (bacteria sequestered within thrombus may not be killed until dural sinuses recanalize)
- Relapse and recurrence of meningeal and ocular signs reported up to 6 weeks after recovery
- Follow-up for at least 6 months advised
- Surgery indicated only to drain non-draining sinus infection or abscess collection
- Anticoagulation: no consensus; proponents suggest prevention of thrombus propagation with anti-inflammatory properties; opponents suggest thrombus walls off infection; if used, start with unfractionated heparin infusion (APTT 1.5-2), switch to LMWH once improved

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. 3% of sinusitis cases progress to orbital cellulitis; 60-85% of orbital cellulitis is secondary to sinusitis
> 2. Chandler classification: 5 stages from preseptal cellulitis to cavernous sinus thrombosis
> 3. Disease does NOT necessarily progress chronologically through stages - stage 2 can jump to stage 5
> 4. Orbital cellulitis is more common in children (50% under 6 years) and young adults (76-85% under 20 years)
> 5. Ethmoid sinusitis causes medial orbital abscesses; frontal sinusitis causes superior abscesses
> 6. Visual loss is irreversible if retinal ischaemic time exceeds 100 minutes - urgent intervention needed
> 7. CT is first-line imaging but may miss up to 50% of early intracranial complications; low threshold for MRI
> 8. Stage 3 CT: lenticular rim-enhancing collection with fat plane between abscess and displaced medial rectus
> 9. Initial IV cephalosporin + metronidazole; 14 days of oral antibiotics; 24-hour medical trial before surgery
> 10. Cavernous sinus thrombosis: mortality 14-79%; antibiotics for at least 2 weeks beyond clinical resolution
> 11. Sphenoid and ethmoid sinuses are the most common source of cavernous sinus thrombosis
> 12. Multidisciplinary involvement is important but leadership should be by an experienced ENT surgeon


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - In children, the complication is often the first presentation without prior history of rhinosinusitis
> - CT cannot distinguish between oedema, preseptal cellulitis and allergy at Stage 1
> - Always consider occult intracranial complications even with a normal neurological examination (5.4% simultaneous orbital and intracranial complications; rises to 13-24% if surgical drainage needed)
> - Bacteria within the cavernous sinus thrombus are shielded from antibiotics - hence the prolonged course needed
> - Bilateral cavernous sinus signs propagate via the intercavernous sinuses
> - Streptococcus anginosus species are the most frequently cultured organisms and have a tendency towards abscess formation
