# Cough Reflex, Chronic Aspiration and Burning Mouth Syndrome

**Question 1:** *Describe the mechanism of cough reflex and its pharmacological management. Discuss the management of chronic aspiration.* (Q15, Dec 2019)

**Question 2:** *Define burning mouth syndrome. Discuss its etiology and management.* (2015-16)

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3 Chapter 56 (Chronic Aspiration); Volume 3 Chapter 38 (Orofacial Pain — Burning Mouth Syndrome); Volume 3 Chapter 58 (Larynx Anatomy)

---

## Introduction

The cough reflex is a vital protective mechanism of the tracheobronchial tree that works in conjunction with the swallowing mechanism and laryngeal sphincteric function to prevent aspiration. When these protective mechanisms fail, chronic aspiration may ensue, leading to significant pulmonary morbidity and mortality. The management of chronic aspiration requires a stepwise approach from conservative measures to surgical intervention. Burning mouth syndrome is a separate but important condition representing oral dysaesthesia — a misperception of the normal oral environment — that the otorhinolaryngologist must recognise and manage appropriately.

---

## Part A: Mechanism of the Cough Reflex

### Physiology of the Cough Reflex

The cough reflex is a complex protective mechanism involving afferent sensory receptors, central processing and coordinated efferent motor responses. It serves to clear the tracheobronchial tree of foreign material, excess secretions and inhaled irritants.

### Components of the Cough Reflex Arc

**1. Afferent limb (sensory):**
- Cough receptors are located primarily in the larynx, trachea, carina and major bronchi
- Additional receptors exist in the pharynx, nose, sinuses, external ear canal (Arnold's nerve — auricular branch of vagus), pleura and pericardium
- Vagal afferents (superior laryngeal nerve and recurrent laryngeal nerve) are the principal sensory pathways involved in the cough reflex
- Rapidly adapting receptors (RARs) respond to mechanical stimulation and chemical irritants
- C-fibre receptors respond to chemical mediators (bradykinin, capsaicin, prostaglandins)

**2. Cough centre (central processing):**
- Located in the medulla oblongata (nucleus tractus solitarius)
- Receives afferent input and coordinates the motor response
- Can be modulated by higher cortical centres (voluntary cough suppression or initiation)

**3. Efferent limb (motor):**
- Vagus nerve — controls glottic closure (recurrent laryngeal nerve to intrinsic laryngeal muscles)
- Phrenic nerve — controls diaphragmatic contraction
- Spinal nerves — control intercostal and abdominal wall muscles

### Phases of the Cough Reflex

The cough reflex consists of three distinct phases:

**Phase 1 — Inspiratory phase:**
- Deep inspiration with open glottis
- Lung volume increases to maximise expiratory force

**Phase 2 — Compressive phase:**
- Glottic closure by adduction of the true and false vocal folds
- Simultaneous contraction of expiratory muscles (abdominal and intercostal muscles) against the closed glottis
- Intrathoracic pressure rises rapidly to 100-300 mmHg

**Phase 3 — Expulsive phase:**
- Sudden opening of the glottis
- Rapid expulsion of air at velocities up to 500 mph
- The strong expiratory airflow is assisted by abdominal muscles and compression of the lungs
- Dynamic compression of airways creates high linear air velocities that dislodge mucus and foreign material

### Pharmacological Management of Cough

**Antitussive agents (cough suppressants):**
- Codeine phosphate — opioid acting on the central cough centre in the medulla
- Dextromethorphan — non-opioid centrally acting antitussive
- Pholcodine — centrally acting, less addictive than codeine

**Mucoactive agents:**
- Guaifenesin — expectorant that thins secretions
- N-acetylcysteine — mucolytic that breaks disulphide bonds in mucus
- Carbocisteine — reduces mucus viscosity

**Treating underlying cause:**
- Proton pump inhibitors — for cough secondary to gastro-oesophageal reflux
- Inhaled corticosteroids — for cough-variant asthma
- Nasal corticosteroids — for post-nasal drip syndrome
- Antihistamines — for allergic causes
- ACE inhibitor withdrawal — if drug-induced cough

## Part B: Management of Chronic Aspiration

### Definition and Pathophysiology

Aspiration is contamination of the tracheobronchial tree with secretions, food material and other contaminants. Prevention of aspiration occurs primarily through reflex laryngeal closure, laryngeal elevation and cessation of breathing during swallowing, and any aspirated contents are expelled through coughing.

A certain amount of aspiration is normal in humans, especially during sleep, but is tolerated without complications in healthy subjects with normal tracheobronchial ciliary function and normal immunology. In cases where the cough reflex is reduced or absent, 'silent' aspiration may occur.

The response to aspiration depends on the pH of the aspirate, microorganisms present and the person's pulmonary and immunological status. Chronic small volume aspiration may risk pneumonia or respiratory failure through chronic pulmonary emphysematous disease or bronchiectasis.

### Causes of Chronic Aspiration

- Neurological disease — stroke (especially brainstem), progressive neurological conditions, traumatic brain injury
- Vagal nerve damage — high vagal injury affecting motor and sensory function of the larynx and pharynx
- Post-surgical — following head and neck surgery, thyroid surgery
- Presbyphagia — age-related decline in swallowing reserve
- Cervical spine pathology — large osteophytes overhanging the laryngeal inlet
- [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]]-related dysphagia

### Evaluation of the Aspirating Patient

**Flexible nasendoscopy:**
- Demonstrates pooling of secretions in the hypopharynx
- Assesses oedema of the laryngeal mucosa and diminished supraglottic sensation
- Evaluates vocal cord mobility and laryngeal competence

**Videofluoroscopic (VF) swallowing assessment:**
- Always performed in conjunction with a swallowing therapist
- Provides information about the nature of the swallowing disturbance and degree of aspiration
- Uses limited amounts of barium; water-based contrast agent initially if aspiration is suspected

**Functional endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES):**
- Uses dyed liquids, semi-solids and solids with nasopharyngeal fibre-optic observation and video recording
- Advantages: avoids radiation, can be performed at the bedside, provides biofeedback to the patient
- Swallowing strategies can be trialled and recorded during assessment

### Principles of Management

Management follows a stepwise approach from conservative to surgical:

#### Step 1: Conservative Measures
- Swallowing therapy — postural adjustments, swallowing manoeuvres (chin tuck, supraglottic swallow)
- Dietary modification — texture modification of food and thickened liquids
- Nasogastric tube feeding — for temporary nutritional support
- PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) — for longer-term enteral feeding

#### Step 2: [[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]]
- A cuffed tracheostomy tube provides some protection against aspiration
- However, it is not the ideal solution to chronic aspiration
- Requires close attention and frequent suctioning of the oropharynx
- Cuff pressure against the posterior tracheal wall helps reduce secretion aspiration

#### Step 3: Surgery to the Cricopharyngeal Sphincter
- Indicated when pooling is due to hypertonic or discoordinated cricopharyngeal sphincter
- **Botulinum toxin injection** — 'medical myotomy'; 80-120 units of botulinum toxin A administered into cricopharyngeus in three separate injections under direct visualisation through a laryngoscope
- If successful, can be repeated or followed by endoscopic CO2 laser myotomy
- External approach myotomy is very rarely required

#### Step 4: Vocal Fold Medialization
- For laryngeal incompetence due to unilateral or bilateral [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal fold paralysis]]
- [[Injection Laryngoplasty|Injection laryngoplasty]] — temporary (suitable when recovery is expected)
- Medialization thyroplasty — more permanent (implants: Gore-Tex, titanium, hydroxylapatite, silicone)
- Arytenoid adduction — for posterior glottic gap
- May need to be combined with cricopharyngeal myotomy

#### Step 5: Laryngeal Suspension
- Enhanced laryngeal suspension — thyroid cartilage partially excised, thyrohyoid approximation achieved
- Performed with cricopharyngeal myotomy and can be combined with medialization thyroplasty
- Elevates the larynx away from hypopharyngeal secretions

#### Step 6: Glottic and Supraglottic Closure
- Montgomery procedure — stripping vocal cord mucosa bilaterally and suturing together through median laryngofissure
- Two-layered supraglottic closure via pharyngotomy approach
- Problems: high risk of breakdown, tracheostomy dependence, no voice, not easily reversible

#### Step 7: Laryngotracheal Separation (Lindeman Procedure)
- The airway is closed below the subglottis
- The distal trachea is brought out to the skin surface as a stoma
- Tracheo-oesophageal diversion — modification where proximal tracheal segment is anastomosed end-to-side to the oesophagus

#### Step 8: Narrow-Field Laryngectomy
- Reserved for severe cases with limited likelihood of recovery yet reasonable life expectancy
- Removes the laryngeal skeleton but spares the hyoid, strap muscles and hypopharyngeal mucosa
- Multiple layer closure reduces fistula incidence
- Definitive but irreversible

| Procedure | Indication | Reversibility |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| Botulinum toxin/myotomy | Cricopharyngeal dysfunction | Yes |
| Vocal fold medialization | Glottic incompetence | Partially |
| Laryngeal suspension | Reduced laryngeal elevation | Yes |
| Glottic/supraglottic closure | Severe aspiration | Difficult |
| Laryngotracheal separation | Life-threatening aspiration | Possible |
| Narrow-field laryngectomy | Intractable aspiration | No |

## Part C: Burning Mouth Syndrome

### Definition

Burning mouth syndrome (BMS), also known as glossodynia or oral dysaesthesia, is a condition characterised by a burning sensation in the oral mucosa, typically in the absence of any visible mucosal abnormality. These conditions represent a misperception of the normal oral environment rather than a disease of the oral mucosa or supporting tissues.

### Clinical Features

- The most common presenting variation is a feeling of oral dryness or stickiness in the presence of plentiful oral saliva
- Patients often have more than one dysaesthesia symptom, such as a burning feeling and persistent dryness
- The condition is often worse as the day progresses
- Relieved by eating or oral activity
- Enhanced by stressful life events
- Typically affects perimenopausal or postmenopausal women
- Common sites: tip of the tongue, anterior hard palate, lower lip mucosa

### Aetiology

**Primary (idiopathic) BMS:**
- It is unclear whether there is a true local neuropathic problem or whether it is an issue with the understanding and interpretation of neural signals in the brain
- There is experimental and treatment evidence supporting both peripheral and central neuropathic mechanisms
- Associated with an underlying anxiety disorder in many cases
- Similar demographic to other conditions including irritable bowel syndrome and fibromyalgia

**Secondary BMS (must be excluded):**
- Mucosal atrophy from haematinic deficiency (iron, vitamin B12, folate) — haematinics should be checked if burning sensation is reported
- Oral candidiasis
- Xerostomia (drug-induced or [[Sjogren Syndrome|Sjogren's syndrome]])
- Allergic contact stomatitis (dental materials)
- Diabetes mellitus
- Hormonal changes (menopause)
- Neurodegenerative disease — MRI is a sensible precaution if sensory changes are reported, even if there are no cranial nerve deficiencies on testing

### Investigation

- Full blood count and haematinics (iron, ferritin, vitamin B12, folate) to exclude deficiency
- Blood glucose to exclude diabetes
- Oral swab for candida if clinically suspected
- MRI brain — if sensory changes are reported, to exclude neurodegenerative disease
- Salivary flow rate — to objectively assess xerostomia
- Allergy testing — if contact stomatitis suspected

### Management

**Step 1: Explanation and reassurance:**
- Treatment starts by explaining to the patient that there is no disease or oral condition even though it seems to be the case
- Explain that the problem is with the 'feeling nerves' in the mouth
- This reassurance is therapeutic in itself

**Step 2: Treat secondary causes:**
- Iron/B12/folate replacement if deficient
- Antifungal treatment if candidiasis present
- Manage xerostomia (saliva substitutes, pilocarpine)
- Adjust medications causing dry mouth

**Step 3: Pharmacological treatment:**
- **Tricyclic antidepressants** are the most effective treatment
- Nortriptyline has a better side-effect profile than amitriptyline
- Starting dose: 10 mg at night, titrated up gradually
- **Gabapentin** — used as an alternative
- **Clonazepam** — used as an alternative (low-dose, topical or systemic)

**Step 4: Non-pharmacological approaches:**
- Simple oral distractors such as chewing gum are useful for mild cases
- Psychological intervention — cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
- Hypnosis — can be a useful alternative
- These are particularly helpful as the condition is commonly associated with an underlying anxiety disorder

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The cough reflex arc consists of afferent vagal receptors, medullary cough centre and efferent motor nerves to the glottis, diaphragm and abdominal muscles
> 2. Three phases of cough: inspiratory, compressive (closed glottis with rising intrathoracic pressure) and expulsive
> 3. Silent aspiration occurs when the cough reflex is reduced or absent — a dangerous clinical scenario
> 4. Chronic aspiration management follows a stepwise approach from conservative measures to surgical intervention
> 5. Botulinum toxin injection into cricopharyngeus (80-120 units) serves as a medical myotomy and diagnostic trial
> 6. FEES has advantages over videofluoroscopy: no radiation, bedside performance and real-time biofeedback
> 7. Narrow-field laryngectomy is the definitive but irreversible option for intractable chronic aspiration
> 8. Burning mouth syndrome represents a misperception of the normal oral environment, not mucosal disease
> 9. BMS is worse as the day progresses and relieved by eating — distinguishing it from organic mucosal disease
> 10. Tricyclic antidepressants (nortriptyline preferred) are the most effective treatment for BMS


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Silent aspiration** is more dangerous than overt aspiration — absent cough reflex means no warning sign
> - **Arnold's nerve cough** — stimulation of the external ear canal (auricular branch of vagus) can trigger cough reflex
> - **Botulinum toxin trial before myotomy** — a positive response to botulinum injection predicts success with definitive cricopharyngeal myotomy
> - **BMS paradox** — burning sensation is relieved by eating (unlike organic mucosal pathology which is aggravated by food)
> - **Always check haematinics** in BMS — iron, B12 and folate deficiency can cause mucosal atrophy mimicking BMS
> - **MRI in BMS** — sensible precaution if sensory changes reported, even without cranial nerve signs, to exclude neurodegenerative disease
