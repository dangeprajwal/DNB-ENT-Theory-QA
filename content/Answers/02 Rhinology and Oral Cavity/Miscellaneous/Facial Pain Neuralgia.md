# Facial Pain, Trigeminal Neuralgia and Rhinogenic Headache

**Question 1:** *Discuss the etiology of facial pain. Describe the clinical features and management of trigeminal neuralgia.* (Q56, Dec 2021)

**Question 2:** *Enumerate headache types and their etiologies. Describe the clinical features and management of Sluder's neuralgia.* (Q66, Jun 2022)

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1 Chapter 112 (Facial Pain); Volume 3 Chapter 38 (Orofacial Pain)

---

## Introduction

Facial pain is one of the most challenging symptoms encountered in otorhinolaryngology practice. The face and nasal region receive complex sensory innervation primarily through the trigeminal nerve (CN V), with additional contributions from the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), vagus nerve (CN X) and upper cervical nerves. Facial pain may arise from rhinological, dental, neurological, vascular or psychogenic causes, and accurate diagnosis requires systematic evaluation. The otorhinolaryngologist must be familiar with both primary pain conditions such as trigeminal neuralgia and referred pain patterns from sinonasal disease.

Headache of rhinogenic origin is a common referral to the ENT surgeon. While acute and [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|chronic rhinosinusitis]] are well-established causes of facial pain and headache, more controversial entities such as contact point headache and Sluder's sphenopalatine ganglion neuralgia require careful evaluation and a high index of clinical suspicion.

---

## Part A: Etiology and Classification of Facial Pain

Facial pain can be classified based on the underlying aetiology into the following broad categories:

### 1. Rhinogenic Causes
- Acute rhinosinusitis — frontal, maxillary or ethmoidal pain corresponding to the affected sinus
- Chronic rhinosinusitis — dull, persistent aching facial pressure
- Mucosal contact points — middle turbinate contacting the septum or lateral wall
- Nasal septal pathology — deviated nasal septum, septal spurs
- Acute barotrauma — sinus squeeze during flying or diving

### 2. Neurogenic Causes
- Trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux)
- Post-herpetic neuralgia
- Glossopharyngeal neuralgia
- Sluder's sphenopalatine ganglion neuralgia

### 3. Vascular Causes
- Migraine
- Cluster headache (migrainous neuralgia)
- Temporal arteritis

### 4. Dental and Oral Causes
- Dental caries, periapical abscess
- Temporomandibular joint dysfunction
- Impacted wisdom teeth

### 5. Otogenic Causes
- Referred otalgia from pharyngeal or laryngeal pathology
- Acute otitis media

### 6. Ophthalmological Causes
- Acute glaucoma
- Orbital cellulitis
- Optic neuritis

### 7. Psychogenic Causes
- Atypical facial pain (persistent idiopathic facial pain)
- Depression-associated facial pain

| Category | Key Examples | Pain Character |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| Rhinogenic | Acute sinusitis, contact points | Dull ache, pressure |
| Neurogenic | TN, post-herpetic, Sluder's | Sharp, lancinating |
| Vascular | Migraine, cluster headache | Throbbing, periodic |
| Dental | TMJ dysfunction, abscess | Localised, throbbing |
| Psychogenic | Atypical facial pain | Diffuse, constant |

## Part B: Trigeminal Neuralgia

### Definition and Epidemiology

Trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux) is a condition characterised by paroxysmal attacks of severe, lancinating, electric shock-like pain in the distribution of one or more divisions of the trigeminal nerve. It is more common in women, with onset typically after the age of 50 years. The annual incidence is approximately 4-5 per 100,000 population.

### Aetiology

The most common cause is neurovascular compression of the trigeminal nerve root entry zone at the pons by an aberrant arterial loop, most commonly the superior cerebellar artery. Other causes include:

- Vascular compression — superior cerebellar artery (most common), anterior inferior cerebellar artery, or venous compression
- Multiple sclerosis — demyelination of the trigeminal root entry zone (must be excluded, especially in younger patients)
- Posterior fossa tumours — cerebellopontine angle tumours, meningiomas
- Idiopathic — no identifiable cause in some cases

### Clinical Features

The pain of trigeminal neuralgia has several characteristic features:

- **Quality:** Severe, paroxysmal, lancinating, electric shock-like pain
- **Distribution:** Unilateral, typically affecting V2 (maxillary) and/or V3 (mandibular) divisions; V1 (ophthalmic) involvement is less common
- **Duration:** Each paroxysm lasts seconds to minutes, with pain-free intervals between attacks
- **Trigger zones:** Light touch to specific areas of the face, lips, gums or chin provokes attacks
- **Triggers:** Eating, talking, shaving, tooth brushing, cold wind on the face
- **Periodicity:** Attacks may cluster over weeks to months, followed by remission periods
- **No neurological deficit:** Sensory examination is normal between attacks in classical TN

**Important:** The longer a patient suffers with trigeminal neuralgia, the more difficult it may be to reverse the neural pathways responsible for pain.

### Investigations

- **MRI brain with thin cuts through posterior fossa** — mandatory to exclude multiple sclerosis, posterior fossa pathology and cerebellopontine angle tumours
- **MR angiography** — to identify neurovascular compression
- **Trigeminal reflex testing** — may show abnormalities in secondary TN

### Management

#### A. Medical Management (First Line)

**Carbamazepine** is the drug of choice for trigeminal neuralgia. It is effective in approximately 70-80% of patients initially.

- **Starting dose:** 100 mg twice daily, titrated up to 400-1200 mg/day
- **Monitoring:** Liver function tests and full blood count (risk of aplastic anaemia, hepatotoxicity)

**Second-line agents:**
- Gabapentin
- Lamotrigine
- Topiramate
- Oxcarbazepine (better tolerated than carbamazepine)

#### B. Surgical Management

Surgical intervention is considered when medical management fails or produces intolerable side effects. There is added risk of worsening the pain syndrome or causing new pain to develop after surgical intervention.

**Percutaneous procedures (destructive):**
- Percutaneous radiofrequency thermocoagulation of the Gasserian ganglion — selective destruction of pain fibres while preserving touch
- Percutaneous balloon microcompression
- Percutaneous glycerol rhizolysis

**Open surgical procedures:**
- **Microvascular decompression (Jannetta procedure)** — the only non-destructive surgical option; involves posterior fossa craniotomy and placement of a Teflon sponge between the offending vessel and trigeminal nerve root. Offers the best long-term cure rate (>80% pain-free at 5 years) but carries risks of craniotomy

**Stereotactic radiosurgery:**
- Gamma knife radiosurgery — focused radiation to the trigeminal root entry zone; non-invasive but slower onset of relief

| Treatment | Mechanism | Success Rate | Key Risk |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Carbamazepine | Na+ channel blocker | 70-80% initial | Aplastic anaemia |
| Radiofrequency | Thermal nerve ablation | 90% initial | Facial numbness |
| MVD (Jannetta) | Vascular decompression | 80%+ at 5 years | Craniotomy risks |
| Gamma knife | Focused radiation | 70-80% | Delayed response |

## Part C: Post-Herpetic Neuralgia

Post-herpetic neuralgia is pain following a herpes zoster infection of the trigeminal ganglion. It is defined as pain persisting beyond 3 months after resolution of the acute zoster rash. The ophthalmic division (V1) is most commonly affected.

Features include continuous burning pain with superimposed lancinating episodes, allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli) and sensory loss in the affected dermatome.

Management includes:
- Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline)
- Gabapentin or pregabalin
- Topical lidocaine patches or capsaicin cream
- Prevention with herpes zoster vaccination in elderly patients

## Part D: Headache Types in ENT Practice

Headaches commonly encountered in otorhinolaryngology include:

### 1. Rhinogenic Headache
- Caused by acute or chronic rhinosinusitis
- Pain corresponds to the affected sinus — frontal ([[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]]), cheek/infraorbital (maxillary), between eyes (ethmoid), vertex/occipital (sphenoid)
- Worse on bending forward, straining
- Associated with nasal congestion, purulent discharge, hyposmia

### 2. Contact Point Headache
The theory implicates mucosal contact points within the nose as a cause of facial pain and headache. However, evidence shows that the majority of people with contact points experience no facial pain. A positive response to topical anaesthesia (lidocaine) applied to the contact point supports the diagnosis. Surgical correction (septoplasty, turbinate reduction) may be considered in selected cases, but outcomes are variable.

### 3. Cluster Headache (Migrainous Neuralgia)
- Severe unilateral periorbital pain lasting 15 minutes to 3 hours
- Occurs in clusters — multiple attacks per day for weeks, then remission
- Associated autonomic features: ipsilateral lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea, ptosis, miosis (partial Horner's syndrome)
- Male predominance (5:1)
- Management: acute — high-flow oxygen, subcutaneous sumatriptan; prophylaxis — verapamil, lithium, corticosteroids

### 4. Migraine
- Unilateral throbbing headache, 4-72 hours
- Associated nausea, vomiting, photophobia, phonophobia
- With or without aura (visual, sensory or motor)
- Management: triptans for acute attacks, beta-blockers or topiramate for prophylaxis

### 5. Tension-type Headache
- Bilateral, band-like pressure
- Mild to moderate intensity
- Not aggravated by physical activity
- Most common primary headache disorder

## Part E: Sluder's Neuralgia (Sphenopalatine Ganglion Neuralgia)

### Definition

Sluder's neuralgia, first described by Greenfield Sluder in 1908, is sphenopalatine ganglion neuralgia — a collective group of neuralgic, motor, sensory and gustatory symptoms attributed to irritation of the sphenopalatine (pterygopalatine) ganglion.

### Anatomy

The sphenopalatine ganglion (pterygopalatine ganglion) lies in the pterygopalatine fossa, suspended from the maxillary nerve (V2) by the pterygopalatine nerves. It receives:
- Sensory fibres from V2 (maxillary division of trigeminal)
- Parasympathetic fibres from the greater petrosal nerve (CN VII)
- Sympathetic fibres from the deep petrosal nerve (via vidian nerve)

### Clinical Features

- Unilateral deep facial pain centred around the nose, periorbital region and upper jaw
- Pain may radiate to the palate, teeth, temple and mastoid area
- Associated autonomic features — lacrimation, nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea on the affected side
- Gustatory disturbance may occur
- Attacks may be spontaneous or triggered by nasal pathology
- Pain is typically paroxysmal but may become constant

### Diagnosis

- Clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic pattern of pain and autonomic features
- Diagnostic block — application of local anaesthetic (lidocaine) to the sphenopalatine ganglion via the nasal cavity. Pain relief supports the diagnosis
- CT/MRI to exclude structural causes
- Nasal endoscopy to identify mucosal contact points or other nasal pathology

### Management

**Conservative:**
- Treatment of underlying nasal pathology (rhinosinusitis, contact points)
- Topical nasal anaesthetic sprays for acute relief
- Medical management similar to other neuralgias — carbamazepine, gabapentin

**Interventional:**
- Sphenopalatine ganglion block — transnasal application of local anaesthetic and steroid
- Radiofrequency ablation of the sphenopalatine ganglion in refractory cases
- Endoscopic vidian neurectomy — division of the vidian nerve to denervate the sphenopalatine ganglion; reserved for severe refractory cases

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Facial pain has rhinogenic, neurogenic, vascular, dental, ophthalmological and psychogenic causes — systematic evaluation is essential
> 2. Trigeminal neuralgia is characterised by paroxysmal, unilateral, lancinating pain in V2/V3 distribution with trigger zones
> 3. MRI is mandatory in all cases of TN to exclude multiple sclerosis and posterior fossa tumours
> 4. Carbamazepine is the first-line drug for trigeminal neuralgia; gabapentin, lamotrigine and topiramate are second-line agents
> 5. Microvascular decompression (Jannetta procedure) offers the best long-term surgical cure for TN with >80% success at 5 years
> 6. The longer TN persists, the more difficult it becomes to reverse established pain pathways
> 7. Sluder's neuralgia is sphenopalatine ganglion neuralgia presenting with unilateral facial pain and autonomic features
> 8. Diagnostic sphenopalatine ganglion block with local anaesthetic confirms Sluder's neuralgia
> 9. Contact point headache is controversial — most people with mucosal contact points have no facial pain
> 10. Cluster headache presents with severe unilateral periorbital pain with ipsilateral autonomic features and responds to oxygen and sumatriptan


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **TN trigger zones** are pathognomonic — light touch triggers severe pain, but firm pressure does not
> - **Young patient with TN** — always investigate for multiple sclerosis (demyelination at root entry zone)
> - **Carbamazepine response** in TN is so characteristic that lack of response should prompt reconsideration of diagnosis
> - **Sluder's vs cluster headache** — both have autonomic features; Sluder's is centred on the nose/maxilla while cluster headache is periorbital
> - **Contact point surgery** should only be considered after a positive lidocaine test at the contact point
> - **Post-herpetic neuralgia** prevention is better than cure — zoster vaccination reduces incidence
