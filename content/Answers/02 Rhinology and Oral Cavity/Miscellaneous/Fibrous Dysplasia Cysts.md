## Introduction

Cystic lesions of the nasolabial region and fibrous dysplasia of the craniofacial skeleton are important topics for the otorhinolaryngologist. Cysts of the jaws and facial bones arise from various epithelial residues — odontogenic (from tooth-forming apparatus) or non-odontogenic (from embryonic epithelial remnants within bony canals). The nasopalatine duct cyst is the most common non-odontogenic cyst. Fibrous dysplasia is a benign bone disorder characterised by replacement of normal bone with fibrous tissue and immature woven bone, presenting unique challenges in the craniofacial region due to proximity to vital structures such as the orbit and optic nerve.

## Part A: Cystic Swellings of the Nasolabial Area

Cysts of the bony facial skeleton can be classified based on the source of their epithelial lining:

### Classification of Cysts of the Bony Facial Skeleton

| Category | Subcategory | Source of Lining |
|----------|------------|-----------------|
| Odontogenic | Developmental & inflammatory | Tooth-forming organ residues |
| Non-odontogenic | Nasopalatine duct cyst | Remnants of Jacobson's organ |
| Non-odontogenic | Intraosseous dermoid | Entrapped embryonic epithelium |
| Non-epithelialized | Solitary/aneurysmal bone cyst | No epithelial lining |

### Cystic Swellings in the Nasolabial Region

The following cystic lesions may present in the nasolabial area:

**1. Nasopalatine duct cyst (incisive canal cyst)** — the most common non-odontogenic cyst, arising from epithelial remnants of the vestigial Jacobson's organ within the nasopalatine canal. Presents in the midline of the anterior maxilla.

**2. Median palatine cyst** — a posteriorly located variant of the nasopalatine duct cyst, arising along the midline palatal raphe.

**3. Median alveolar cyst** — an anteriorly located variant, presenting in the alveolar ridge region.

**4. Palatine papilla cyst** — a superficially located variant within the soft tissue of the incisive papilla.

**5. Dentigerous (follicular) cyst** — an odontogenic cyst formed by expansion of the dental follicle around the crown of an unerupted tooth; may present in the premaxillary region around impacted teeth.

**6. Radicular (periapical) cyst** — inflammatory odontogenic cyst arising from the rests of Malassez in association with a non-vital tooth root.

**7. Nasolabial cyst (Klestadt's cyst)** — a soft tissue cyst arising in the nasolabial fold region, external to bone, from epithelial remnants of the nasolacrimal duct.

**8. Dermoid and epidermoid cysts** — arise from entrapped epithelium at lines of embryonic closure.

**9. Globulomaxillary cyst** — traditionally described as a fissural cyst between the globular and maxillary processes, though its existence as a distinct entity is debated.

## Part B: Nasopalatine Duct Cyst — Investigations and Management

### Clinical Features

The nasopalatine duct cyst and its variants are the most common non-odontogenic cysts, accounting for 5-10% of all jaw cysts. They arise from epithelial remnants of the vestigial Jacobson's organ (part of the olfactory system in some mammals) within the nasopalatine canal, which runs from the nasal septum to the palate in the anterior maxilla immediately behind the maxillary central incisor teeth.

- **Age:** Seen over a broad age range, peaking in middle-aged adult males
- **Presentation:** May present with swelling and pain in the anterior palate, and may be associated with a salty taste (from cyst fluid drainage)
- **Asymptomatic cases:** Many are asymptomatic and identified incidentally on routine dental radiography

### Investigations

**1. Radiography:**
- The radiolucency is typically symmetrical and heart-shaped with a sclerotic border
- Located in the midline anterior maxilla, between or above the roots of the central incisor teeth
- May resemble periapical pathology of the incisor teeth
- Asymptomatic lesions less than 10 mm should be observed rather than removed

**2. Vitality testing:**
- Testing the vitality of the incisor teeth is an important diagnostic step — teeth are vital in nasopalatine duct cyst (unlike radicular cysts where the associated tooth is non-vital)
- This is the key distinguishing feature from periapical inflammatory cysts

**3. CT scan:**
- Helpful for larger lesions to delineate extent and relationship to adjacent structures
- Demonstrates a well-defined midline radiolucency in the incisive canal region

**4. Histopathology:**
- The typical histological appearance is a thick-walled sac, with a lumen lined by both squamous and respiratory type epithelia
- Often contains sizeable neurovascular bundles (branches of the long sphenopalatine vessels and nasopalatine nerve)
- Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination, especially when incisor teeth are diseased or non-vital

### Management

**Observation:**
- Asymptomatic lesions less than 10 mm identified incidentally on radiography should be observed with periodic follow-up rather than removed

**Surgical enucleation:**
- Symptomatic cysts or those larger than 10 mm are managed by surgical enucleation
- Approach is via the palatal mucosa through an incision around the incisive papilla
- The cyst is carefully dissected from surrounding structures
- Care must be taken to avoid damage to the roots of adjacent incisor teeth and neurovascular structures (nasopalatine nerve and vessels)
- The entire cyst lining must be submitted for histological examination
- Submission should always be accompanied by a description of location in relation to roots and crown of adjacent teeth, the status of the teeth, and the size, shape, margins and radiodensity of the lesion

**Marsupialization:**
- May be considered for very large cysts to reduce size before definitive enucleation
- Less commonly used

## Part C: Fibrous Dysplasia — Types, Pathology and Management

### Definition

Fibrous dysplasia (FD) is a benign bone disorder characterised by the progressive replacement of normal medullary bone with abnormal fibrous tissue and immature woven bone. It results from a post-zygotic activating mutation of the GNAS1 gene encoding the alpha subunit of the stimulatory G-protein, leading to constitutive activation of adenylyl cyclase and increased cAMP production.

### Types

**1. Monostotic Fibrous Dysplasia (80%)**
- Affects a single bone
- Craniofacial involvement occurs in up to 25% of cases
- The maxilla and mandible are most commonly affected
- Although monostotic FD in long bones does not cross the joint line, it can cross bony sutures in the maxillofacial skeleton
- Most cases (75%) are diagnosed before the age of 30 and tend to stabilise after the patient reaches skeletal maturity
- Patients are frequently asymptomatic, often diagnosed incidentally

**2. Polyostotic Fibrous Dysplasia (20%)**
- Various areas of the skeleton can be affected
- Craniofacial involvement present in 40-50% of cases
- May present early with manifestations of bone pain and deformity
- Both variants can lead to symptoms of vascular or neurological compression

**3. McCune-Albright Syndrome**
- Polyostotic FD combined with endocrine hyperfunction (such as precocious puberty and/or hyperthyroidism) and cafe-au-lait spots
- This rare condition primarily affects females
- Associated with a gain of function mosaic mutation in the Gs alpha subunit

| Feature | Monostotic | Polyostotic | McCune-Albright |
|---------|-----------|-------------|-----------------|
| Frequency | 80% | 20% | Rare |
| Bones affected | Single | Multiple | Multiple |
| Craniofacial | 25% | 40-50% | Common |
| Endocrine | No | No | Yes (precocious puberty) |
| Cafe-au-lait | No | Possible | Yes |
| Malignant risk | 0.5% | 0.5% | 4% |

### Clinical Features in the Craniofacial Region

- Craniofacial disease can present as headaches or facial asymmetry
- Progressive painless bony swelling — most common presentation
- Sphenoid and frontal sinus FD may lead to optic nerve compression and mucocele formation respectively
- Nasal obstruction from expansion of ethmoid or maxillary bone
- Proptosis and diplopia when orbit is involved
- Dental malocclusion when maxilla or mandible is involved

### Investigations

**CT scan:**
- Lesions appear fairly homogenous with a characteristic 'ground glass' appearance
- As the lesion scleroses, 'cotton wool' areas appear
- Demonstrates extent of bony involvement and relationship to vital structures

**MRI:**
- Helps evaluate the soft tissue component
- Can distinguish FD from other lesions such as meningiomas and bone cysts
- FD displays low to intermediate signal on T1-weighted images and is hypointense on T2-weighted images

**Biopsy:**
- If there is any doubt about diagnosis, biopsy is recommended
- Histology shows replacement of normal bone by fibrous tissue with irregularly shaped trabeculae of woven bone (Chinese letter pattern)

### Malignant Transformation

- FD has a low rate of malignant transformation to osteosarcoma
- Occurs in 0.5% of polyostotic forms
- Occurs in 4% of lesions with McCune-Albright syndrome
- Any painful, rapidly growing lesion should raise suspicion for malignant transformation
- **There is no role for [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] in treating FD** (risk of sarcomatous change)

### Surgical Management

**Conservative management** is recommended for most cases:

**1. Medical treatment:**
- Limited to symptomatic relief
- Bisphosphonates (pamidronate, zoledronic acid) are useful in reducing the incidence of fractures and bone pain
- Regular monitoring with serial imaging

**2. Observation:**
- Asymptomatic patients with monostotic FD can be observed
- Most lesions stabilise after skeletal maturity

**3. Endoscopic sinus surgery:**
- Can provide symptomatic relief by improving sinus drainage pathways
- Paranasal sinus lesions causing sinusitis may be managed endoscopically
- Ideally performed with the aid of computer-assisted navigation due to distorted anatomy

**4. Optic nerve decompression:**
- Indicated when sphenoid bone FD causes optic nerve compression
- Can be performed endoscopically with navigation
- Urgent intervention required for progressive visual loss

**5. Local controlled resection and recontouring:**
- Reserved for more severe cases and those with cosmetic deformity
- Feasibility depends on symptoms and location
- Aim is to restore normal contour rather than complete excision (which is rarely possible in craniofacial FD)
- Radical resection is avoided as lesion involves vital structures

**6. Mucocele management:**
- Frontal sinus FD may lead to mucocele formation
- Endoscopic drainage or marsupialisation of mucocele

### Fibrous Dysplasia vs Ossifying Fibroma — Differential Diagnosis

It is important to distinguish FD from ossifying fibroma (OF), which requires different management:

| Feature | Fibrous Dysplasia | Ossifying Fibroma |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| Margins | Ill-defined, merges with bone | Well-defined, encapsulated |
| CT appearance | Ground glass, diffuse | Eggshell rim, central lucency |
| Growth pattern | Stabilises at maturity | Continues to grow |
| Treatment | Conservative/recontouring | Complete excision |
| Recurrence | Low after maturity | High if incomplete excision |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Nasopalatine duct cyst is the most common non-odontogenic cyst, accounting for 5-10% of all jaw cysts
> 2. It arises from epithelial remnants of the vestigial Jacobson's organ within the nasopalatine canal
> 3. Classic radiographic appearance is a heart-shaped midline radiolucency with sclerotic border in the anterior maxilla
> 4. Vitality testing of adjacent incisor teeth is the key diagnostic step — teeth are vital (unlike radicular cysts)
> 5. Asymptomatic nasopalatine cysts less than 10 mm should be observed rather than surgically removed
> 6. Fibrous dysplasia is monostotic in 80% of cases; maxilla and mandible are most commonly affected in the craniofacial skeleton
> 7. McCune-Albright syndrome = polyostotic FD + endocrine hyperfunction + cafe-au-lait spots (primarily females)
> 8. CT shows characteristic ground glass appearance in fibrous dysplasia
> 9. Malignant transformation rate is 0.5% (polyostotic) and 4% (McCune-Albright) — radiotherapy is contraindicated
> 10. Management is conservative — bisphosphonates for pain, endoscopic surgery for sinus drainage, recontouring for cosmetic deformity


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Heart-shaped radiolucency** in the anterior maxilla midline with vital adjacent teeth = nasopalatine duct cyst until proven otherwise
> - **Salty taste** in the mouth may be the presenting complaint of a nasopalatine duct cyst (fluid drainage)
> - **Ground glass on CT** is the hallmark of fibrous dysplasia — cotton wool areas indicate sclerosis
> - **FD crosses sutures** in the craniofacial skeleton (unlike long bones where it does not cross joints)
> - **Never irradiate fibrous dysplasia** — risk of sarcomatous transformation
> - **Skeletal maturity** is the turning point — most monostotic FD stabilises, reducing need for intervention
