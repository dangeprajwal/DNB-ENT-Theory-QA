# Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma — Aetiopathogenesis, Classification, Clinical Features and Management

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Nasopharyngeal carcinoma — etiopathogenesis, clinical features and management. Histologic subtypes, T classification (AJCC). Classification and etiopathogenesis.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 8 (Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma)

---

## Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) has a different aetiology, epidemiology and biology compared to other cancers of the upper aerodigestive tract. It has been called an 'enigmatic tumour'. The deep location of the nasopharynx and the wide spectrum of presenting symptoms make early diagnosis difficult. The distinct tumour biology requires different staging and treatment paradigms compared to other head and neck cancers. NPC demonstrates one of the largest geographical variations in incidence among all head and neck cancers — the incidence in endemic areas can be up to 50 times higher than low prevalence areas. Central to its pathogenesis is the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), with genetic susceptibility and environmental factors contributing to a complex, multistep carcinogenesis.

---

## PART A: Applied Anatomy

The nasopharynx is the most superior part of the pharynx with the inferior border defined as the lower level of the soft palate. Anteriorly it connects to the nasal cavities through the posterior choana; superiorly and posteriorly, the roof is formed by the floor of the sphenoid sinus and the clivus. The cartilaginous Eustachian tubes open on the lateral walls. The fossa of Rosenmüller (lateral pharyngeal recess) extends posterolaterally — most early NPC primaries originate here.

### Key Anatomical Relationships

| Structure | Significance |
|---|---|
| Fossa of Rosenmüller | Most common site of origin; difficult to visualize; ICA is postero-lateral |
| Pharyngobasilar fascia | Outermost layer; lateral to it are the [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]]s |
| Floor of sphenoid | Thin bone; cancer easily spreads to sphenoid sinus and orbital apex |
| Sphenopalatine foramen | Route of spread to pterygopalatine fossa, foramen rotundum, cavernous sinus |
| Carotid sheath | Cancer spreads along it superiorly to foramen lacerum and intracranially |

### Lymphatic Drainage

The nasopharynx has extensive bilateral lymphatic drainage. First echelon nodes are the retropharyngeal lymph nodes (nodes of Rouvière), followed by upper jugular nodes and upper posterior triangle nodes, then progressing inferiorly in a stepwise fashion. Skip metastases are very rare.

## PART B: Epidemiology and Aetiopathogenesis

### Epidemiology

- Over 86 000 new cases worldwide (2012) with over 50 000 deaths
- **Highest incidence:** Southern China (Cantonese population, Guangdong Province) — once 30 per 100 000 males per year in Hong Kong (1980s), now declining to ~20 per 100 000
- **Intermediate incidence:** Southeast Asia (Malay, Indonesian), North Africa (Tunisia, Algeria), Inuits (Alaska, Canada, Greenland)
- **Low incidence:** Caucasians, Japanese, Koreans, Northern Chinese (<1 per 100 000)
- Male:female ratio 3:1; peak age 50–55 years
- Migrants from endemic areas retain elevated risk, which decreases in second and third generation migrants
- Approximately 10% of NPC patients have familial clustering

### Aetiology — The Triad

NPC results from a complex interplay of three factors:

**1. Genetic susceptibility:**
- Wide geographical variation implies genetic predisposition
- HLA subtypes: A2, B14, B46 → increased risk; A11, B13, B22 → decreased risk
- Genome-wide association studies identify strong association at 6p21 locus (HLA region)
- Individual HLA subtypes may confer impaired immune response to EBV, allowing latent infection

**2. Epstein-Barr virus (EBV):**
- Central to pathogenesis of endemic NPC
- EBV DNA sequences are found in the nucleus of all cancer cells of non-keratinizing NPC
- EBV typically infects B-lymphocytes but can infect nasopharyngeal epithelium in genetically susceptible individuals
- Proposed sequence: genetic susceptibility → increased risk of latent EBV infection in nasopharyngeal epithelium → EBV genome incorporation → malignant transformation
- EBV is NOT found in keratinizing (WHO type 1) NPC

**3. Environmental factors:**
- Childhood consumption of salted fish increases risk (carcinogenic nitrosamines)
- Preserved foods also increase risk
- Smoking moderately increases risk (more pronounced effect on keratinizing NPC in Western populations)

## PART C: Pathology and Classification

### WHO Classification of NPC

The original WHO classification categorized NPC into three subtypes. The revised classification combined types 2 and 3:

| Classification | Histology | EBV Association |
|---|---|---|
| Keratinizing SCC (WHO type 1) | Well-differentiated, keratinization | EBV negative |
| Non-keratinizing — differentiated (WHO type 2) | Lymphocyte-rich; no keratinization | EBV positive |
| Non-keratinizing — undifferentiated (WHO type 3) | Lymphoepithelial carcinoma; abundant T-lymphocytes (CD8+) | EBV positive |

- In endemic areas: non-keratinizing NPC constitutes >95% of cases
- In low-incidence populations: non-keratinizing still predominates, but 25% are keratinizing subtype
- The term 'lymphoepithelial carcinoma' describes the distinctive feature of malignant cells infiltrated with lymphocytes and plasma cells
- Basaloid SCC is a rare subtype

### Immunohistochemistry

- EBER (EBV-encoded small RNA) immunostaining is used to detect EBV in cancer cells
- Helps differentiate NPC from other head and neck cancers, particularly in metastatic lymph node tissue

## PART D: Clinical Features and Presentation

Early NPC produces minimal symptoms. The central location means cancer can spread in all directions, producing symptoms that may not immediately suggest a nasopharyngeal origin. Symptoms are classified into four categories:

### Nasal Symptoms (~50%)
- Blood-stained nasal discharge or post-nasal drip (most common)
- Nasal obstruction (if tumour large enough to obstruct posterior choana)
- Cacosmia

### Otological Symptoms (30–40%)
- Unilateral hearing loss, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], ear blockage
- Caused by Eustachian tube dysfunction from tumour bulk/invasion
- Otoscopic examination reveals middle ear effusion
- **Key principle:** All adults with unexplained persistent unilateral OME should have the nasopharynx examined to exclude NPC

### Cervical Symptoms (~70% at presentation)
- Enlarging upper neck mass — most common symptom prompting specialist referral
- Up to 70% have enlarged cervical nodes at presentation; one-third bilateral
- Most frequently involved nodes: level II and upper level V
- Spread is orderly, superior to inferior; skip metastases very rare
- Isolated supraclavicular node is almost never from NPC (staged N3b)

### Neurological Symptoms (advanced disease)
- Headache (20%) — vertex or occiput, caused by clivus invasion
- Facial pain and midface numbness — invasion of pterygopalatine fossa (V2)
- CN palsies: V2, V3 and VI most common; III and IV indicate cavernous sinus invasion
- Ophthalmoplegia, decreased vision, proptosis — orbital apex invasion
- Trismus — direct invasion of pterygoid muscles
- Horner syndrome — carotid encasement (uncommon; usually with X, XI palsies)

### Systemic Metastasis
- Uncommon presentation; usually with advanced local/nodal disease
- Common sites: liver, lung, bone; brain metastasis rare
- ~1% develop dermatomyositis as a paraneoplastic syndrome (12% of dermatomyositis patients in endemic areas develop NPC)

## PART E: Diagnosis and Staging

### Diagnostic Workup

**Endoscopy and biopsy:**
- Nasopharyngoscopy (flexible or rigid endoscope, <4 mm diameter) is the primary investigation
- Fossa of Rosenmüller must be carefully inspected
- Most cancers present as a mass with abnormal capillaries and ulceration; some are infiltrative (submucosal)
- Biopsy using ethmoid biting forceps under endoscopic guidance; GA biopsy for inconclusive cases (multiple deep biopsies from roof, central NP, both fossae of Rosenmüller)

**Laboratory tests:**

| Test | Sensitivity | Specificity | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| EBV IgA VCA | 89% | 80% | Screening; low cost |
| EBV IgA EA | 63% | 95% | Confirmation |
| Plasma EBV DNA (qt-PCR) | 96% | 93% | Superior to serology; prognostication, monitoring, recurrence screening |
| NP brushing for EBV DNA | 98.9% | 99.3% | Highest accuracy |
| FNAC + EBER staining | High | High | Definitive diagnosis from neck node |

Plasma EBV DNA is the most versatile tumour marker — it correlates with stage, rapidly drops to undetectable levels within 1–2 weeks of completing treatment, and rising levels after treatment signify recurrence or distant metastasis.

**Imaging:**
- **MRI** — preferred modality for staging; superior soft tissue resolution; delineates parapharyngeal extension, perineural spread, marrow infiltration
- **CT** — widely available; shows bone erosions; used for radiation planning
- **[[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]]** — useful for detecting distant metastases (especially with bulky nodal disease); superior for detecting recurrent disease; recommended with rising EBV DNA after treatment
- **Ultrasound + FNAC** — high sensitivity and specificity for nodal metastasis

### AJCC 8th Edition Staging

| T Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| T1 | Confined to NP, or extends to oropharynx/nasal cavity without parapharyngeal involvement |
| T2 | Parapharyngeal extension and/or infiltration of medial/lateral pterygoid or prevertebral muscles |
| T3 | Invades bony skull base, cervical vertebra, pterygoid structures, paranasal sinuses |
| T4 | Intracranial extension and/or CN involvement, hypopharynx, orbit, parotid, beyond lateral pterygoid |

| N Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| N1 | Unilateral cervical and/or unilateral/bilateral retropharyngeal nodes, ≤6 cm, above caudal border of cricoid |
| N2 | Bilateral cervical nodes, ≤6 cm, above caudal border of cricoid |
| N3 | >6 cm and/or extension below caudal border of cricoid |

**Key differences from other HNSCC N staging:** Number of nodes is NOT considered; laterality (bilateral = N2), size (>6 cm) and inferior extension (below cricoid = N3) are the determining factors.

| Stage | TNM |
|---|---|
| I | T1 N0 M0 |
| II | T1N1, T2N0-1 M0 |
| III | T1-2N2, T3N0-2 M0 |
| IVA | T4 or N3, M0 |
| IVB | Any T, any N, M1 |

## PART F: Treatment

### Primary Treatment

NPC is highly radiosensitive. [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]] is the cornerstone of locoregional treatment for all stages without distant metastases.

**Radiotherapy — IMRT:**
- IMRT is the standard of care
- Dose: 66–72 Gy to GTV (gross tumour volume); 60 Gy to high-risk CTV; 54–60 Gy for prophylactic nodal irradiation
- Bilateral neck always irradiated (levels I–V)
- Simultaneous integrated boost allows different doses to different targets in the same treatment
- Local control rates >90% with IMRT
- IMRT reduces complications of conventional 2D RT: hearing loss, xerostomia, temporal lobe necrosis, trismus

**Chemotherapy:**
- Concurrent chemoradiation is the gold standard for stage II (with higher tumour load), III and IV disease
- The Intergroup 0099 trial established concurrent cisplatin + adjuvant cisplatin/5-FU as the standard
- MAC-NPC meta-analysis (19 trials, 4798 patients): significant overall survival benefit with chemotherapy (HR 0.79); most significant with concurrent chemotherapy (HR of death: 0.6)
- Cisplatin: weekly 30–40 mg/m² or 3-weekly 100 mg/m²; total dose ≥200 mg/m² during RT needed for survival benefit
- Induction chemotherapy (cisplatin + capecitabine) is being investigated
- Cetuximab has NOT shown benefit in NPC (unlike other HNSCC)

**Treatment by stage:**
- Stage I: Radiotherapy alone
- Stage II (low risk): Radiotherapy alone
- Stage II (higher tumour load), III, IVA: Concurrent chemoradiation
- IVB (distant metastasis): Palliative chemotherapy ± treatment of oligometastases

### Salvage Treatment

**Local failure:**
- Nasopharyngectomy for salvaging radiation failures in rT1 and rT2 disease (5-year local control ~70–74%)
- Approaches: transpalatal (inferior), transcervico-mandibulo-palatal (inferolateral), midfacial degloving (anterior), maxillary swing (anterolateral, best lateral exposure, 74% 5-year local control), endoscopic endonasal (>80% 2-year local control), robotic nasopharyngectomy (86% 2-year local control)
- Re-irradiation: 3D stereotactic RT/IMRT offers improved results over 2D (5-year OS 40%); brachytherapy for small tumours <2 cm (5-year survival 50–60%)

**Nodal failure:**
- Radical [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] is the standard of care (5-year local control 66%, 5-year OS 37%)
- Level I nodes rarely involved and can be spared
- Extracapsular spread in up to 70%
- Brachytherapy tubes may be placed during neck dissection for advanced nodal disease

**Distant metastasis:**
- First-line: cisplatin + 5-fluorouracil (median OS ~12 months)
- Second-line: gemcitabine, docetaxel, capecitabine, paclitaxel (response rates 50–70%)
- Oligometastases: surgical resection, radiofrequency ablation or RT may offer better survival than chemotherapy alone

## PART G: Complications of Treatment

### Early Complications
- Mucositis (up to 90% need enteral feeding)
- Xerostomia (IMRT allows parotid-sparing for T1-T2)
- [[OME Pathophysiology|Otitis media with effusion]] (grommets NOT recommended during/soon after RT — high risk of persistent perforation)

### Late Complications
- Sensorineural hearing loss; osteoradionecrosis of temporal bone
- Olfactory dysfunction, nasal crusting, intranasal adhesions
- Radiation fibrosis — neck stiffness, trismus
- Lower CN palsies — from skull base ORN or fibrosis (differential: local recurrence)
- Hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction — subtle hormonal deficiencies requiring lifelong replacement
- Temporal lobe necrosis — personality changes, memory loss, seizures (rare with IMRT)
- Late dysphagia (up to 50% with concurrent chemoradiation)
- Osteoradionecrosis of skull base and mandible
- Radiation-induced malignancies (0.3–3%, usually >5 years post-RT) — SCC at field margins, fibro/osteosarcomas
- ICA aneurysm — rare but life-threatening; sentinel bleeds followed by massive [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]]; treat with endovascular stenting or ICA ligation with bypass

## PART H: Prognosis

Treatment results have improved remarkably — threefold reduction in mortality in Hong Kong over 30 years. Contemporary 5-year disease-specific survival (AJCC 8th edition):

| Stage | 5-Year DSS |
|---|---|
| I | 100% |
| II | 90% |
| III | 67% |
| IVA | 67% |
| IVB (previous) | 68% |
| IVC (distant metastasis) | 18% |

Locoregional control rates exceed 90% at 2–5 years with IMRT. Distant metastases are now the dominant pattern of failure (5-year distant recurrence rate 19%). Most failures occur within 2 years but recurrence can present >10 years post-treatment.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. NPC has the largest geographical variation in incidence among all HN cancers — highest in Southern China (Cantonese), with the male:female ratio 3:1 and peak age 50–55 years
> 2. The aetiology involves a triad of genetic susceptibility (HLA A2, B14, B46), EBV infection and environmental factors (salted fish, preserved foods, smoking) — EBV DNA is found in all cancer cells of non-keratinizing NPC
> 3. The WHO classification divides NPC into keratinizing SCC (type 1, EBV negative) and non-keratinizing carcinoma (types 2 and 3, EBV positive) — non-keratinizing constitutes >95% of cases in endemic areas
> 4. Up to 70% of patients present with cervical lymphadenopathy — the most frequently involved nodes are level II and upper level V, with orderly superior-to-inferior spread
> 5. All adults with unexplained persistent unilateral OME should be investigated for NPC — otological symptoms occur in 30–40% of patients from Eustachian tube dysfunction
> 6. Plasma EBV DNA (qt-PCR) is the most versatile tumour marker — 96% sensitivity and 93% specificity for detection; also useful for staging, monitoring treatment response and detecting recurrence
> 7. The N staging of NPC differs from other HNSCC — number of nodes is NOT considered; laterality (bilateral = N2), size (>6 cm) and extension below cricoid (N3) determine the N stage
> 8. IMRT is the standard of care, achieving local control rates >90% — concurrent chemoradiation with cisplatin is the gold standard for stage II (high tumour load) and stage III–IV disease (MAC-NPC meta-analysis, HR death 0.6)
> 9. Salvage nasopharyngectomy for local failure achieves 70–74% 5-year local control — approaches include maxillary swing (best lateral exposure), endoscopic and robotic techniques; only rT1 and rT2 benefit from salvage
> 10. Cetuximab has NOT shown benefit in NPC unlike other HNSCC — newer agents (gemcitabine, taxanes) are available for recurrent or metastatic disease with response rates of 50–70%

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The fossa of Rosenmüller is the most common site of origin for NPC — it must be carefully inspected during nasopharyngoscopy; asking the patient to swallow and then say 'arh' closes the velopharynx and pulls the Eustachian cushions anteriorly, exposing a deep fossa
> - Plasma EBV DNA levels drop to undetectable within 1–2 weeks of completing treatment — persistently elevated levels signify residual disease; a progressive rise after initial drop indicates recurrence
> - Nasopharyngeal biopsy should NOT be assessed too early after radiotherapy — tumour cells continue to undergo apoptosis for weeks; disease should only be considered persistent if biopsy is positive at 12 weeks after RT
> - Grommets are NOT recommended during or soon after radiotherapy for NPC-related OME — they are associated with a high incidence of persistent perforation and purulent otorrhoea
> - The maxillary swing approach provides the widest exposure of the nasopharynx and parapharyngeal space for salvage nasopharyngectomy — it allows palpation of the parapharyngeal ICA during dissection (5-year local control 74%)
> - Dermatomyositis occurs as a paraneoplastic syndrome in approximately 1% of NPC patients — up to 12% of dermatomyositis patients in endemic areas develop NPC
