# Obstructive Sleep Apnoea — Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, Classification and Management

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Polysomnography — levels and indications. Medical management of OSAS. OSA — pathophysiology. Indications for surgical intervention. OSAS — diagnostic criteria and classification. Surgical management of severe OSA including tongue base surgery. Evaluation and management of OSA in an obese adult.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapters 73 (Physiology of Sleep and Sleep Disorders), 74 (Obstructive Sleep Apnoea: Medical Management) and 75 (The Surgical Management of Snoring and OSA)

---

## Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea–hypopnoea syndrome (OSAHS) is characterised by repetitive upper airway obstruction during sleep, causing sleep fragmentation, cardiovascular stimulation and oxygen desaturation. It affects approximately 5% of the population — 4% of men and 2% of women. The disorder has become increasingly prevalent with the obesity epidemic, particularly as 75% of individuals with BMI greater than 40 have OSA. OSAHS is associated with significant cardiovascular morbidity, metabolic dysfunction and a sevenfold increase in road traffic accident risk. Successful management requires accurate diagnosis through polysomnography, and treatment ranges from CPAP (the mainstay for moderate–severe disease) through mandibular advancement splints to multilevel surgical intervention. The ENT surgeon plays a central role in identifying sites of obstruction and selecting appropriate surgical procedures.

---

## PART A: Definitions and Classification

### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Apnoea | Absence of breathing ≥10 seconds |
| Obstructive apnoea | >90% reduction in airflow ≥10 s with continued respiratory effort |
| Hypopnoea | ≥30% reduction in airflow amplitude with ≥3% oxygen desaturation or EEG arousal |
| AHI | Apnoea–Hypopnoea Index: events per hour of sleep |
| OSAHS | AHI >5/hr with excessive daytime somnolence or ≥2 symptoms |

### AHI Severity Classification

| Severity | AHI (events/hour) |
|---|---|
| Normal | <5 |
| Mild | 5–15 |
| Moderate | 16–30 |
| Severe | >30 |

### ICSD-3 Classification of Sleep Disorders

The International Classification of Sleep Disorders (Third Edition) categorises sleep disorders into six major groups:

1. **Insomnia** — difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep
2. **Sleep-related breathing disorders** — OSA, central sleep apnoea (CSA), obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS)
3. **Central disorders of hypersomnolence** — narcolepsy, idiopathic hypersomnia
4. **Circadian rhythm sleep–wake disorders** — delayed/advanced sleep phase, jet lag, shift work
5. **Parasomnias** — NREM (sleepwalking, night terrors), REM (REM behaviour disorder)
6. **Sleep-related movement disorders** — restless legs syndrome, periodic limb movement syndrome

### Related Conditions

**Upper airway resistance syndrome (UARS):** Increased respiratory effort against a partially obstructed airway causing arousals but without frank apnoeas or hypopnoeas. AHI is typically normal but the respiratory disturbance index is elevated.

**Central sleep apnoea (CSA):** Cessation of breathing with a patent airway due to absent central respiratory drive. Cheyne-Stokes respiration is a form seen in heart failure patients.

**Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS):** Defined by BMI ≥30 combined with sleep-disordered breathing, nocturnal hypoxia and daytime hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45 mmHg). Approximately one-third of individuals with BMI >40 have elevated CO2. OHS carries a significantly worse prognosis than OSA alone.

## PART B: Pathophysiology

### Mechanism of Upper Airway Obstruction

The pharynx is the only segment of the airway that lacks rigid support. During sleep, the loss of tonic activity in the pharyngeal dilator muscles (particularly genioglossus) combined with the negative intraluminal pressure generated during inspiration leads to airway narrowing or collapse. This is exacerbated by anatomical factors that reduce the pharyngeal lumen and by neuromuscular factors that impair compensatory reflexes.

### Contributing Factors

**Anatomical factors** that narrow the airway include obesity (fat deposition in [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]]s), retrognathia, macroglossia, enlarged tonsils, elongated soft palate, nasal obstruction and craniofacial abnormalities. **Neuromuscular factors** include reduced pharyngeal dilator muscle tone during sleep, impaired upper airway reflexes and central respiratory drive instability.

### Pathophysiological Cascade

The repetitive cycle of obstruction, arousal and recovery produces a cascade of systemic effects:

1. **Intermittent hypoxia** — repeated oxygen desaturation generates free radicals and oxidative stress, activating inflammatory pathways
2. **Sympathetic activation** — arousals cause surges in sympathetic nervous system activity, increasing heart rate, blood pressure and peripheral vasoconstriction
3. **Inflammatory cascade** — elevated cytokines (TNF-alpha, IL-6, IL-8, CRP), adhesion molecules and free radicals promote systemic inflammation
4. **Insulin resistance** — intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation impair glucose metabolism, contributing to metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes
5. **Endothelial dysfunction** — oxidative stress and inflammation damage the vascular endothelium, promoting atherosclerosis
6. **Cardiovascular consequences** — hypertension (present in approximately 50%), coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias (particularly atrial fibrillation), stroke and pulmonary hypertension

### Metabolic Syndrome Association

OSAHS is independently associated with metabolic syndrome, which comprises central obesity, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance and elevated fasting glucose. The relationship is bidirectional — obesity promotes OSA, and OSA exacerbates metabolic dysfunction through intermittent hypoxia and sleep deprivation.

## PART C: Clinical Assessment and Evaluation

### Epidemiology and Risk Factors

OSAHS affects 2–4% of middle-aged males and 1–2% of females. The Wisconsin Sleep Cohort Study estimated a prevalence of 4% in males and 2% in females. Risk factors include:

- **Obesity** — the most important modifiable risk factor; 75% of those with BMI >40 have OSA
- **Male sex** — 2:1 male-to-female ratio
- **Increasing age** — prevalence rises with age
- **Ethnic variation** — Chinese populations develop OSA at lower BMI thresholds
- **Anatomical factors** — retrognathia, macroglossia, enlarged tonsils, nasal obstruction
- **Neck circumference** — >43 cm (17 inches) significantly increases risk
- **Alcohol and sedatives** — reduce pharyngeal muscle tone
- **Family history** — genetic component to craniofacial morphology

### Symptoms

| Nocturnal Symptoms | Daytime Symptoms |
|---|---|
| Loud snoring | Excessive daytime somnolence |
| Witnessed apnoeas/breath-holds | Unrefreshing sleep |
| Choking/gasping arousals | Morning headaches |
| Nocturia | Poor concentration/memory |
| Restless sleep | Irritability/mood changes |
| Nocturnal sweating | Reduced libido |

### Clinical Examination

Clinical assessment should include BMI (>28 is significant), neck circumference (>43 cm in males), Mallampati score (grades I–IV), Friedman tongue position, nasal airway assessment, oropharyngeal examination (tonsil size, soft palate length, uvula), assessment of retrognathia and dental occlusion.

### Screening Tools

**Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS):** A validated 8-item questionnaire scoring the likelihood of dozing in various situations (0–24). A score >10 indicates clinically significant daytime somnolence. The ESS is subjective and correlates poorly with AHI.

**STOP-BANG Questionnaire:** Screens for OSA risk using eight parameters — Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apnoeas, blood Pressure, BMI >35, Age >50, Neck circumference >40 cm, Gender male. A score of ≥3 indicates high risk of OSA.

## PART D: Diagnostic Investigations — Polysomnography

### Levels of Sleep Study

| Level | Type | Parameters Measured | Setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Full PSG | EEG, EOG, EMG, airflow, respiratory effort, oximetry, body position, ECG | In-hospital, attended |
| II | Comprehensive portable | Same as Level I | Home, unattended |
| III | Cardio-respiratory polygraphy | Airflow, respiratory effort, oximetry ± body position | Home, unattended |
| IV | Overnight oximetry | Oxygen saturation ± heart rate | Home, unattended |

### Overnight Pulse Oximetry (Level IV)

Overnight oximetry is the simplest screening tool. It records oxygen saturation throughout the night and calculates the oxygen desaturation index (ODI) — the number of desaturations of ≥4% per hour. An ODI >15 is suggestive of moderate–severe OSA. However, oximetry is specific but insensitive — it has a good positive predictive value (a positive result reliably indicates OSA) but a poor negative predictive value (a normal result does not exclude OSA). It cannot distinguish obstructive from central events and misses UARS entirely.

### Cardio-Respiratory Polygraphy (Level III)

Home multichannel monitoring measures nasal airflow (pressure transducer), thoracic and abdominal respiratory effort (bands), oxygen saturation and sometimes body position and snoring sound. It does not include EEG and therefore cannot score sleep stages or arousals. It is widely used as the first-line diagnostic tool in many centres and is adequate for diagnosing moderate–severe OSA in patients with a high pre-test probability.

### Full Polysomnography (Level I)

Full in-hospital attended PSG is the gold standard for diagnosis of OSAHS. In addition to the respiratory parameters measured by Level III devices, it includes electroencephalography (EEG), electro-oculography (EOG) and submental/tibial electromyography (EMG). This allows sleep staging (N1, N2, N3 and REM sleep), detection of arousals, assessment of sleep architecture and identification of periodic limb movements.

### Indications for Full PSG

- Diagnostic uncertainty after Level III/IV studies
- Suspected central sleep apnoea or Cheyne-Stokes respiration
- Concomitant sleep disorders (narcolepsy, parasomnias, periodic limb movement disorder)
- CPAP titration when autoCPAP trials fail
- Pre-operative assessment before upper airway surgery
- Assessment of complex patients with cardiopulmonary comorbidities
- Paediatric sleep-disordered breathing (PSG is the gold standard in children)
- Occupational requirements (commercial drivers, pilots)
- Discordance between clinical suspicion and Level III/IV results

### Sleep Staging on PSG

Normal sleep progresses through stages in 90-minute cycles. **N1** (light sleep, 5% of total sleep time) shows alpha wave dropout and theta waves. **N2** (45–55%) is characterised by sleep spindles and K-complexes. **N3** (slow-wave/deep sleep, 15–20%) shows delta waves and is the most restorative stage. **REM sleep** (20–25%) shows low-voltage mixed-frequency EEG with rapid eye movements and muscle atonia — this is the stage most associated with vivid dreaming and when OSA events are often most severe due to maximal muscle atonia.

## PART E: Medical Management

### CPAP Therapy

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is the mainstay of treatment for moderate–severe OSAHS. First described by Sullivan in 1981, CPAP acts as a pneumatic splint, maintaining upper airway patency by delivering continuous positive pressure through a nasal or oronasal mask.

**Types of CPAP:**
- **Fixed-pressure CPAP** — delivers a single set pressure throughout the night, determined by manual titration
- **Autotitrating CPAP (APAP)** — automatically adjusts pressure in response to detected flow limitation, snoring or apnoeas; useful for initial titration and for patients whose pressure requirements vary with position or sleep stage

**Pressure titration methods:**
- Manual in-laboratory titration during attended PSG
- Split-night study — diagnostic PSG in the first half, CPAP titration in the second
- Autotitrating CPAP trial — 1–2 week home trial with pressure data reviewed
- Predictive formula: P = (0.16 × BMI) + (0.13 × NC) + (0.04 × AHI) − 5.12 (where NC = neck circumference in cm)
- Average therapeutic pressure is 5–10 cmH2O

**NICE guidance** recommends CPAP as the first-line treatment for moderate and severe OSAHS. For mild OSA, CPAP is recommended only when lifestyle modifications and other measures have failed and there is significant daytime somnolence or cardiovascular comorbidity.

**Side effects** include nasal congestion and dryness, mask leak and skin abrasions, claustrophobia, aerophagia, conjunctivitis and noise disturbance to bed partners. Heated humidification reduces nasal symptoms. Compliance remains the major limitation — long-term adherence of ≥4 hours per night is achieved in approximately 50–70% of patients.

### Other Medical Treatments

**Mandibular advancement splints (MAS):** Custom-fitted oral appliances that protrude the mandible forward, increasing posterior airway space. Evidence shows approximately 52% improvement in AHI and 50–75% patient compliance. MAS are recommended for mild–moderate OSA or for patients who cannot tolerate CPAP. They are less effective than CPAP for severe disease.

**Lifestyle modifications:**
- Weight loss — bariatric surgery can dramatically reduce AHI in morbidly obese patients
- Avoidance of alcohol and sedatives, particularly in the evening
- Positional therapy — devices to prevent supine sleeping in position-dependent OSA
- Smoking cessation — reduces upper airway inflammation
- Regular exercise — improves OSA severity independent of weight loss

### Driving and Medico-Legal Issues

OSAHS is associated with a sevenfold increase in road traffic accidents. In many jurisdictions, patients with untreated moderate–severe OSAHS must not drive. In the UK, patients must inform the DVLA and demonstrate adequate treatment compliance before driving can resume. Commercial vehicle drivers have even stricter requirements.

## PART F: Surgical Management

### Principles

Surgery for OSA aims to enlarge the upper airway at the level(s) of obstruction. The concept of multilevel obstruction is fundamental — most patients with moderate–severe OSA have obstruction at multiple sites (nose, palate, tongue base, epiglottis). Surgery is generally considered when CPAP has failed or is not tolerated, or when there is a surgically correctable anatomical abnormality.

### Drug-Induced Sleep Endoscopy (DISE)

Sleep nasendoscopy (drug-induced sedation endoscopy) is a key investigation before surgical planning. Performed under propofol or midazolam sedation, it allows three-dimensional visualisation of the pharyngeal airway during simulated sleep. It identifies the precise site(s) and pattern of obstruction (anteroposterior, lateral, concentric collapse) at the level of the velum, oropharynx, tongue base and epiglottis. DISE has been validated as a reliable tool and guides site-specific surgical selection, improving surgical outcomes.

### Indications for Surgical Intervention

- Failure of or intolerance to CPAP therapy
- Failure of or intolerance to MAS
- Identifiable surgically correctable anatomical abnormality (enlarged tonsils, nasal obstruction, retrognathia)
- Patient preference for definitive treatment rather than lifelong device use
- Mild–moderate OSA with a clear single-site obstruction
- As adjunctive treatment to reduce CPAP pressure requirements (e.g. nasal surgery)
- Severe OSA with tongue base obstruction amenable to TORS or hypoglossal nerve stimulation

### Surgical Procedures

#### Nasal Surgery
Septoplasty, turbinate reduction and [[FESS Steps Complications|functional endoscopic sinus surgery]] improve nasal airway resistance. Nasal surgery alone has minimal impact on AHI or snoring but is important as an adjunct — it improves CPAP tolerance and reduces therapeutic CPAP pressure requirements.

#### Uvulopalatopharyngoplasty (UPPP)
UPPP involves excision of the uvula, posterior soft palate margin and redundant lateral pharyngeal tissue, usually combined with [[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]]. Success rates are 76% in the short term but decline to approximately 45% at long-term follow-up. The main limitation is that UPPP addresses only palatal-level obstruction and does not treat tongue base collapse. Complications include velopharyngeal insufficiency (28.5%), haemorrhage (0–6%), pharyngeal dryness (10%), dysphagia and voice change.

#### Laser-Assisted Uvulopalatoplasty (LAUP)
Performed under local anaesthesia using CO2 laser to reshape the soft palate. Short-term success is 79% but declines to 55% at long-term follow-up. Post-operative pain is worse than with UPPP. LAUP is generally not recommended for OSA (only for simple snoring) by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine.

#### Radiofrequency Tissue Reduction
Radiofrequency ablation of the soft palate (channelling or coblation-assisted uvulopalatoplasty/CAUP) causes submucosal tissue volume reduction through controlled thermal injury and fibrosis. Long-term success for palatal procedures is approximately 37%. It can also be applied to the tongue base. The procedure is minimally invasive with lower morbidity than UPPP.

#### Tongue Base Surgery — Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS)
TORS has emerged as a significant advance for tongue base obstruction. Using the da Vinci robotic system, lingual tonsil tissue and tongue base tissue are excised transorally with excellent visualisation and precision. Studies demonstrate approximately 80% polysomnographic success at 3-year follow-up. TORS is typically combined with palatal surgery as part of a multilevel approach. It is indicated for patients with tongue base obstruction identified on DISE who have failed or cannot tolerate CPAP.

#### Other Tongue Base Procedures
- **Genioglossus advancement** — bony window advanced from the inner mandibular surface to pull genioglossus muscle forward
- **Hyoid suspension** — hyoid bone repositioned anteriorly and superiorly
- **Tongue base suspension** — suture-based technique to prevent posterior tongue collapse
- **Midline glossectomy/lingualplasty** — volume reduction of the tongue base
- **Radiofrequency ablation of tongue base** — office-based, multiple sessions required

#### Pillar Implants
Three polyester implants placed into the soft palate to stiffen it and reduce vibration. Subjective improvement of approximately 53%. Used primarily for simple snoring and mild OSA.

#### Maxillomandibular Advancement (MMA)
Bilateral sagittal split osteotomy of the mandible combined with [[Le Fort Fractures|Le Fort]] I maxillary osteotomy advances the entire facial skeleton by 10–12 mm, dramatically increasing posterior airway space at all pharyngeal levels. Success rates of approximately 60% in the short term. MMA is considered for severe OSA with significant craniofacial contribution, particularly retrognathia, when other surgical options have failed. It carries significant morbidity including malocclusion, numbness and altered facial aesthetics.

#### Hypoglossal Nerve Stimulation
An implantable neurostimulator device delivers electrical stimulation to the hypoglossal nerve during inspiration, activating the genioglossus muscle to prevent tongue base collapse. The device consists of an implantable pulse generator, a stimulation electrode on the hypoglossal nerve and a respiratory sensing lead. Early clinical data show significant promise with sustained AHI reduction. Patient selection criteria typically include moderate–severe OSA, failure of CPAP, BMI <32, and absence of concentric palatal collapse on DISE.

#### [[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]]
Tracheostomy bypasses the upper airway entirely and is the only procedure guaranteed to eliminate OSA. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) considers tracheostomy a last resort, reserved for life-threatening OSA when all other treatments have failed, or as a temporary measure in the perioperative period for patients undergoing major head and neck surgery.

### Surgical Algorithm

A stepwise approach is recommended:
1. Optimise nasal airway (septoplasty, turbinate reduction) — facilitates CPAP use
2. Identify level(s) of obstruction with DISE
3. Palatal-level obstruction → UPPP ± tonsillectomy
4. Tongue base obstruction → TORS, hypoglossal nerve stimulation or genioglossus advancement
5. Multilevel obstruction → combined procedures (e.g. modified UPPP + TORS)
6. Skeletal contribution → maxillomandibular advancement
7. Refractory life-threatening OSA → tracheostomy

## PART G: Evaluation and Management of the Obese Adult with OSA

### Approach to the 45-Year-Old Obese Male

**History:** Enquire about snoring (onset, severity, positional variation), witnessed apnoeas, excessive daytime somnolence (quantify with ESS), unrefreshing sleep, morning headaches, nocturia, reduced concentration, driving concerns and sexual dysfunction. Document comorbidities — hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome. Review medications, alcohol intake and smoking history. Assess occupational implications.

**Examination:** Calculate BMI, measure neck circumference (>43 cm significant), assess Mallampati score and Friedman tongue position, examine nasal airway, oropharynx (tonsil size, palatal configuration, uvula), dental occlusion and retrognathia. Examine for signs of cor pulmonale (raised JVP, peripheral oedema). Blood pressure measurement.

**Investigations:**
- Bloods — FBC, TFTs (exclude hypothyroidism), fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipid profile
- Level III/IV sleep study initially; full PSG if diagnostic uncertainty or complex presentation
- Arterial blood gas if OHS suspected (daytime hypercapnia)
- DISE if surgical intervention is being considered

**Management plan (stepwise):**
1. **Lifestyle modification** — weight loss programme (target 10% weight loss), dietary counselling, exercise, alcohol avoidance, smoking cessation, positional therapy if supine-dependent
2. **CPAP therapy** — initiate autoCPAP trial for moderate–severe OSAHS; ensure mask fitting, heated humidification, compliance monitoring
3. **MAS** — if CPAP intolerant, refer for custom mandibular advancement splint (mild–moderate OSA)
4. **Bariatric surgery** — consider referral for morbidly obese patients (BMI >40 or >35 with comorbidities) — can dramatically improve or resolve OSA
5. **Upper airway surgery** — if CPAP and MAS fail, perform DISE to identify obstruction level(s) and select appropriate procedure(s)
6. **Multidisciplinary management** — coordinate with respiratory medicine, cardiology, endocrinology and bariatric surgery as indicated
7. **Driving advice** — counsel regarding DVLA notification requirements and road safety

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The majority of OSA morbidity arises from the pathophysiological cascade of intermittent hypoxia rather than from sleep fragmentation alone — this explains the strong association with cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome and stroke even in patients who do not report excessive daytime somnolence
> - A normal overnight oximetry does not exclude OSA — oximetry is specific but insensitive and will miss patients with UARS and mild OSA; clinical suspicion should prompt Level III or Level I investigation regardless of oximetry results
> - The concept of multilevel obstruction is fundamental to surgical planning — isolated palatal surgery (UPPP) in a patient with concomitant tongue base collapse will fail; DISE should be performed before any surgical intervention to identify all levels of obstruction
> - In the morbidly obese patient, bariatric surgery may be more effective than upper airway surgery — weight loss of even 10% can significantly reduce AHI, and bariatric surgery can resolve OSA in a substantial proportion of patients
> - Nasal surgery alone rarely cures OSA but plays an important adjunctive role — it improves CPAP tolerance, reduces therapeutic pressure requirements, and may allow patients who previously could not use CPAP to become compliant
> - TORS for tongue base obstruction represents a significant advance over older techniques — the robotic approach provides superior access and visualisation compared to open or conventional transoral approaches, with approximately 80% polysomnographic success sustained at 3 years
