# Paediatric [[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|Obstructive Sleep Apnoea]] — Definition, Evaluation and Management

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Sleep apnoea — definition. Evaluate an obese child with OSA.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 27 (Paediatric Obstructive Sleep Apnoea)

---

## Introduction

Paediatric obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is defined as prolonged partial upper airway obstruction and/or intermittent complete obstruction that disrupts normal ventilation during sleep and normal sleep patterns. It differs significantly from adult OSA in aetiology, pathophysiology and management. Habitual snoring affects approximately 12% of children, while OSA occurs in 0.7–1.8%. Adenotonsillar hypertrophy is the most common cause, peaking between ages 3 and 6 years, but obesity is an increasingly important contributor. Untreated paediatric OSA causes neurobehavioural impairment, cardiovascular dysfunction, growth disturbance and enuresis. Adeno[[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]] remains the first-line surgical treatment but does not achieve normal polysomnographic results in all children, particularly those who are obese or have severe pre-operative disease.

---

## PART A: Definitions

### Sleep Apnoea in Children

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Obstructive apnoea | Complete cessation of airflow ≥2 respiratory cycles despite continued respiratory effort |
| Obstructive hypopnoea | ≥50% reduction in airflow with ≥3% desaturation or arousal |
| OSA | Prolonged partial/intermittent complete obstruction disrupting ventilation and sleep |
| AHI | Number of apnoeas + hypopnoeas per hour of sleep |

**Important note:** Paediatric definitions differ from adult criteria — the threshold for abnormality is lower in children. An AHI >1 event per hour is considered abnormal in children (compared with >5 in adults). However, the scoring criteria used dramatically affect diagnosis — using AASM versus Stanford definitions changes the diagnostic rate from 19% to 99% in the same population.

### Severity Classification in Children

| Severity | AHI (events/hour) |
|---|---|
| Mild | 1–5 |
| Moderate | 5–10 |
| Severe | >10 |

## PART B: Aetiology and Pathophysiology

### Three Pathophysiological Groups

Paediatric OSA can be categorised into three groups based on the underlying cause:

**Group 1 — Adenotonsillar hypertrophy:** The most common cause in otherwise healthy children. Lymphoid tissue hypertrophy peaks between ages 3 and 6 years, corresponding to the highest prevalence of OSA in children. The relationship between tonsil size and OSA severity is not always linear — even moderately enlarged tonsils can cause significant obstruction in a child with a narrow airway.

**Group 2 — Obesity:** An increasingly important cause paralleling the childhood obesity epidemic. Obese children have OSA due to fatty infiltration of parapharyngeal tissues, reduced functional residual capacity, and altered respiratory mechanics. Obesity is the strongest predictor of residual OSA after adenotonsillectomy.

**Group 3 — Craniofacial and syndromic conditions:** Children with structural or neurological abnormalities have increased risk of OSA:

| Condition | OSA Prevalence |
|---|---|
| Down syndrome | 2/3 (66%) have SDB |
| Achondroplasia | 87% |
| Treacher Collins syndrome | 54% |
| Cleft palate (repaired) | 8.5% |
| CHARGE syndrome | High prevalence |
| Prader-Willi syndrome | High prevalence |
| Craniosynostosis syndromes | Variable |
| Neuromuscular disease | Variable |

### Consequences of Untreated Paediatric OSA

**Neurobehavioural effects:** Sleep fragmentation and intermittent hypoxia impair cognitive development, attention, memory and behaviour. Children with OSA may be misdiagnosed with attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). Academic performance is measurably reduced.

**Inflammatory basis:** Paediatric OSA is associated with systemic inflammation — elevated CRP, endothelin-1, TNF-alpha and myeloid-related proteins (MRP 8/14) have been demonstrated.

**Cardiovascular consequences:** Intermittent hypoxia causes baroreceptor impairment, raised blood pressure and, in severe untreated cases, cor pulmonale — though this is now rare with earlier recognition.

**Growth impairment:** Disruption of growth hormone secretion (which occurs predominantly during slow-wave sleep) can impair linear growth.

**Enuresis:** Nocturnal enuresis is significantly more common in children with OSA and often resolves after treatment.

## PART C: Evaluation of the Obese Child with OSA

### Clinical History

**Night-time symptoms:** Snoring (onset, frequency, severity), witnessed apnoeas, gasping or choking, mouth breathing, restless sleep, unusual sleeping positions (hyperextended neck, seated), nocturnal sweating, enuresis.

**Daytime symptoms:** Excessive daytime somnolence (less common in children than adults — children often become hyperactive rather than sleepy), behavioural problems, poor concentration, morning headaches, poor school performance, nasal obstruction, mouth breathing.

Clinical history alone is unreliable for diagnosing paediatric OSA — a meta-analysis demonstrated poor positive predictive value of symptoms for PSG-confirmed OSA.

### Clinical Examination

- **General:** Height, weight, BMI (plotted on age-appropriate growth charts), blood pressure
- **Facies:** Adenoid facies, retrognathia, midface hypoplasia, syndromic features
- **Nasal:** Anterior rhinoscopy for septal deviation, turbinate hypertrophy, nasal polyps
- **Oral:** Tonsil size (Brodsky grade 0–4 or Friedman grade 0–4), Mallampati score, palatal configuration, dental occlusion, tongue size
- **Neck:** Neck circumference
- **Chest:** Signs of respiratory distress, pectus excavatum
- **Cardiovascular:** Signs of cor pulmonale

### Indications for Investigations (Beyond Clinical Assessment)

In the UK, a consensus statement suggests that adenotonsillectomy without sleep studies is reasonable in otherwise healthy children with a clear clinical history and significantly enlarged tonsils. However, investigations are specifically indicated in the following groups:

- Age less than 2 years
- Weight less than 15 kg
- Down syndrome
- Cerebral palsy
- Craniofacial abnormalities
- **Obesity**
- Significant comorbidities (cardiac, respiratory, neuromuscular)
- Discordance between history and examination findings
- Consideration of non-surgical treatment
- Complex or recurrent OSA

The obese child therefore falls within the group requiring objective investigation before treatment.

### Overnight Oximetry

Overnight pulse oximetry is the most commonly used screening tool in paediatric practice. It has a positive predictive value of 97% — a positive result (significant desaturations) reliably indicates OSA. However, the negative predictive value is only 53% — a normal oximetry does not exclude OSA. It is useful for confirming suspected moderate–severe disease but cannot be relied upon to exclude the diagnosis.

### Polysomnography (Gold Standard)

Full attended polysomnography is the gold standard for diagnosing paediatric OSA. It measures nasal airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, CO2, EEG (sleep staging), EOG, EMG, ECG and body position. PSG quantifies severity (AHI), identifies the type of events (obstructive vs central), assesses sleep architecture and determines oxygen nadir. It is particularly important in obese children to establish baseline severity and guide management decisions.

## PART D: Management

### Conservative Measures

**Weight management:** For the obese child, weight loss is a fundamental component of management. Referral to a paediatric dietitian and multidisciplinary weight management programme is essential. Even modest weight reduction can improve AHI. Weight management should be initiated alongside other treatments rather than in isolation, as the time required for weight loss should not delay treatment of significant OSA.

**Intranasal corticosteroids:** A randomised controlled trial demonstrated that fluticasone nasal spray reduces AHI in children with mild–moderate OSA. Nasal steroids may be used as first-line treatment for mild OSA or as an adjunct to surgery.

### Adenotonsillectomy

Adenotonsillectomy remains the first-line surgical treatment for paediatric OSA regardless of BMI. Key evidence includes:

**CHAT Trial (Childhood Adenotonsillectomy Trial):** A landmark randomised controlled trial of 464 children aged 5–9 years with OSA. The primary outcome (neuropsychological attention and executive function at 7 months) was negative — no significant difference between surgery and watchful waiting. However, secondary outcomes showed significant improvements in polysomnographic parameters, behaviour, quality of life and symptoms. Importantly, 46% of children in the watchful waiting group had spontaneous resolution of OSA.

**Meta-analysis of 355 children:** Adenotonsillectomy normalised AHI in 83% of children.

**Study of 578 children:** Only 27% achieved a strictly normal AHI of <1 after adenotonsillectomy.

### Treatment Failure and Residual OSA

Residual OSA after adenotonsillectomy occurs in 11.6–31% of children. The strongest predictors of treatment failure are:
- **Obesity** — the most important predictor
- Severe pre-operative OSA (high baseline AHI)
- Age >7 years
- Syndromic or craniofacial conditions

In the obese child specifically, adenotonsillectomy is less likely to be curative, but it remains the recommended first-line intervention as it typically improves (even if it does not normalise) the AHI.

### Management of Persistent OSA After Surgery

- Repeat sleep study 6–8 weeks post-operatively to assess residual disease
- DISE (drug-induced sleep endoscopy) to identify persistent sites of obstruction
- Weight management intensification
- CPAP therapy — recommended for residual moderate–severe OSA; compliance in children is poor (average 3.3 hours/night)
- Further surgery based on DISE findings (e.g. supraglottoplasty, tongue base reduction)
- Nasopharyngeal airway — temporary measure in young children or those with craniofacial abnormalities

### Post-Operative Considerations

Children with obesity and OSA are at increased risk of post-operative respiratory complications (10.4% respiratory complication rate reported). Overnight monitoring is recommended after adenotonsillectomy in obese children. Codeine-based analgesia is contraindicated in children undergoing tonsillectomy due to the risk of fatal respiratory depression in CYP2D6 ultra-rapid metabolisers.

### Quality of Life Assessment

Treatment outcomes should be assessed using validated quality-of-life instruments:
- **OSA-18** — an 18-item disease-specific quality of life questionnaire for paediatric OSA
- **T14** — a 14-item tonsillectomy-specific outcome measure

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Children with OSA often present with hyperactivity and behavioural disturbance rather than the excessive daytime somnolence typical of adults — misdiagnosis as ADHD is well recognised and should prompt screening for SDB
> - The obese child with OSA represents a dual challenge — adenotonsillectomy is less likely to be curative but remains the recommended first-line surgical intervention, as it typically reduces even if it does not normalise the AHI
> - A negative overnight oximetry in a child with strong clinical suspicion of OSA should not be reassuring — the 53% negative predictive value means that nearly half of children with OSA will have normal oximetry, and full PSG should be arranged
> - The 46% spontaneous resolution rate in the CHAT trial watchful waiting group must be interpreted cautiously — these were children with mild–moderate OSA, and watchful waiting is not appropriate for severe disease or children with significant comorbidities
> - Weight management must be initiated alongside, not instead of, other treatments in the obese child — the time required for meaningful weight reduction should not delay treatment of significant OSA that is causing neurobehavioural impairment or cardiovascular strain
