# Lip and Buccal Carcinoma, Imaging in Oral Cancer

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the anatomy and lymphatic drainage of the lip. Discuss the clinical features, investigations and management of squamous cell carcinoma of the lip. What is the role of MRI and [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]] in oral cancer management? Describe the aetiology of gingivobuccal sulcus carcinoma and its management when there is mandibular involvement.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 12 (Oral Cavity Tumours Including Lip Reconstruction), Chapter 2 (Aetiology of Head and Neck Cancer), Chapter 37 (Imaging of the Neck)

---

## Introduction

The lip and buccal mucosa are two important subsites of the oral cavity with distinct epidemiological profiles. Lip carcinoma, predominantly squamous cell carcinoma of the lower lip, is strongly associated with chronic ultraviolet radiation exposure and carries a generally favourable prognosis when treated early. In contrast, buccal (gingivobuccal sulcus) carcinoma is the commonest oral cancer subsite in South East Asia, driven by the widespread cultural practice of betel quid (paan) chewing. The global incidence of oral cancer exceeds 300,000 new diagnoses annually, and it is the eighth most common malignancy worldwide. Cross-sectional imaging with MRI and PET-CT plays a pivotal role in the staging, treatment planning and post-treatment surveillance of oral cancer, each modality offering distinct strengths. Effective management of these tumours requires a multidisciplinary approach, with an understanding of the complex anatomy, patterns of regional spread, reconstructive options and the principles of mandibular management when bone is involved.

## PART A: Lip Anatomy and Lymphatic Drainage

### Surface Anatomy and Landmarks

The lips surround the oral cavity, extending from the nose superiorly to the chin inferiorly and bounded laterally by the nasolabial folds. They are separated at the oral commissure into upper and lower lip. The oral cavity begins at the mucocutaneous junction (vermilion border). Key external landmarks include:

- **Upper lip:** Philtral columns (near-vertical ridges from columella to vermilion), Cupid's bow (curved white roll between philtral column bases), philtral dimple
- **Lower lip:** The white roll is the main external landmark
- **Vermilion:** Extends from the white roll to the wet-dry border; composed of stratified squamous epithelium with a thin keratin layer; contains no glands; dryer and duller than mucosa
- **Mucosa:** Non-keratinized epithelium rich in minor salivary glands; lines the inner lip surface

### Cross-Sectional Structure

From external to internal, the lip comprises: skin and vermilion, subcutaneous fat (bulk of lip thickness), orbicularis oris muscle, submucous glands and mucosa.

### Musculature

| Muscle Group | Key Muscles | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Group I (modiolus) | Orbicularis oris, buccinator, zygomaticus major, risorius | Sphincter, oral competence |
| Group II (upper lip) | Levator labii superioris, zygomaticus minor | Elevate upper lip |
| Group III (lower lip) | Depressor labii inferioris, mentalis, platysma | Depress/elevate lower lip |

The **orbicularis oris** is the sphincter muscle of the lips, essential for oral competence. It originates at the modiolus just lateral to the oral commissure; the two muscles insert into a midline raphe in the lower lip but cross the midline to insert into the contralateral philtral column in the upper lip. The **mentalis** muscles are the primary elevators of the lower lip, maintaining it against the upper lip at rest.

### Innervation

- **Sensory:** Infraorbital nerve (maxillary division, V2) -- upper lip; mental nerve (mandibular division, V3) -- lower lip
- **Motor:** Buccal branches of facial nerve (VII) -- orbicularis oris; marginal mandibular branch of VII -- mentalis, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris

### Blood Supply

The arterial supply is from the **superior and inferior labial arteries**, branches of the facial artery. Each labial artery communicates with its counterpart across the lip, forming a continuous arterial circle around the oral sphincter. The arteries lie at the wet-dry border, just deep to orbicularis oris, sending numerous perforators through muscle to skin. The blood supply is so rich that local flaps can survive even without including a labial artery.

### Lymphatic Drainage

| Lip Region | Primary Nodal Drainage | Bilateral Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Upper lip (lateral) | Ipsilateral submandibular | Low |
| Upper lip (midline) | Submandibular, contralateral | Possible |
| Commissure | Periparotid, submandibular | Low |
| Lower lip (lateral) | Ipsilateral submandibular | Low |
| Lower lip (central) | Submental nodes | YES -- crosses midline |

Both submandibular and periparotid nodal regions subsequently drain to the jugulodigastric nodes. Drainage from the central lower lip frequently crosses the midline, making bilateral neck treatment a consideration.

## PART B: Squamous Cell Carcinoma of the Lip -- Features and Management

### Epidemiology and Risk Factors

- **Lower lip:** Accounts for 90--95% of lip SCC; the more sun-exposed surface
- **Upper lip:** More commonly affected by basal cell carcinoma
- **Demographics:** Male predominance; elderly patients; strong association with chronic UV exposure
- **Other risk factors:** Pipe and cigar smoking (increased risk compared to other head and neck subsites), immunosuppression, xeroderma pigmentosum
- **Actinic cheilitis:** Premalignant condition of the lower lip from chronic UV damage; treated by lip shave (vermilionectomy) with mucosa sutured directly to skin

### Clinical Features

Patients typically present with a painless ulcer or nodule on the vermilion of the lower lip. The lesion may be exophytic, ulcerative or verrucous in character. Key features to assess include:

- Size and extent of the lesion
- Involvement of the commissure (worse prognosis)
- Evidence of perineural invasion -- numbness of the lip (mental nerve) or upper lip (infraorbital nerve)
- Fixation to underlying mandible
- Cervical lymphadenopathy

### Investigations

- Incisional biopsy with depth assessment
- Orthopantomogram (OPG) if mandibular involvement suspected
- MRI for soft tissue extent, depth of invasion, perineural spread
- CT for cortical bone assessment
- Ultrasound-guided FNA of suspicious cervical nodes
- CT chest for advanced disease or multiple positive nodes

### Staging

The lip is staged using the AJCC 8th Edition oral cavity staging system. Depth of invasion (DOI) has replaced tumour thickness as a T-staging criterion:

- T1: Size up to 2 cm, DOI up to 5 mm
- T2: Size up to 2 cm with DOI >5--10 mm, OR size >2--4 cm with DOI up to 10 mm
- T3: Size >4 cm OR any tumour with DOI >10 mm
- T4a: Invasion through cortical bone, inferior alveolar nerve, floor of mouth, skin of face

### Management of the Primary Site

Treatment depends on tumour stage and the resulting defect size. Surgery is the mainstay, with reconstruction guided by the fraction of lip width excised:

| Defect Size | Reconstruction Options |
|---|---|
| Up to 1/3 lip width | Wedge excision + primary closure |
| 1/3 to 1/2 lip width | Karapandzic flap OR Abbe-Sabattini flap |
| Greater than 1/2 width | Bernard-von Burow advancement, Karapandzic, or [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]] |
| Vermilion only | Vermilion advancement flap or mucosal advancement |

**Key Reconstructive Flaps:**

- **Karapandzic flap:** Neurovascular rotation-advancement flap creating paired full-thickness mucomusculocutaneous lip flaps supplied by branches of the facial artery. It preserves sensory and motor nerves to the lip segment, maintaining oral competence. Considered arguably the best method for lower lip defects that cannot be directly closed. Microstomia is inevitable with large defects.
- **Abbe-Sabattini flap:** Lip-switch flap from the opposite lip, based on a single labial artery pedicle. The flap should be half the width of the lip defect to evenly distribute tissue. A two-stage procedure (pedicle divided at 2--3 weeks). Creates an insensate, adynamic segment; better suited for upper lip reconstruction.
- **Estlander flap:** Similar to Abbe-Sabattini but used for defects involving the commissure; single-stage but results in a blunted new commissure.
- **Bernard-von Burow (Freeman modification):** Bilateral cheek advancement facilitated by excision of full-thickness triangles above and below the defect at the nasolabial fold. The Freeman modification converts the lower incision to follow the natural lip-chin junction, preserving the orbicularis oris and thus some oral competence.
- **Vermilionectomy (lip shave):** For diffuse actinic cheilitis; mucosa is sutured directly to skin without undermining.

### Management of the Neck

- Elective [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] (levels I--III) for tumours greater than T2, depth of invasion >4--5 mm, or poorly differentiated histology
- Comprehensive neck dissection for clinically or radiologically positive nodes
- Central lower lip tumours carry a risk of bilateral drainage, so bilateral neck treatment may be necessary

### Prognosis

Overall prognosis for lip SCC is good, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 80% for early-stage disease. Adverse prognostic factors include perineural invasion, commissure involvement, large tumour size and positive cervical nodes.

## PART C: Gingivobuccal Sulcus Carcinoma -- Aetiology and Management

### Surgical Anatomy

The buccal mucosa is the mucosal lining of the inner surface of the cheek, extending from the oral commissure anteriorly to the retromolar trigone posteriorly. The inferior and superior boundaries are delineated by the mandibular and maxillary gingiva-buccal sulci respectively. The mucosa is non-keratinized, firmly attached to the underlying buccinator muscle. Minor salivary glands lie within the cheek. The parotid duct pierces the buccinator to enter the oral cavity adjacent to the first maxillary molar tooth. Sensory innervation is via the buccal branch of V3. The cheek thickness from mucosa to external skin is 1--3 cm.

### Aetiology

The buccal mucosa is the commonest site for oral cancer in South East Asia, accounting for up to 40% of oral cancers at this site. This contrasts sharply with Western countries where buccal carcinoma accounts for only 2--10% of oral cancers. The major aetiological factors are:

**Betel quid (Paan):** The primary driver of the geographic disparity. Main ingredients include the Piper betel leaf, slaked lime, spices, tobacco and areca nut. The slaked lime lowers the pH, accelerating the release of alkaloids from both tobacco and areca nut. The areca nut is now recognized as independently carcinogenic and is the main aetiological agent in [[Oral Submucous Fibrosis|oral submucous fibrosis]]. Individuals who chew betel frequently have a preference regarding which side they chew, and this corresponds to the side of tumour development.

**Khaini:** A mixture of tobacco and lime that is retained specifically in the inferior gingivobuccal sulcus, leading to cancer at this precise site. Oral cancer increases in a dose-dependent fashion with these agents.

**Other risk factors:** Smoking and alcohol consumption (particularly in populations where betel chewing is not prevalent), oral submucous fibrosis and lichen planus (both considered premalignant).

### Epidemiology

- Male-to-female ratio: approximately 1:1 in Western countries; 4:1 in India; as high as 27:1 in Taiwan (reflecting that betel quid users are primarily male)
- Typically occurs over the age of 40 years, although earlier in betel chewers
- Usually well-differentiated histologically

### Presentation

Buccal carcinoma may be verrucous, exophytic or ulcero-infiltrative. Patients present with pain, an intraoral mass, ulceration or trismus. Of concern, 40% or more present with Stage III/IV disease. Palpable lymphadenopathy may be as high as 57% for T3/T4 lesions, while occult nodal metastasis may be present in 26% of clinically N0 patients. Advanced tumours may extend into external skin, mandible or maxilla.

### Workup

Biopsy should be of sufficient depth to assess depth of invasion (critical for neck management). Imaging with MRI/CT is required, augmented with ultrasound if necessary. MRI is preferred for soft tissue extent; CT for cortical bone assessment.

### Treatment

**Primary site:**

- T1/T2: Peroral excision with 1 cm margins; buccinator muscle should be included as the deep margin
- T3/T4: Facial access incisions (upper or lower lip splits) may be required; partial maxillectomy or mandibular resection (rim or segmental) as indicated
- The parotid duct may need to be repositioned or ligated; ligation results in initial parotid swelling that subsides as the gland atrophies
- External skin is included with the specimen if involved clinically or on imaging

**Reconstruction:**

- Small T1 defects: primary closure or healing by secondary intention (with vigorous mouth-opening exercises to prevent trismus)
- Moderate defects: buccal fat pad, temporoparietal fascial flap
- Larger defects: radial forearm free flap (RFFF) or anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap; these restore cheek thickness and can be bi-paddled for mucosal and skin surfaces
- RFFF has been shown to give better post-operative mouth opening than split skin graft or buccal fat pad
- T4 tumours requiring segmental mandibulectomy: composite free flap reconstruction (vascularized fibula)

**Neck management:**

- Regional spread is usually to ipsilateral levels I and II
- Unlike oral tongue carcinoma, skip metastases to level IV are unusual
- Elective neck dissection (END) levels I--III for tumours greater than T2 or thickness greater than 5 mm; some institutions perform END at 3--4 mm thickness or if lymphatic infiltration is demonstrated
- Comprehensive neck dissection for palpable or pathological nodes; nodes at the facial artery crossing the mandible should be included

**Post-operative [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]]:** Indicated for 2 or more positive nodes, extracapsular spread, margins less than 3 mm, and Stage III/IV disease. Some authors suggest PORT even for Stage I/II or tumours thicker than 10 mm.

### Management with Mandibular Involvement

The mandible should be considered at risk at any point where tumour contacts bone. Tumours invade the mandible either in an invasive pattern (fingers/islands of tumour advancing deeply) or an erosive pattern (broad advancing front with osteoclast activity). Key principles:

- **Rim (marginal) mandibulectomy:** For T1/T2 tumours with early invasion (<5 mm into bone) and adequate residual mandibular height; maintains bony continuity and preserves sensation of lower lip; associated with excellent functional and cosmetic outcome
- **Segmental mandibulectomy:** Required for extensive mandibular invasion, deeply invading tumours abutting the full depth of mandible, previously irradiated mandible, involvement of the inferior alveolar nerve or intraosseous tumours; 1 cm soft tissue margin should be achieved
- **Reconstruction:** Segmental mandibular resection should be accompanied by composite microvascular free flap (typically fibula free flap) whenever feasible; small lateral defects may use a reconstruction plate with soft tissue flap; central defects or larger lateral defects require vascularized bone

Prognosis following bony resection does not differ between marginal and segmental resection when guidelines are followed. The presence of bone involvement itself is the main prognostic determinant rather than the depth of bone invasion. Local recurrence is usually a consequence of soft tissue margin status rather than the method of mandibular resection.

### Prognosis and Recurrence

- Recurrence rates: 26--80%, usually occurring within 2 years
- Factors influencing recurrence: tumour thickness, margins less than 3 mm, tumour differentiation
- Distant failure is more common than regional failure compared to tongue carcinoma
- Five-year survival varies depending on population and treatment modality

## PART D: Role of MRI in Oral Cancer

MRI is an accurate method for the assessment of oral cavity malignancies and is considered the primary imaging modality for soft tissue staging.

### Strengths of MRI

- **Superior soft tissue contrast:** Best for assessing tumour extent in the tongue, floor of mouth and buccal mucosa
- **Depth of invasion (DOI):** A critical parameter in AJCC 8th Edition staging; MRI accurately measures DOI (9.5 mm depth cut-off for predicting positive nodes in one study)
- **Perineural invasion:** Demonstrates enhancement and thickening of named nerves (inferior alveolar, mental, lingual, infraorbital)
- **Bone marrow invasion:** T1 and STIR fat suppression sequences are very sensitive for detecting bone marrow involvement; a negative MRI can confidently exclude bone marrow invasion
- **Mandibular involvement assessment:** Higher sensitivity than CT for detecting marrow invasion, though CT is superior for cortical bone assessment; MRI sensitivity 97% versus PET-CT specificity 84%

### Techniques

- Axial T1, T2 and STIR sequences are standard
- Placement of a rolled piece of gauze in the oral vestibule before scanning improves visualization of small tumours (as cheek-puffing used with CT is not possible during MRI)
- Gadolinium-enhanced sequences for soft tissue delineation

### Limitations

- Dental amalgam artefact
- Swallowing and motion artefact (longer acquisition times)
- Cannot reliably detect micrometastases in the N0 neck (detectability threshold is below spatial and contrast resolution)

## PART E: Role of PET-CT in Oral Cancer

PET-CT with 18F-FDG combines metabolic information with anatomical detail and has specific indications in oral cancer management.

### Indications and Value

- **Primary tumour detection:** Sensitivity of 96% for detecting the primary tumour when contrast-enhanced CT is combined with PET; open-mouth PET-CT improves tumour localization and extent assessment
- **Advanced disease staging:** Valuable for detecting distant metastases in Stage III/IV disease
- **Unknown primary:** Important role in identifying the primary site in patients presenting with cervical metastasis of unknown origin
- **Post-treatment surveillance:** Should be the imaging modality of choice for detection of residual or recurrent disease; however, a waiting period of at least 12 weeks post-treatment is recommended to reduce false positive findings (CT or MRI should be used if imaging is needed during this waiting period)
- **Prognostic value:** SUV nodal maximum of 5.7 is an independent prognostic indicator for 5-year neck cancer control; a cut-off SUV of 2.5 is associated with greater risk of cervical lymph node metastasis
- **Bone assessment:** PET-CT is more specific than MRI (84% vs 61%) but less sensitive (78% vs 97%) for bone marrow invasion; in patients with positive MRI findings, a negative PET-CT is useful for ruling out bone marrow invasion in dentate patients

### Limitations

- **N0 neck:** Cannot be used to reliably decide on elective neck dissection; sensitivity for occult cervical metastases varies between 50--64% and specificity between 81--97%
- **False positives:** Inflammatory conditions, dental infection, recent biopsy site
- **False negatives:** Small tumours (<1 cm), low-grade malignancies
- **Limited value in early disease:** Not recommended as routine for T1/T2 tumours with clinically negative neck

### Complementary Imaging Strategy

| Modality | Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| MRI | Soft tissue extent, DOI, bone marrow, perineural spread | Dental artefact, motion artefact |
| CT | Cortical bone erosion, dental anatomy | Poor soft tissue contrast |
| PET-CT | Distant metastases, recurrence, unknown primary | False positives, limited in N0 |
| Ultrasound + FNA | Cervical node assessment, depth of primary | Operator dependent |

The combined use of CT, MRI and PET-CT improves sensitivity for mandibular invasion detection to 83.3% without loss of specificity. The optimal approach for comprehensive oral cancer staging is MRI for soft tissue assessment combined with CT for bony evaluation, supplemented by PET-CT for advanced disease, recurrence detection and distant metastatic workup.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The orbicularis oris muscle is the sphincter of the lips, essential for oral competence; its preservation or correct orientation is the primary goal in any lip reconstruction.
> 2. Lymphatic drainage of the central lower lip crosses the midline to bilateral submental and submandibular nodes, necessitating consideration of bilateral neck treatment in lip SCC at this site.
> 3. Squamous cell carcinoma of the lower lip (90--95% of lip SCC) is strongly associated with chronic UV exposure; basal cell carcinoma preferentially affects the upper lip.
> 4. Lip reconstruction follows the rule of thirds: up to one-third direct closure, one-third to one-half Karapandzic or Abbe flap, and greater than one-half Bernard-von Burow advancement or free flap.
> 5. Gingivobuccal sulcus carcinoma is the commonest oral cancer in South East Asia (up to 40%) driven by betel quid (paan/khaini) use; areca nut is independently carcinogenic and causes oral submucous fibrosis.
> 6. Buccal carcinoma spreads regionally to ipsilateral levels I--II; skip metastases to level IV are unusual, unlike oral tongue carcinoma.
> 7. MRI is the primary imaging modality for oral cavity soft tissue staging -- it is superior for assessing depth of invasion, perineural spread and bone marrow involvement.
> 8. PET-CT should be performed at least 12 weeks after radiotherapy completion to reduce false positive findings; CT or MRI is preferred during the earlier post-treatment period.
> 9. Marginal (rim) mandibulectomy should be the resection of choice whenever oncologically sound; segmental mandibulectomy is required for extensive bone invasion and should be accompanied by composite microvascular free flap reconstruction.
> 10. Cross-sectional imaging including PET-CT cannot reliably detect micrometastases and therefore cannot replace elective neck dissection in clinically N0 oral cancer patients.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The Karapandzic flap preserves both sensory and motor nerve supply to the reconstructed lip, making it the technique of choice for maintaining oral competence in defects of one-third to two-thirds of the lower lip.
> - Khaini (tobacco and lime retained in the gingivobuccal sulcus) causes cancer at this precise site -- always document the specific side and type of smokeless tobacco habit when evaluating buccal carcinoma.
> - A radial forearm free flap for buccal reconstruction gives better post-operative mouth opening than a split skin graft or buccal fat pad, which is an important functional consideration in patients already at risk of trismus.
> - A negative MRI can confidently exclude bone marrow invasion of the mandible; in equivocal cases, combining MRI with PET-CT improves diagnostic accuracy in dentate patients.
> - When performing a rim (marginal) mandibulectomy, the anterior and posterior margins should be curved and edges rounded to minimize risk of iatrogenic fracture and bone exposure; if the residual mandibular depth is less than 10 mm, convert to a segmental resection.
> - In lip SCC, always examine for perineural invasion along the mental and infraorbital nerves -- numbness of the lip or upper lip demands imaging and wide resection with consideration of nerve sacrifice.
