## Introduction

Oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs) represent a group of conditions with an increased risk of malignant transformation to squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). The WHO (2005) replaced the older terminology of "premalignant lesion" and "premalignant condition" with the unified term "potentially malignant disorder," acknowledging that not all lesions in these conditions will undergo transformation. Leukoplakia is the most common OPMD, while erythroplakia carries the highest malignant potential. White lesions of the oral mucosa encompass a broad differential diagnosis ranging from benign developmental conditions to potentially malignant and frankly malignant lesions. Oral candidiasis is another important cause of white oral lesions and must be differentiated from true premalignant conditions. Oral lichen planus (OLP), a chronic T-cell mediated mucocutaneous disease, has a low but definite risk of malignant transformation (0.5--2%) and is included among the OPMDs. This answer comprehensively covers the classification of OPMDs, oral lichen planus, the differential diagnosis of white oral lesions, oral candidiasis, and the approach to non-healing palatal ulcers. For [[Oral Submucous Fibrosis|OSMF]], see the dedicated file (Oral_Submucous_Fibrosis.md).

## PART A: Classification of Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders (OPMDs)

### WHO Classification

The WHO (2005) unified framework replaces the older distinction between "premalignant lesions" (morphologically altered tissue with increased cancer risk) and "premalignant conditions" (generalized states with increased cancer risk) with the single term "potentially malignant disorders."

**Table 1: Classification of Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders**

| Category | Disorder | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Lesion | Leukoplakia | White patch; cannot be rubbed off |
| Lesion | Erythroplakia | Red velvety patch; highest risk |
| Lesion | Erythroleukoplakia | Mixed red and white; intermediate |
| Condition | OSMF | Areca nut; fibrosis; trismus |
| Condition | Oral lichen planus | T-cell mediated; 0.5--2% risk |
| Condition | Sideropenic dysphagia | PVS/PBK; iron deficiency |
| Condition | Syphilitic glossitis | Tertiary syphilis |
| Condition | Discoid lupus | Autoimmune; palatal lesions |

Other conditions with malignant potential include xeroderma pigmentosum, epidermolysis bullosa dystrophica, and dyskeratosis congenita.

### Squamous Epithelial Dysplasia

SCC of the oral cavity is well known to arise from epithelial precursor lesions, which may be localized or represent wider areas of "field cancerization." These precursor lesions harbour varying degrees of epithelial dysplasia, defined by the accumulation of architectural and cytological abnormalities. By convention, the WHO recommends that oral dysplasia is assigned mild, moderate and severe grades (with an option for binary high/low grading). Grading is subjective and prone to inter-observer variation, but remains the principal guide to management. A meta-analysis of oral dysplastic lesions indicates a malignant transformation rate of approximately 12%, with a mean time to transformation of 4.3 years.

Histological progression from normal epithelium to hyperplasia, dysplasia and finally invasive carcinoma is related to sequential genetic changes causing genetic instability. Key molecular alterations include LOH of 9p21 (70--80% of HNSCC), as well as LOH 3p, 17p, 11q and 13q. Carcinogens from tobacco (nitrosamines, benz-(a)-pyrene) produce mutations in p53.

### Leukoplakia

Leukoplakia is defined as a white patch or plaque on the oral mucosa that cannot be rubbed off and cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other definable disease. It is the most common OPMD.

**Clinical subtypes:**

- **Homogeneous leukoplakia** — uniformly flat, thin, white patch; lower malignant potential (<5%)
- **Non-homogeneous leukoplakia** — includes speckled (erythroleukoplakia), nodular and verrucous subtypes; higher risk
- **Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL)** — multifocal, progressive, spreading white lesion with very high transformation rate (up to 70% progress to verrucous carcinoma or SCC)

Leukoplakia of the floor of the mouth is considered a premalignant condition with an annual transformation rate of 1--2.9%, and it is not uncommon to find a carcinoma focus within an excised leukoplakic lesion.

**Risk factors for malignant transformation in leukoplakia:**

- Non-homogeneous type (speckled, nodular, verrucous)
- Floor of mouth or ventrolateral tongue location
- Female gender
- Large size (>200 mm squared)
- Presence of epithelial dysplasia (moderate or severe)
- Non-smoker (paradoxically higher transformation in non-smokers)
- Persistence despite cessation of habits

### Erythroplakia

Erythroplakia is a red velvety patch that cannot be attributed to any other condition. It carries the highest malignant potential of all OPMDs. At the time of initial biopsy, 14--50% of erythroplakic lesions already contain invasive carcinoma or carcinoma in situ. All erythroplakic lesions demand urgent biopsy and definitive management.

### Management of OPMDs

**Step 1 — Risk factor elimination:**
- Cessation of tobacco (smoking and smokeless), alcohol and betel/paan use
- Address haematinic deficiencies (iron, folate, vitamin B12)

**Step 2 — Biopsy:**
- All OPMDs require incisional biopsy, particularly non-homogeneous lesions
- Multiple biopsies for large or multifocal lesions
- Include the most clinically suspicious area (reddest, most raised, or most indurated)

**Step 3 — Management based on dysplasia grade:**

| Dysplasia Grade | Management Approach |
|---|---|
| No dysplasia | Observation; eliminate risk factors |
| Mild dysplasia | Observation with close follow-up |
| Moderate dysplasia | Excision or close surveillance |
| Severe dysplasia / CIS | Excision (laser or cold steel) |

**Step 4 — Long-term surveillance:**
- Field cancerization means new lesions can develop at other sites in previously exposed mucosa
- Long-term follow-up is mandatory irrespective of treatment
- No proven chemoprevention despite trials with retinoids, vitamin A and beta-carotene

## PART B: Oral Lichen Planus (OLP) — Features and Management

### Pathogenesis

Oral lichen planus is a very common oral condition. It is a T-cell mediated attack on the basement membrane and basal cell layer of the skin or mucosa. This causes a reactive increase in epithelial width (acanthosis — producing a white appearance) or a reduction in epithelial cell production with consequent thinning (atrophy/ulceration — producing a red appearance). Both can be present simultaneously in the same lesion. About 30% of patients have both oral and cutaneous lesions.

### Clinical Features

**Oral manifestations:**
- Most common presentation: bilateral reticular pattern on buccal mucosa (lacy white streaks — Wickham's striae)
- Common sites: lateral aspects of the tongue and buccal mucosa
- Most frequently asymptomatic and seen as an incidental finding
- Aggressive forms: atrophic and erosive variants causing discomfort with spicy and flavoured foods
- Lesions tend to be intermittent and recurrent; they recur in the same places (unlike aphthae which vary in position)

**Six clinical types (WHO classification):**
1. Reticular — commonest; white lace-like network
2. Papular — small white raised dots
3. Plaque-like — resembles leukoplakia
4. Atrophic — red, thinned mucosa
5. Erosive/ulcerative — painful; most symptomatic
6. Bullous — rare; vesicles/bullae

**Cutaneous manifestations (Six Ps):**
- Pruritic, purple, polygonal, planar, papules, plaques
- Itchy purple spots on wrists, legs and trunk

**Desquamative gingivitis:** OLP (along with pemphigus and pemphigoid) can present as desquamative gingivitis — red, sore, peeling gingivae; biopsy determines the underlying condition.

### Histopathology

- Saw-tooth (irregular) rete ridge pattern
- Civatte bodies (apoptotic keratinocytes in the basal layer)
- Band-like (lichenoid) T-lymphocyte infiltrate at the dermo-epidermal junction
- Degeneration of the basal cell layer

### Malignant Potential

OLP has a low potential for malignant transformation (0.5--2%). The WHO considers it a potentially malignant disorder. The individual risk for a lichenoid lesion is not clear. Patients must be made aware of the possibility of malignancy so that regular monitoring can take place.

### Lichenoid Reactions — Differential Diagnosis

Lichenoid reactions are lesions that mimic OLP clinically but are triggered by identifiable causes. The lesions on the mucosa are essentially the same as those seen in LP, but they are distinct on biopsy.

**Causes of lichenoid reactions:**
- **Dental materials** — amalgam or other dental restorations adjacent to the lesion
- **Drugs** — ACE inhibitors and beta-adrenergic blockers are the commonest triggers; also disease-modifying drugs (gold, penicillamine, sulphasalazine)
- **Graft versus host disease** — similar to widespread lichenoid reaction after allogeneic bone marrow transplant
- **Hepatitis C** — associated in some populations

Discontinuing the offending medicine gradually results in improvement. Angiotensin-2 receptor blockers do not give this reaction and are a good alternative where an ACE inhibitor is suspected.

### Management of OLP

Management follows the same algorithmic approach used for immunobullous conditions:

**First-line — Topical corticosteroids:**
- Betamethasone mouthwash (1 mg dissolved in 10 ml water, rinse for 2 minutes twice daily)
- Beclomethasone inhaler (2 puffs three times daily directly onto lesion)
- Topical clobetasol cream/ointment for stubborn localized lesions

**Second-line — Topical calcineurin inhibitors:**
- Topical tacrolimus cream or ointment for refractory localized lesions
- Tacrolimus as a mouth rinse — useful bridge between topical steroids and systemic medication for widespread lichenoid lesions
- Topical pimecrolimus as an alternative
- Note: tacrolimus is ineffective against pemphigus and pemphigoid (antibody-driven, not T-cell driven)

**Third-line — Systemic [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|immunotherapy]]:**
- Systemic corticosteroids for severe erosive disease
- Azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil for refractory cases
- Systemic immunotherapy should only be used by a specialist with appropriate experience

**Supportive measures:**
- Pain management (benzydamine mouthwash, lignocaine gel)
- Oral hygiene maintenance
- Eliminate contributing factors (replace dental amalgam if lichenoid reaction suspected)
- Correct haematinic deficiencies (iron, folate, B12) which exacerbate lichenoid lesions

**Surveillance:**
- Regular follow-up for malignant transformation (0.5--2% risk)
- Biopsy any area showing change in character (new ulceration, induration, rapid growth)

## PART C: White Lesions of Oral Mucosa — Differential Diagnosis

White lesions of the oral mucosa are most commonly caused by thickening of the epithelium (acanthosis or hyperkeratosis) although reduced connective tissue blood flow can also produce white lesions (as in submucous fibrosis). White lesions are more frequent than red lesions but are less commonly associated with dysplasia.

**Table 2: Classification of White Oral Lesions**

| Category | Local Causes | Systemic Causes |
|---|---|---|
| Keratotic | Frictional keratosis | Lichen planus |
| Keratotic | Smoker's keratosis | Lupus erythematosus |
| Infective | Burns (chemical/thermal) | Candidiasis |
| Developmental | Idiopathic keratosis | White sponge naevus |
| Neoplastic | Carcinoma (early SCC) | Papillomas (some) |
| Other | Skin grafts | Syphilitic keratosis |

### Key Diagnostic Feature: Can the White Lesion Be Rubbed Off?

- **YES** (removable) — pseudomembranous candidiasis (thrush), necrotic slough, chemical burn, materia alba
- **NO** (non-removable) — leukoplakia, lichen planus, frictional keratosis, white sponge naevus, chronic hyperplastic candidiasis, hairy leukoplakia, lupus erythematosus

### Important Individual Conditions

**White sponge naevus:** A dominant-inherited developmental condition causing characteristic epithelial changes. It usually starts in childhood at the posterior buccal mucosa and spreads slowly anteriorly over years, giving a "shaggy" white lesion covering much of the buccal and palatal mucosa, including areas not subject to dental trauma (such as the buccal sulcus). It is symptomless and requires no treatment.

**Hairy leukoplakia:** Caused by persistence of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). Characteristically found on the lateral border of the tongue as corrugated white plaques that cannot be rubbed off. It raises the suspicion of immunodeficiency and is associated with HIV infection. It is not a premalignant lesion.

**Frictional keratosis:** White patch caused by chronic mechanical irritation (sharp tooth, denture flange, habitual cheek biting/morsicatio buccarum). Resolves when the source of irritation is removed.

**Smoker's keratosis:** Diffuse white change of the palatal mucosa in pipe and cigar smokers (nicotinic stomatitis). Cessation of smoking leads to resolution.

### Approach to a White Lesion

1. Take a detailed history (tobacco, alcohol, betel use; drug history; dental history; duration)
2. Assess whether the lesion can be rubbed off
3. Assess the clinical pattern (reticular = OLP; homogeneous flat white = leukoplakia; corrugated lateral tongue = hairy leukoplakia)
4. Identify the site (floor of mouth and ventrolateral tongue lesions carry the highest malignant risk)
5. Biopsy all lesions that cannot be attributed to an identifiable and removable cause
6. If candidiasis is suspected — treat first, then re-evaluate and biopsy if persistent

## PART D: Oral Candidiasis — Classification and Management

### Mycology

Candida albicans is the most common fungal organism found in the mouth, although other species (C. krusei, C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. dubliniensis) can be found. These non-albicans species tend to be more resistant to common antifungal agents. Candida species are commensals in many individuals; a positive swab denotes candidial carriage, not infection. Quantitative fungal assays can be used to demonstrate high levels where necessary.

### Classification of Oral Candidiasis

**Table 3: Types of Oral Candidiasis**

| Type | Predisposing Factors |
|---|---|
| Acute pseudomembranous (thrush) | Immunosuppression; antibiotics |
| Acute atrophic (antibiotic sore mouth) | Broad-spectrum antibiotics |
| Chronic erythematous | None specific; aging |
| Chronic atrophic (denture-related) | Denture wearing (esp. at night) |
| Chronic hyperplastic (CHC) | Tobacco smoking |
| Median rhomboid glossitis | Smoking; dentures; immune defects |
| Angular cheilitis | Dentures; diabetes; haematinic def. |
| Chronic mucocutaneous | Immune defect; endocrinopathy |

### Individual Types — Key Features

**Acute pseudomembranous candidiasis (thrush):**
White patches that can be rubbed off leaving an erythematous, raw base. Complicates systemic, topical or inhaled corticosteroid therapy and antibiotic therapy. Produces widespread erythema and soreness of the mouth and pharynx. The clinician must remember that not every white lesion should be assumed to be candidiasis.

**Denture-related stomatitis (chronic atrophic candidiasis):**
The most common form of mild, chronic, oral candidiasis. It occurs only beneath a denture or appliance and is often not sore. Dentures worn through the night or with a dry mouth favour this infection. Managed exclusively by proper denture hygiene including removing the denture at night and cleaning it with an appropriate antiseptic. There is no need for a topical antifungal except in the immunocompromised. If untreated, papillary hyperplasia of the underlying tissues may develop.

**Chronic hyperplastic candidiasis (CHC):**
A raised white lesion on the oral mucosa (typically at the commissures) that cannot be rubbed off. CHC has been considered potentially malignant but the link with oral cancer is now less clear. It is associated with candida invasion into the tissues. Histologically, the picture is often that of high-grade dysplasia, but this is a reactive change to the presence of candidal hyphae in the tissues. When systemic antifungals are given, the dysplastic appearance settles. Where dysplasia is reported in CHC, the patient must be given a systemic antifungal and the tissue re-biopsied to ensure the changes resolve. The clinical picture usually returns in time and may need repeated systemic antifungal courses.

**Angular cheilitis:**
Bilateral inflammation at the angles of the mouth. Mechanical factors (overclosed bite in edentulous patients, denture problems) and nutritional deficiencies (iron, folate, B12) predispose. Associated with orofacial granulomatosis, diabetes and HIV. Candida species or staphylococci are isolated from most patients. Topical miconazole treats both organisms. Miconazole with hydrocortisone combination allows quicker resolution and reduces recurrence.

**Median rhomboid glossitis:**
Red, depapillated, smooth, rhomboidal area on the centre of the tongue dorsum. Associated with an invasive but non-pathogenic candidiasis. Smoking, denture wearing and immune defects (HIV, diabetes) predispose. Usually asymptomatic; no treatment required. A "kissing" lesion may be present on the palatal vault.

### Management of Oral Candidiasis

**Topical antifungals:**
- Nystatin suspension (rinse and swallow)
- Miconazole oral gel
- Amphotericin B lozenges

**Systemic antifungals:**
- Fluconazole — first-line for moderate-to-severe or chronic hyperplastic candidiasis
- Itraconazole — for refractory or fluconazole-resistant cases

**Address predisposing factors:**
- Denture hygiene — remove dentures at night, clean properly, treat with antiseptic
- Control of diabetes mellitus
- Correct haematinic deficiencies (iron, folate, B12)
- Reduce immunosuppression where possible
- Patients on inhaled corticosteroids should rinse the mouth after use

**Important principles:**
- Positive oral swab for Candida does not equal infection — it may represent carriage
- Not every white lesion is candidiasis — always consider the broader differential
- CHC requires systemic (not topical) antifungals and re-biopsy after treatment
- Non-albicans species are increasingly prevalent and are more resistant to common antifungals

## PART E: Non-Healing Ulcer of the Hard Palate — Differential Diagnosis

A non-healing ulcer of the hard palate (present for more than 2 weeks) demands investigation to exclude malignancy. The palate is a unique site where minor salivary gland tumours, mucosal malignancies and various infective/inflammatory conditions converge.

### Differential Diagnosis

**Malignant:**
- Squamous cell carcinoma
- Minor salivary gland carcinoma — adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC) is the commonest minor salivary gland malignancy at this site; mucoepidermoid carcinoma is also common
- Lymphoma (including extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma — midline destructive)
- Melanoma (rare primary oral site)
- Kaposi sarcoma (HIV-associated)

**Infective:**
- Tuberculosis (chronic granulomatous ulcer)
- Syphilis (tertiary gumma with palatal perforation)
- Deep fungal infections — [[Invasive Fungal Rhinosinusitis|mucormycosis]] (rhinocerebral type causes palatal necrosis, particularly in immunocompromised and diabetic patients), histoplasmosis, aspergillosis (aspergilloma causing palatal necrosis)
- Oral candidiasis (erosive forms)

**Inflammatory/Autoimmune:**
- Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA/Wegener's) — midline granulomatous disease
- Extranodal NK/T-cell lymphoma (nasal type) — lethal midline granuloma
- Erosive lichen planus (atypical palatal location raises suspicion of lupus)
- Discoid lupus erythematosus — distinct from OLP on biopsy; found in sites unusual for LP, such as the palate

**Traumatic/Iatrogenic:**
- Denture ulcer — chronic irritation from ill-fitting denture
- Chemical or thermal burn
- Post-radiation mucositis

**Necrotizing sialometaplasia:**
This is an uncommon but important benign disorder that mimics malignancy clinically and sometimes histologically. It typically affects the minor salivary glands of the palate. It presents as a palatal swelling that breaks down to give localized, irregular ulceration. Lesions can be large (up to 5 cm) and may cause local paraesthesia or anaesthesia. Systemic features (pyrexia, malaise) may be present. It is thought to arise from local ischaemia, possibly caused by direct trauma (denture, dental local anaesthetic injection), recreational cocaine use, [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], alcohol or tobacco smoking. It is self-limiting and generally heals within 4--90 days. Despite the extensive ulceration, bony involvement is rare. Biopsy is essential to differentiate from malignancy, particularly SCC and minor salivary gland carcinoma. No surgical intervention is required.

**Cocaine-induced midline destructive lesion (CIMDL):**
Progressive destruction of nasal septum, palate and midline structures due to chronic intranasal cocaine use. Can cause palatal perforation. Must be differentiated from GPA and ENKTCL.

### Approach to Investigation

1. Thorough history — duration, smoking, denture use, immunosuppression, drug/substance use
2. Examination — size, induration, fixity, dental state, neck palpation
3. Incisional biopsy — essential; must be of adequate depth
4. If necrotizing sialometaplasia is suspected — allow 6--8 weeks for spontaneous healing; re-biopsy if no resolution
5. Imaging (CT/MRI) for deep or extensive lesions to assess bone involvement and extent
6. Blood investigations — HIV serology, syphilis serology, autoimmune screen, haematinics as indicated

> 1. The WHO (2005) replaced "premalignant lesion" and "premalignant condition" with the unified term "potentially malignant disorder" (OPMD) to encompass all conditions with increased risk of oral SCC.

>

>
> 7. White oral lesions that can be rubbed off suggest pseudomembranous candidiasis, whereas those that cannot be rubbed off point to leukoplakia, lichen planus, frictional keratosis or white sponge naevus.
>
> 8. Chronic hyperplastic candidiasis (CHC) shows histological dysplasia that is reactive to candidal hyphae invasion; systemic antifungals settle the dysplastic appearance and re-biopsy is mandatory to confirm resolution.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Not every white lesion is candidiasis** — always consider the full differential before initiating antifungal treatment; biopsy persistent lesions after candida treatment to exclude dysplasia.
>
> - **Floor of mouth leukoplakia** is the most dangerous subsite for leukoplakia, with an annual transformation rate of 1--2.9%; a carcinoma focus is not uncommon within excised floor-of-mouth leukoplakic lesions.
>
> - **OLP versus aphthae** — LP lesions recur in the same places repeatedly, whereas aphthous ulcers vary in position; this distinction is clinically useful when patients present with recurrent oral soreness.
>
> - **Tacrolimus is effective for T-cell mediated conditions** (OLP, lichenoid reactions) but ineffective for antibody-driven conditions (pemphigus, pemphigoid) — understanding the immunological mechanism guides topical therapy selection.
>
> - **CHC management requires systemic antifungals** — topical agents are insufficient because candidal hyphae invade the tissues; always re-biopsy after antifungal treatment to confirm resolution of the reactive dysplasia.
>
> - **Necrotizing sialometaplasia must not be resected** — awareness of this self-limiting entity prevents unnecessary surgery; if a palatal ulcer shows benign histology, allow 6--8 weeks for spontaneous healing before re-biopsy.
