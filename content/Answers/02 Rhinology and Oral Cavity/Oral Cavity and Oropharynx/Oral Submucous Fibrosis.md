# Oral Submucous Fibrosis

> [!question] Questions
> Oral submucous fibrosis — aetiopathogenesis, medical and surgical management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 2 (Aetiology of Head and Neck Cancer), Chapter 12 (Oral Cavity Tumours), Chapter 26 (Head and Neck Pathology) and Chapter 42 (Benign Diseases of the Oral Cavity)

---

## Introduction

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) is a chronic, progressive, premalignant condition characterized by fibrosis of the oral mucosa, resulting in marked rigidity and an inability to open the mouth. It is a recognized oral potentially malignant disorder. OSMF is strongly associated with the chewing of betel quid (paan) and areca nut, and is particularly common in the Indian subcontinent and South-East Asia where these habits are prevalent. The condition causes significant functional impairment including trismus, dysphagia and speech difficulty. OSMF is listed as a contraindication for transoral surgical approaches (TLM) to oropharyngeal tumours due to the severe limitation of mouth opening it produces.

---

## PART A: Aetiopathogenesis

### Aetiological Factors

**Areca nut (betel nut)** is the main aetiological agent in oral submucous fibrosis. In the Indian subcontinent and parts of South-East Asia, areca nut is consumed as a component of betel quid (paan). The ingredients of paan vary throughout South-East Asia but the main components include:

| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| Piper betel leaf | Wrapper; contains phenolic compounds |
| Areca nut (Areca catechu) | Main aetiological agent in OSMF |
| Slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) | Lowers pH, accelerates alkaloid release |
| Tobacco | Additional carcinogen |
| Spices and flavourings | Variable additions |

The slaked lime lowers the pH, which accelerates the release of alkaloids from both the tobacco and areca nut. For many years, the tobacco content alone was credited as the carcinogenic agent in betel quid; however, it is now recognized that the areca nut is also carcinogenic, as well as being the main aetiological agent in OSMF. Paan is strongly associated with OSMF as a premalignant lesion. Chewing paan correlates with alveolobuccal cancer. Individuals who consume betel quid frequently have a preference regarding which side they chew, and this corresponds to the side of tumour development.

### Pathogenesis

The pathogenesis of OSMF involves progressive fibrosis of the oral submucosa leading to reduced connective tissue blood flow and mucosal rigidity. The areca nut contains alkaloids (primarily arecoline and arecaidine) which stimulate fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. The key pathological processes include:

- **Excessive collagen deposition** — areca nut alkaloids stimulate fibroblasts to produce increased amounts of collagen (particularly type I collagen) while simultaneously reducing collagen degradation
- **Reduced connective tissue blood flow** — progressive fibrosis causes ischaemia of the oral mucosa, resulting in mucosal blanching and epithelial atrophy
- **Muscle fibrosis** — fibrosis extends into the underlying muscles (buccinator, muscles of mastication, muscles of the soft palate), producing trismus and restricted palatal movement
- **Epithelial atrophy** — reduced vascularity leads to thinning of the epithelium, which becomes more susceptible to the effects of carcinogens

The molecular changes associated with OSMF and progression to malignancy follow the general model for head and neck carcinogenesis: sequential inactivation of tumour suppressor genes and activation of proto-oncogenes. Genetic changes include loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of 9p21 (seen in 70–80% of HNSCC), as well as LOH 3p, 17p, 11q and 13q. Mutations in p53 are associated with carcinogen exposure.

### Premalignant Potential

OSMF is a recognized oral potentially malignant disorder. The widespread chewing of betel nut and paan causes both OSMF and oral cavity SCC, and OSMF itself carries a significant risk of malignant transformation. Oral submucous fibrosis and lichen planus may both involve the buccal mucosa, and both are considered premalignant conditions. The malignant transformation rate for oral dysplastic lesions is approximately 12%, with a mean time to transformation of 4.3 years.

Patients who chew betel often have areas of erythroleukoplakia of the buccal mucosa or submucous fibrosis and consequent trismus, making the detection of invasive squamous cell carcinoma difficult. This highlights the importance of regular surveillance and biopsy of suspicious lesions in patients with established OSMF.

## PART B: Clinical Features and Classification

### Clinical Features

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| Trismus | Progressive limitation of mouth opening |
| Mucosal blanching | Pale, marble-like appearance of oral mucosa |
| Burning sensation | Pain on eating spicy food |
| Palpable fibrous bands | Vertical bands in buccal mucosa, lips, soft palate |
| Vesicle formation | May be an early feature |
| Xerostomia | Due to fibrosis of minor salivary glands |
| Referred otalgia | From involvement of pterygoid muscles |
| Nasal voice | Fibrosis of soft palate restricts velopharyngeal closure |

### Clinical Grading

| Grade | Interincisal Distance | Clinical Features |
|---|---|---|
| Grade I (Early) | >35 mm | Stomatitis, burning sensation, blanching |
| Grade II (Moderate) | 20–35 mm | Palpable fibrous bands, restricted tongue mobility |
| Grade III (Severe) | <20 mm | Thick, palpable bands, severe trismus, dysphagia |

### Sites of Involvement

The buccal mucosa is the most commonly affected site, followed by the soft palate, lips, tongue and floor of mouth. Fibrosis may extend to involve the pharynx and upper oesophagus in advanced cases.

---

## PART C: Diagnosis

- **Clinical examination** — trismus, palpable fibrous bands, blanching of oral mucosa, restricted tongue protrusion; in patients with concurrent betel chewing, erythroleukoplakia may coexist
- **Biopsy** — essential to confirm diagnosis and exclude malignancy; histopathology shows epithelial atrophy, dense submucosal fibrosis with hyalinized collagen, reduced vascularity and chronic inflammatory cell infiltrate
- **Assessment of mouth opening** — interincisal distance measured in millimetres; serial measurements document progression or response to treatment
- **Imaging** — MRI may be useful to assess the extent of fibrosis, particularly involvement of muscles of mastication and parapharyngeal extension

Any oral ulcer or suspicious mucosal change in a patient with OSMF must be biopsied to exclude malignant transformation.

---

## PART D: Medical Management

Medical management is the mainstay for early and moderate OSMF (Grades I and II).

### Habit Cessation

Cessation of areca nut and betel quid use is the single most important intervention. Without habit cessation, all other treatments are of limited value. Patient education regarding the premalignant nature of the condition is essential.

### Pharmacological Treatment

- **Intralesional corticosteroids** — triamcinolone acetonide or dexamethasone injected into fibrous bands; reduces inflammation and may soften fibrous tissue; often combined with hyaluronidase
- **Intralesional hyaluronidase** — breaks down hyaluronic acid in the ground substance, potentially facilitating drug penetration and reducing fibrosis
- **Topical corticosteroids** — for symptomatic relief of burning sensation and stomatitis
- **Nutritional supplementation** — iron, B-complex vitamins, zinc, antioxidants (vitamin A, C, E); addresses nutritional deficiencies common in these patients
- **Pentoxifylline** — a methylxanthine derivative that improves microcirculation and may reduce fibrosis
- **Colchicine** — inhibits collagen synthesis and may be used as adjunctive therapy
- **Lycopene** — an antioxidant carotenoid that has shown benefit in reducing symptoms and improving mouth opening

### Physiotherapy

- **Mouth-opening exercises** — vigorous mouth-opening exercises must be performed regularly to prevent progression of trismus; various devices (e.g. tongue depressors, Therabite jaw exerciser) may assist
- **Heat therapy** — local heat application prior to exercises may improve tissue extensibility

---

## PART E: Surgical Management

Surgical intervention is reserved for moderate to severe OSMF (Grade II–III) that has failed to respond to medical management, or when mouth opening is severely compromised.

### Surgical Options

**1. Fibrotomy / Release of fibrous bands**
- Excision or incision of palpable fibrous bands in the buccal mucosa
- May be performed with electrocautery, laser or cold knife
- Must be followed by aggressive physiotherapy and mouth-opening exercises to prevent recurrence
- Defect requires coverage with a graft or flap to prevent re-fibrosis

**2. Coronoidectomy**
- Indicated when trismus persists despite release of mucosal fibrous bands
- Suggests fibrosis has extended to the muscles of mastication (temporalis tendon) or the coronoid process is mechanically impinging
- May be performed bilaterally if required

**3. Reconstruction of surgical defect**

| Option | Indication | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Buccal fat pad flap | Moderate defects | Pedicled; reliable vascularity; good results |
| Split-thickness skin graft | After fibrotomy | Stabilized with silicone sheets; trismus risk |
| Nasolabial flap | Moderate defects | Pedicled; reliable; cosmetically acceptable |
| Radial free forearm flap | Extensive defects | Thin, pliable; for severe bilateral disease |
| Anterolateral thigh flap | Large defects | Bulky; for extensive defects |

Split-thickness skin grafts may be used following fibrotomy, with the aid of silicone sheets to stabilize the graft. However, the use of a skin graft to reconstruct deeper resections may leave a very thin cheek with potentially poor aesthetics. Local flaps such as the buccal fat pad or temporoparietal fascial flap may be used for reconstruction if tumour extension does not compromise their use.

**4. Microvascular free-flap reconstruction**
- Reserved for severe, extensive OSMF or when OSMF coexists with malignancy requiring composite resection
- Radial free forearm flap or anterolateral thigh flap restores the thickness of the cheek
- If external skin is involved, the flaps can be bi-paddled to provide reconstruction of mucosal and skin surfaces

### Post-operative Care

Post-operative vigorous mouth-opening exercises are essential following any surgical intervention. Without sustained physiotherapy, recurrence of trismus is almost inevitable. Patients must be counselled that lifelong exercises and surveillance are required.

---

## PART F: OSMF and Malignancy — Surgical Implications

When oral malignancy develops in the setting of OSMF, management is complicated by the trismus:

- OSMF is a listed contraindication for transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) and transoral robotic surgery (TORS) due to limited mouth opening
- Mandibulotomy (lip-split with paramedian osteotomy) or visor approach may be required to achieve adequate surgical access
- Lip-split incisions (upper or lower) may be required to facilitate access, particularly in patients with concurrent oral submucous fibrosis
- Buccal carcinoma in the setting of OSMF often presents at an advanced stage because the fibrosis and trismus make detection of invasive carcinoma difficult
- In South-East Asian populations, the male-to-female ratio for buccal carcinoma reflects the consumption of betel quid — in Taiwan, where betel quid users are primarily male, the ratio is as high as 27:1

> 1. Oral submucous fibrosis is a recognized oral potentially malignant disorder — the areca nut (not tobacco alone) is the main aetiological agent
> 2. Paan (betel quid) contains Piper betel leaf, slaked lime, areca nut and tobacco — the slaked lime lowers pH and accelerates release of alkaloids from areca nut and tobacco
> 3. Areca nut alkaloids stimulate fibroblast proliferation and excessive collagen deposition while reducing collagen degradation, leading to progressive submucosal fibrosis
> 4. Reduced connective tissue blood flow from fibrosis causes mucosal blanching and epithelial atrophy — explaining why OSMF appears as a white lesion of the oral mucosa
> 5. OSMF and lichen planus are both considered premalignant conditions that may involve the buccal mucosa — the malignant transformation rate for oral dysplastic lesions is approximately 12%
> 6. Patients with OSMF and concurrent betel chewing habit often have areas of erythroleukoplakia, making detection of early invasive SCC difficult — regular surveillance and biopsy of suspicious lesions is essential

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Betel quid chewers frequently have a preferred side for chewing — this corresponds to the side of tumour development, demonstrating the dose-dependent, site-specific nature of the carcinogenic exposure
> - In buccal carcinoma associated with OSMF, over 40% of patients present with Stage III/IV disease because trismus makes clinical detection of early invasive carcinoma difficult
> - Split-thickness skin grafts after fibrotomy should be stabilized with silicone sheets — but may leave a thin cheek with poor aesthetics; buccal fat pad flap gives better results for moderate defects
> - The molecular progression from OSMF to malignancy follows the stepwise model: LOH 9p21, LOH 3p, 17p → p53 mutation → invasive carcinoma
> - When OSMF coexists with malignancy requiring mandibular resection, composite free-flap reconstruction (radial forearm or ALT flap) with bi-paddled skin paddles can reconstruct both mucosal and external skin surfaces
