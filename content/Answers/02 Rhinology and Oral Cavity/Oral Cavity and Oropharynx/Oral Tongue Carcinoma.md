# Oral Tongue Carcinoma

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - What is the differential diagnosis of an ulcer on the lateral border of the tongue? Discuss the role of [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] in T1/T2 oral cavity malignancy. Describe the TNM staging of oral cavity carcinoma (AJCC 8th Edition). How would you manage the N0 neck in oral cavity malignancy? Discuss the clinical features, diagnosis and management of T3N1M0 carcinoma of the right anterolateral tongue.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 12 (Oral Cavity Tumours Including Lip Reconstruction)

---

## Introduction

The oral tongue is one of the commonest sites for oral cancer, accounting for 22–39% of oral cavity carcinomas. Over 90% of tumours of the oral cavity are squamous cell carcinomas. Globally, over 300,000 people are diagnosed with oral cancer each year, making it the eighth most common malignancy worldwide. The incidence has risen by over 20% in the last 30 years in England and Wales, particularly in those under 65 years. Tobacco and alcohol consumption are the major aetiological factors and oral cancer is considered largely preventable. The oral tongue is the freely mobile anterior two-thirds of the tongue, demarcated from the base of tongue by the circumvallate papillae. Within this site, most tumours occur in the middle third, commonly on the lateral aspect, followed by the ventral surface. Only 4–5% of tongue carcinomas occur on the dorsum. If oral cancer is detected when confined to the oral mucosa, 5-year survival rates exceed 80%, decreasing to 40% for regional disease and 20% if distant metastasis has occurred.

## PART A: Differential Diagnosis of an Ulcer on the Lateral Border of the Tongue

### Malignant Causes

- **Squamous cell carcinoma** — the commonest malignancy; lateral border is the most frequent site; associated with tobacco, alcohol, betel quid use
- **Minor salivary gland tumours** — adenoid cystic carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma
- **Lymphoma** — may present as an ulcerative mass, particularly at the tongue base

### Premalignant Conditions

- **Leukoplakia** — white patch that cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other disease; annual malignant transformation rate of 1–2.9% for floor of mouth leukoplakia
- **Erythroplakia** — red velvety patch; higher malignant potential than leukoplakia
- **[[Oral Submucous Fibrosis|Oral submucous fibrosis]]** — associated with areca nut in betel quid; recognized oral potentially malignant disorder

### Infective Causes

- **Syphilitic chancre** (primary syphilis) — painless indurated ulcer
- **Tuberculous ulcer** — undermined edges, painful; usually secondary to pulmonary TB
- **Fungal infection** (deep mycoses) — histoplasmosis, blastomycosis

### Inflammatory/Autoimmune Causes

- **Aphthous ulceration** — minor, major or herpetiform; recurrent and painful
- **Lichen planus** — erosive type may present as chronic ulceration
- **Behcet's disease** — oral ulceration with systemic manifestations

### Traumatic Causes

- **Traumatic ulcer** — sharp tooth, ill-fitting denture, habitual cheek/tongue biting
- **Chemical or thermal burn**
- **Radiation mucositis**

### Other Causes

- **Blood dyscrasias** — agranulocytosis, leukaemia, aplastic anaemia
- **Drug-related ulceration** — methotrexate, nicorandil

> **Clinical Pearl:** Any non-healing ulcer on the tongue persisting for more than 3 weeks should be biopsied to exclude malignancy, particularly on the lateral border in patients with risk factors.

## PART B: Surgical Anatomy of the Oral Tongue

The oral tongue may be subdivided into the tip, dorsum, lateral borders and ventral surface. The ventral and lateral surfaces are lined by non-keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium (lining mucosa), while the dorsum and tip are lined by specialized gustatory mucosa with thick, primarily keratinized epithelium. The mucosa overlies the intrinsic muscles and four paired extrinsic muscles: genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus and palatoglossus.

**Motor innervation** is via the hypoglossal nerve to all tongue muscles, except palatoglossus which is supplied by the vagus nerve. **Sensation** is supplied by the lingual nerve (branch of mandibular division of trigeminal). **Taste** fibres of the oral tongue travel with the lingual nerve before passing to the chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve.

**Lymphatic drainage** of the lateral borders is to ipsilateral cervical nodes; however, drainage of the midline, tip and base of tongue occurs bilaterally. Up to 4.5% may have occult cervical disease in the contralateral neck. **Blood supply** is by the paired lingual arteries, the third branch of the external carotid artery. During resection of posterior tongue lesions, the contralateral vascular pedicle should be preserved if the tongue tip is to be maintained. Sacrifice of both hypoglossal nerves results in a non-functioning tongue tip.

## PART C: TNM Staging — AJCC 8th Edition Changes for Oral Cavity

The AJCC 8th Edition introduced significant changes to oral cavity staging.

### T Stage Changes

The key change is the replacement of tumour thickness by **depth of invasion (DOI)** as DOI has been shown to be a better prognosticator. Three DOI groups have been defined: ≤5 mm, >5 mm but ≤10 mm, and >10 mm. Each incremental increase in DOI results in an increase in T stage. Additionally, 'extrinsic muscle infiltration' is no longer a staging criterion for T4.

| T Stage | Size Criteria | DOI Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| T1 | ≤2 cm | ≤5 mm DOI |
| T2 | ≤2 cm with DOI >5–10 mm OR >2–4 cm | ≤10 mm DOI |
| T3 | >4 cm | OR any tumour >10 mm DOI |
| T4a | Moderate advanced local disease | Cortical bone, skin, etc. |
| T4b | Very advanced local disease | Masticator space, pterygoids, skull base, ICA |

- Clinical DOI (cT) relies on clinical examination and measured radiological evidence
- Pathological DOI (pT) is measured by a perpendicular dropped from a line drawn at the level of the basement membrane

### N Stage Changes

**Extra-nodal extension (ENE)** has been added as a prognostic variable in addition to the number and size of metastatic lymph nodes.

| N Stage | Criteria |
|---|---|
| N1 | Single ipsilateral node ≤3 cm, ENE(−) |
| N2a | Single ipsilateral node >3–6 cm, ENE(−) |
| N2b | Multiple ipsilateral nodes ≤6 cm, ENE(−) |
| N2c | Bilateral/contralateral nodes ≤6 cm, ENE(−) |
| N3a | Any node >6 cm, ENE(−) |
| N3b | Any node(s) with clinical ENE(+) |

- Radiological evidence alone is insufficient to determine ENE — must be associated with overt clinical evidence of invasion of adjacent structures (e.g. overlying skin or adjacent nerve)
- Pathological ENE is subdivided into minor (<2 mm beyond capsule) and major (>2 mm beyond capsule); this distinction is currently for data collection purposes — either is considered ENE positive

## PART D: Management of the N0 Neck in Oral Cavity Malignancy

The management of the clinically N0 neck is one of the most debated topics in head and neck oncology. The decision to perform elective neck dissection (END) or watchful waiting has been largely settled by the landmark D'Cruz et al. randomized controlled trial.

### Evidence for Elective Neck Dissection

D'Cruz et al. reported on 500 patients with oral cavity SCC (245 in the elective-surgery group and 255 in the therapeutic-surgery group), with a median follow-up of 39 months. Results:

- **Overall survival at 3 years:** END 80.0% vs therapeutic dissection 67.5%
- **Disease-free survival:** END 69.5% vs therapeutic 45.9% (P<0.001)
- **Hazard ratio for death:** 0.64 (95% CI 0.45–0.92; P=0.01) favouring END

### Indications for END

| Factor | Threshold for END |
|---|---|
| Tumour thickness (generic) | >3–4 mm |
| Floor of mouth tumour thickness | >1.5–2 mm (lower critical thickness) |
| Buccal carcinoma thickness | >4–5 mm |
| T stage | T2 or greater |
| Histological features | Poor differentiation, double DNA aneuploidy |

- Biopsy tumour thickness >2 mm predicts final tumour thickness >3.5 mm, suggesting END should be considered
- The increased incidence of nodal metastasis in tongue cancer may be due to contraction of tongue muscle promoting entry of cancer cells into lymphatics
- Thicker tumours have access to wider lymphatics in which tumour emboli can form more readily

### Extent of Neck Dissection

- **Tongue carcinoma:** Levels I–IV (because of skip metastases to level IV)
- **Buccal carcinoma:** Levels I–III
- **Floor of mouth:** Levels I–III/IV
- **Maxillary alveolus/hard palate:** Levels I–III (T2 or greater)
- Level V involvement in the absence of positive nodes in levels I–IV is rare for tongue carcinoma
- Bilateral neck dissections should be considered for tumours extending to or beyond the midline

## PART E: Comprehensive Management of T3N1M0 Right Anterolateral Tongue Carcinoma

### Clinical Features at Presentation

Patients with tongue cancer may present with pain, ulceration or a lump on the tongue. Despite the oral tongue being readily visible, many patients still present with a 4–6 month history of symptoms. A T3N1M0 tumour implies either a tumour >4 cm in maximum dimension OR any tumour with >10 mm depth of invasion, with a single ipsilateral cervical node ≤3 cm without ENE. Clinically positive cervical lymphadenopathy at presentation is in the region of 21–34%.

### Diagnosis and Workup

1. **Clinical examination:** Bimanual palpation of the tongue and floor of mouth; systematic examination of the oral cavity and oropharynx with dental mirrors; flexible endoscopic assessment of the upper aerodigestive tract; palpation of cervical lymph nodes

2. **Biopsy:** Incisional biopsy at the periphery of the lesion including normal mucosa; deep biopsy to assess depth of invasion and invasive front pattern

3. **Imaging:**
   - MRI (primary site) — best for soft-tissue extent, depth of invasion, mandibular marrow involvement
   - CT neck with contrast — cervical lymphadenopathy assessment
   - USS + FNAC of suspicious nodes
   - CT chest — screening for distant metastases (indicated for multiple nodes, advanced disease)
   - OPG — if mandibular involvement suspected
   - [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]] — in advanced disease or suspected distant metastases

4. **Pre-treatment assessment:**
   - Dental assessment and necessary extractions
   - Dietician review, consideration of PEG insertion
   - Allen's test if radial free forearm flap anticipated
   - Anaesthetic assessment for airway management
   - Speech and language therapy assessment
   - MDT discussion for treatment planning

### Surgical Management — Primary Site

Surgical resection with 1 cm margins in three dimensions is the treatment of choice. For a T3 right anterolateral tongue carcinoma:

- **Access:** Per oral approach may be possible for anterior lesions with good retraction and lighting; posteriorly based or large lesions may require mandibulotomy (lip split with paramedian osteotomy) for adequate exposure
- **Resection:** Wide local excision or hemiglossectomy depending on extent; use of ultrasonography to aid assessment of deep margin clearance; compartmental resection approach has been advocated with improved outcomes
- **Margins:** 1 cm surgical margins (accounts for specimen shrinkage, ensuring >5 mm pathological margins); UK RCPath guidelines: >5 mm = clear, 1–5 mm = close, <1 mm = positive; even with apparently adequate margins, 10% of resections may demonstrate histologically positive margins
- **Lingual vessels:** If both lingual vessels are resected, the viability of the tongue tip remnant should be carefully assessed
- **Intraoperative assessment:** Continuous visual and palpation reassessment of margins; adjuncts include intraoperative ultrasonography for deep margins

### Reconstruction

The aim is to ensure maximum function of the residual tongue tissue, since the complex function of the tongue cannot be replicated. Preservation of the tip of the tongue helps maximize post-operative function.

- Small T1/T2 lesions: vertical wedge excision with primary closure (if defect does not significantly involve the floor of mouth)
- **T3 lesions (as in this case):** [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|Free flap]] reconstruction is indicated
  - **Radial free forearm flap** — most commonly used; thin, pliable
  - **Anterolateral thigh (ALT) free flap** — increasingly used
  - The skin paddle should not restrict residual tongue function and should augment swallowing
  - Reconstruction should be same size or slightly smaller than the defect
  - Mobile tongue and floor of mouth should be 'separated' in the reconstruction to minimize restriction
- **Functional measures:** Static laryngeal suspension to the mandible and cricopharyngeal myotomy may improve post-operative function for large resections

### Management of the Neck

For T3N1M0 disease, therapeutic neck dissection is clearly indicated:

- **Ipsilateral neck:** Modified radical neck dissection or selective neck dissection (levels I–IV) — levels I–IV should be dissected because of skip metastases to level IV in tongue carcinoma
- **Contralateral neck:** Consider bilateral neck dissections for tumours extending to or beyond the midline; use of frozen section on ipsilateral nodes has been proposed to identify cases where contralateral dissection may be indicated
- **Lingual lymph nodes:** In-continuity resection with the neck specimen or clearance of tissue above mylohyoid should be conducted
- The neck is accessed for microvascular reconstruction of the primary site, facilitating concurrent neck dissection

### Post-operative [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]] (PORT)

PORT is indicated for this case (T3N1M0 = Stage III/IV disease). Specific indications include:

- Positive or close margins
- Multiple cervical nodes
- Extracapsular spread in the neck
- Stage III/IV disease
- Perineural spread
- Tumours thicker than 9–10 mm (even without other features)

Based on involved margins, ECS or multiple positive nodes, 26–62% of patients receiving surgery as primary treatment may require PORT. Local failure following PORT to tongue tumours has been demonstrated to be higher than comparable floor-of-mouth tumours, and some suggest higher doses of PORT should be considered for tongue tumours.

### Prognosis

- Clinical features remain the most reliable prognostic indicators
- Tumour thickness, perineural invasion, cervical metastasis and margin dysplasia all influence prognosis
- Patients with tumours >9 mm thick: 5-year survival of 66%, compared to 100% for tumours <3 mm thick
- Recurrence rates: 10–50%, usually locoregional, within the first 2 years
- Metachronous second tumours occur in nearly 10% of patients
- Younger patients without usual risk factors may have a poorer long-term prognosis

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The oral tongue accounts for 22–39% of oral cancers, with the lateral border being the commonest subsite — most patients present with Stage I/II disease
> 2. AJCC 8th Edition replaced tumour thickness with depth of invasion (DOI) for T staging of oral cavity cancer — three DOI groups: ≤5 mm, >5–10 mm, >10 mm
> 3. Extra-nodal extension (ENE) has been added to N staging in the AJCC 8th Edition — clinical ENE requires overt evidence of adjacent structure invasion
> 4. D'Cruz et al. RCT demonstrated that elective neck dissection improves overall survival (80% vs 67.5% at 3 years) compared to therapeutic neck dissection in oral cavity SCC
> 5. END should be considered for tumours thicker than 3–4 mm, T2 or greater, or those with poor histological features
> 6. END for tongue carcinoma should include levels I–IV because of skip metastases to level IV
> 7. Floor of mouth tumours have a lower critical thickness (1.5–2 mm) for END compared to other oral sites
> 8. Surgical margins of 1 cm in three dimensions are recommended; UK RCPath: >5 mm clear, 1–5 mm close, <1 mm positive
> 9. PORT is indicated for positive margins, multiple nodes, extracapsular spread, perineural invasion and Stage III/IV disease
> 10. Reconstruction should maximize function of the residual tongue — radial free forearm flap or ALT flap for larger defects

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Any non-healing tongue ulcer persisting for >3 weeks in a patient with risk factors mandates biopsy — do not attribute to trauma and observe
> - Biopsy tumour thickness >2 mm predicts final thickness >3.5 mm, suggesting END should be considered from the outset
> - The contralateral lingual artery must be preserved if the tongue tip is to be maintained — sacrifice of both hypoglossal nerves results in a non-functioning tongue tip
> - Tongue muscle contraction may promote entry of cancer cells into lymphatics, explaining the relatively high incidence of occult metastasis (up to 53%) in tongue cancer
> - Tongue cancer in younger patients (<40 years) without traditional risk factors is increasingly recognized and may carry a poorer long-term prognosis — though this does not necessarily translate to decreased survival
> - When mandibulotomy is required for access, the paramedian osteotomy (between lateral incisor and canine) is preferred — plates should be adapted before osteotomy to ensure accurate realignment
