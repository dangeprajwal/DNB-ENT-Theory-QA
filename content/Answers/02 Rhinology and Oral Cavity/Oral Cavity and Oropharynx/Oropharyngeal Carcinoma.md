# Oropharyngeal Carcinoma

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Describe the surgical anatomy of the base of tongue. Discuss the management of cancer of the base of tongue. What is the role of HPV in oropharyngeal carcinoma? Describe the TNM staging of oropharyngeal carcinoma (AJCC 8th Edition). Discuss the surgical management of oropharyngeal carcinoma.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 13 (Oropharyngeal Tumours)

---

## Introduction

The rapid increase in the incidence of HPV-associated oropharyngeal carcinoma (HPV+OPSCC) seen across the developed world represents a major shift in head and neck oncology. In the UK, the incidence of OPSCC underwent a 2-fold increase between 2002 and 2011. A UK multicentre cross-sectional study found the overall proportion of HPV-positive OPSCC was 51.8%, and this proportion remained unchanged throughout the decade — indicating that the absolute number of both HPV-positive and HPV-negative cases was increasing. This discrete disease entity presents in younger, fitter patients who drink less alcohol and smoke less tobacco than those with HPV-negative disease, yet paradoxically their tumours, which present with features traditionally considered as harbingers of poor outcome (multiple cervical nodes, extracapsular spread), respond more favourably to treatment. HPV status is highly prognostic — in the landmark study by Ang et al., 3-year overall survival for HPV+ patients treated with CRT was 82.4% compared to 57.1% for HPV- patients (p<0.001).

## PART A: Surgical Anatomy of the Oropharynx and Base of Tongue

### Boundaries

The oropharynx is bounded superiorly by the soft palate, inferiorly by the upper surface of the epiglottis (the lingual surface of the epiglottis to the tip is included), anteriorly by the palatoglossal arches and the vallate papillae, and laterally by the pharyngoepiglottic folds. The posterior third of the tongue (base of tongue, BOT), including the vallecula, is included in the oropharynx.

### Subsites

| Subsite | Proportion of OPSCC | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Palatine tonsil | ~60% | Commonest subsite; most frequent CUP site |
| Base of tongue | ~30% | Often submucosal; midline extension |
| Soft palate | ~10% | Often bilateral nodal disease |
| Posterior pharyngeal wall | Rare | Submucosal spread; poor prognosis |

### Layers of the Oropharyngeal Wall

The oropharyngeal wall comprises four layers:
1. **Inner mucosal lining** — stratified squamous epithelium (predominantly non-keratinizing)
2. **Submucosa** — contains minor salivary glands (concentrated on oral surface of soft palate)
3. **Muscular layer** — superior and inferior constrictors, stylopharyngeus, palatopharyngeus, salpingopharyngeus
4. **Buccopharyngeal fascia** — outermost layer; separated posteriorly from the prevertebral fascia by the retropharyngeal space

### Waldeyer's Ring

The oropharynx contains an abundance of lymphoid tissue — the palatine and lingual tonsils form the anterolateral components of Waldeyer's ring. These lymphoid aggregates are covered by pharyngeal mucosa which forms epithelial crypts extending deep towards the centre of the tonsils. A particularly large crypt (intratonsillar cleft) is found at the boundary of the caudal 2/3 and cranial 1/3 of the tonsil — this is the remnant of the embryonic second pharyngeal cleft.

### Blood Supply and Innervation

The lateral wall and tonsil take most of their blood supply from the facial artery. Laterally, there is a rich plexus of paratonsillar veins which may be the source of serious venous bleeding during surgery. Surgical access may be achieved transorally, anteriorly via mandibulotomy, or inferiorly and laterally from the neck.

## PART B: Role of HPV in Oropharyngeal Carcinoma

### Epidemiology of Oral HPV Infection

The prevalence of oral HPV infection is much lower than genital infection. Systematic review data concluded the rate of oral infection with any HPV genotype was 4.5%, with HRHPV at 3.5% and HPV-16 at 1%. The NHANES study (2009–10) found overall oral HPV prevalence of 6.9%, with HRHPV at 3.9% and HPV-16 at 1% — amounting to over 2 million US citizens with oral HPV infection at any given time.

### Risk Factors for Oral HPV Infection

- **Sexual activity** — 8-fold increased prevalence in sexually active vs never-had-sex individuals; prevalence >20% in those reporting >20 lifetime sexual partners
- **Male gender** — 10% of men infected vs 3% of women
- **Tobacco use** — 20-fold increased risk for heavy smokers (>21 cigarettes/day); doubles odds of oral HPV infection
- **Bimodal age distribution** — first peak (~7%) in early 30s, second peak (~11%) in early 60s

### Natural History

The vast majority (85%) of oral HPV infections are cleared within 1 year. Only a minority develop persistent infection, and an even smaller percentage progress to malignancy. No premalignant lesion similar to CIN has ever been detected in the oropharynx.

### Molecular Biology of HPV-Induced Transformation

HPV-16 is the causative agent, dominating in OPSCC (~95%) compared to cervical cancer (~60%). The HPV genome contains eight genes — six early genes (E1, 2, 4–7) and two late structural genes (L1, L2).

The oncogenic mechanism operates through two key viral oncoproteins:

- **E6 oncoprotein** — binds and degrades p53 (the 'guardian of the genome'), preventing DNA damage repair and apoptosis
- **E7 oncoprotein** — binds and degrades pRb (retinoblastoma protein), causing unregulated cell cycle progression from G1 to S phase

A critical step is viral integration into the host genome, disrupting the E2 transcriptional repressor gene and leading to unregulated E6/E7 expression. Loss of pRb function leads to overexpression of p16INK4a, which serves as a surrogate marker for HPV infection.

### HPV Detection Methods

| Test | What It Detects | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| p16 IHC | p16 overexpression (surrogate) | Cheap, widely available; low specificity (10–15% false positive) |
| HPV ISH | HPV DNA presence | Localizes to cell nucleus; punctate pattern suggests integration |
| PCR | HPV DNA (consensus or quantitative) | Highly sensitive; can quantify viral load |
| qRT-PCR (gold standard) | E6/E7 mRNA transcripts | Confirms biological activity; costly, labour-intensive |
| RNA-ISH | E6/E7 mRNA in FFPE tissue | 97% sensitivity, 93% specificity; likely future routine standard |

### Diagnostic Algorithm (Johns Hopkins / Westra)

1. Screen with p16 IHC on FFPE tissue
2. All p16-positive tumours undergo HPV-16 specific ISH
3. p16+/HPV-16+ = designated HPV-positive
4. p16+/HPV-16− undergo testing with broader HRHPV ISH probe

### Histopathological Features of HPV+OPSCC

- Non-keratinizing morphology (vs keratinizing in HPV−)
- Poorly differentiated, predominantly basaloid morphology
- Not associated with surface dysplasia
- High level of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) — density has prognostic value

## PART C: TNM Staging — AJCC 8th Edition Changes for OPSCC

The 8th edition introduced separate staging systems for p16+OPSCC and p16−OPSCC.

### Key Changes

1. **Separate chapters** for p16+OPSCC and p16−OPSCC (previously combined with other pharyngeal cancers)
2. **p16+OPSCC T stage:** Tis removed (no premalignant lesion identified); T4b removed (outcomes for T4a and T4b indistinguishable) — single T4 category remains
3. **T0 category** maintained for p16+OPSCC (primary site can be inferred from HPV status) but removed for p16−OPSCC
4. **Separate clinical (cTNM) and pathological (pTNM) staging** for p16+OPSCC

### N Staging for p16+OPSCC

| | Clinical N (cN) | Pathological N (pN) |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | Ipsilateral node(s) all <6 cm | 1–4 positive nodes |
| N2 | Bilateral/contralateral nodes <6 cm | ≥5 positive nodes |
| N3 | Any node >6 cm | — |

- In cTNM: node size and laterality remain discriminatory
- In pTNM: number of involved nodes is the critical factor (not size)

### Stage Grouping for p16+OPSCC

| Stage | TNM Combinations |
|---|---|
| Stage I | T0–T2 N0–N1 M0 |
| Stage II | T0–T2 N2 M0; T3 N0–N2 M0 |
| Stage III | T4 or N3 M0 |
| Stage IV | Any T, any N, M1 |

> **Important:** TNM 8th edition is useful for prognostication but should NOT be used to inform treatment decision-making as its role in treatment allocation requires prospective evaluation.

## PART D: Management of Oropharyngeal Carcinoma

### Clinical Presentation

HPV+OPSCC typically presents with a small primary tumour and multiple, enlarged, cystic cervical lymph nodes with a high prevalence of ECS. In contrast, HPV−OPSCC presents with larger primaries and relatively lower cervical tumour burden. A painless neck lump at level II is the most common presentation — up to 50–70% present with a neck mass. Patients may also present with sore throat, odynophagia, referred otalgia, dysphagia or altered speech.

### Treatment Options

- **T1–T2 N0:** Radical [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] OR transoral surgery + [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]]
- **Other stages:** Primary CRT OR surgery + adjuvant (chemo)radiotherapy
- The goal in early stage disease is single modality treatment

### Non-Surgical Management

**Radiotherapy (RT):** RT alone is effective for early stage T1–T2 N0–N1 disease, delivered at 70 Gy in 35 fractions (or hypofractionated 65–66 Gy in 30 fractions).

**Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT):** Standard of care for Stage III/IV disease. RT 70 Gy/35 fractions with cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on days 1, 22 and 43. The GORTEC 94-01 study demonstrated ≥20% 3- and 5-year survival benefit for adding chemotherapy to RT. Meta-analysis of 17,346 patients confirmed concurrent chemotherapy confers a 6.5% overall survival benefit at 5 years.

**Alternative chemotherapy regimens:**
- Weekly cisplatin (40–50 mg/m2) — no difference in efficacy vs 3-weekly; higher compliance, less toxic
- Carboplatin (3-weekly AUC 5 or weekly AUC 2) — if cisplatin contraindicated
- Cetuximab + RT — if platinum contraindicated; improved median OS (49 vs 29.3 months vs RT alone)

**IMRT** is the accepted standard of care. The PARSPORT study demonstrated reduced grade 3–4 xerostomia from 83% to 29% at 24 months. Dysphagia-optimized IMRT is being prospectively evaluated (DARS trial).

### Transoral Surgery

**Transoral Laser Microsurgery (TLM):**
- Conducted according to principles of Steiner and Ambrosch
- Tumours removed in several planned pieces following transtumoral resection
- Representative marginal biopsies taken from tumour bed (up to 8 for tonsil tumours)
- 3-year OS ~84.5%, disease-specific survival ~91.7% (Liverpool series)

**Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS):**
- En bloc removal of tumour (standard lateral oropharyngectomy for tonsil)
- Constrictor muscles removed with specimen
- Neck dissection usually performed 2 weeks prior (staged approach)
- Ipsilateral lingual and facial arteries ligated during neck dissection
- 2-year locoregional control 91.8%, OS 91% (multicentre cohort of 410 patients)
- Contraindications: [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] extension, pterygoid musculature involvement, retropharyngeal carotid artery

**Key difference:** TLM employs transtumoral resection tailored to tumour anatomy; TORS uses standard en bloc resection with measured pathological margins.

### Adjuvant Treatment After Surgery

Most patients require adjuvant therapy — PORT (21–58%) or POCRT (16–62%). Indications include: close/positive margins, T3–T4 stage, perineural/lymphovascular invasion, N2–N3 nodal disease, and extracapsular spread. A minimum dose of 57.6 Gy is recommended, with up to 63 Gy to areas of ECS. PORT should start within 5 weeks of surgery (ideally) and no later than 6 weeks. Two RCTs (RTOG/EORTC) confirmed that patients with ECS and/or positive margins benefit from post-operative CRT compared to RT alone.

### Management of the Neck

**N0 neck:** Micrometastatic disease prevalence in OPSCC is 10–30%. Selective neck dissection (levels II–III, possibly IV) or elective RT is recommended. Level IIB dissection is not mandated for N0 disease.

**N+ neck (surgical):** Selective neck dissection including at least levels IIA, III and possibly IV. Level IIB is usually included in N+ neck.

**N+ neck (non-surgical):** The PET NECK trial confirmed that a post-treatment [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]] surveillance policy (neck dissection only for FDG-positive/equivocal residual disease at 12 weeks post-CRT) results in equivalent survival and regional control compared to planned neck dissection, with reduced morbidity — 4 out of 5 patients avoided surgery.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. HPV-16 is the causative agent in ~52% of UK OPSCC; in OPSCC there is greater dominance of HPV-16 (~95%) compared to cervical cancer (~60%)
> 2. E6 oncoprotein degrades p53 and E7 degrades pRb — loss of pRb leads to p16 overexpression, which serves as a surrogate marker for HPV infection
> 3. p16 IHC has low specificity (10–15% false positive rate) and should ideally be combined with HPV DNA testing for accurate diagnosis
> 4. AJCC 8th Edition introduces separate staging for p16+ and p16− OPSCC — T4b is removed for p16+; separate clinical and pathological N staging systems
> 5. HPV+OPSCC typically presents with a small primary and multiple cystic cervical nodes — incidental neck lump at level II is the commonest presentation
> 6. 85% of oral HPV infections are cleared within 1 year — no premalignant lesion similar to CIN has been identified in the oropharynx
> 7. CRT is standard of care for advanced OPSCC: RT 70 Gy/35 fractions with cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on days 1, 22 and 43
> 8. IMRT is the accepted standard for RT planning — PARSPORT study reduced grade 3–4 xerostomia from 83% to 29%
> 9. TLM and TORS are minimally invasive transoral surgical approaches with 3-year OS of 84–91% — key difference is transtumoral (TLM) vs en bloc (TORS) resection philosophy
> 10. PET NECK trial confirmed that PET-CT surveillance at 12 weeks post-CRT avoids planned neck dissection in 80% of patients with equivalent oncological outcomes

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - HPV+OPSCC is the commonest cause of carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP) in the head and neck — p16/HPV testing of FNAC specimens from cervical nodes strongly indicates an oropharyngeal primary
> - Tobacco use is a dose-dependent risk-stratifier of outcome in HPV+OPSCC — it is a surrogate of underlying tumour genome mutation load and genetic instability
> - Soft palate OPSCC is more likely to be HPV-negative and has worse outcomes than tonsil and BOT tumours
> - qRT-PCR detecting E6/E7 mRNA is the gold standard for HPV testing, but RNA-ISH (97% sensitivity, 93% specificity on FFPE tissue) is anticipated to become the routine standard
> - When TORS is used, a staged neck dissection 2 weeks before primary resection reduces pharyngocutaneous fistula risk — lingual and facial arteries are ligated during the neck dissection to reduce local blood pressure
> - Adjuvant treatment limits the functional benefits of transoral surgery — current adjuvant treatment protocols (based on pre-HPV era data) are being re-evaluated in ECOG 3311 and PATHOS trials
