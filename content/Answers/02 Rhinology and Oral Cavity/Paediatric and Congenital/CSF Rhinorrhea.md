# CSF Rhinorrhea - Comprehensive Review

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Q86 (Apr 2023): CSF rhinorrhea causes and management. Q125 (Jun 2025): BIH presenting as CSF rhinorrhea - radiology features, diagnosis and management. 2018-17: CSF rhinorrhea - causes, features, surgical management. 2014-4: Evaluate post-traumatic CSF rhinorrhoea.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 108 (CSF Leaks)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea represents an abnormal communication between the subarachnoid space and the nasal cavity through a defect in the skull base and overlying dura. Few procedures reflect the tremendous benefits of endoscopic techniques as dramatically as endoscopic closure of CSF leaks. Remarkable success rates of 90% at the initial attempt, rising to 97% with revision, have been published. Despite these figures, some neurosurgical centres continue to favour open approaches, with certain published series reporting success rates as low as 60%, along with predictable sequelae from brain retraction, olfactory nerve sacrifice, coronal flap, and frontal craniotomy.

Although rhinorrhea can be a socially troubling symptom, the principal motivation for repair is to reduce the risk of meningitis and other intracranial complications. The incidence of meningitis in patients with an active CSF leak has been quoted as 10% per year, although a more recent study by Daudia et al. suggested a rate as low as 0.3% per year per active leak per person. Even today, meningitis carries a definite mortality rate, making surgical repair necessary at the earliest reasonable opportunity.

---

## PART A: AETIOLOGY AND CLASSIFICATION

The most useful classification system is the time-honoured system of classification by aetiology. CSF leaks can be considered as congenital, spontaneous, or post-traumatic. Post-traumatic leaks are further divided into those associated with head trauma, those complicating sinus surgery, and those occurring as part of skull base surgery. A small number of patients with tumours also present with CSF leaks.

### 1. Post-Traumatic CSF Leaks (Most Common Overall)

**Head Trauma:** CSF leaks occur in approximately 2% of head injuries and 12-30% of skull base fractures. Most occur as a result of blunt trauma and may present as CSF otorrhea, CSF oto-rhinorrhea, or CSF rhinorrhea. The conventional viewpoint is that most traumatic CSF leaks will heal with conservative treatment including bedrest. However, it is likely that only the sinus mucosal aspect of the defect closes, as the dura does not regenerate. This produces a relatively fragile closure, and work by Bernal-Sprekelsen et al. has suggested a substantial continuing risk of meningitis in these patients. The authors suggest that this "mono-layer of protection" may be eroded by either the pulsatile effect of the brain or by inflammation within the nose.

Massive head trauma with complex comminuted and displaced fractures can be incredibly difficult to treat. Open approaches may be of value, with the decision sometimes facilitated by the presence of intracranial injuries or significant fractures of the posterior wall of the [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]]. Free tissue transfer has been reported as helpful in carefully selected groups. In massive "egg-shell" fractures of the sphenoid sinuses, fat obliteration can be considered, with treatment of possible subsequent mucoceles left to a secondary procedure.

**Post-Surgical CSF Leaks:** A rate of serious complications of 0.5% or less is generally quoted for endoscopic sinus surgery. The most likely anatomical sites for CSF leaks complicating sinus surgery are:

- The very thin bone of the **lateral lamella of the cribriform plate**
- The area of the anterior skull base weakened by the **anterior ethmoid neurovascular bundle**
- Posteriorly, where there may be confusion regarding the exact anatomical relationship between the **last posterior ethmoidal cell and the sphenoid sinus**

### 2. Spontaneous CSF Leaks (Increasing Recognition)

Spontaneous CSF leaks, sometimes described as "idiopathic," are associated with middle-aged women with a raised body mass index and represent a variant of benign intracranial hypertension (BIH). These patients have a generally elevated intracranial pressure due to poor CSF resorption by the arachnoid villi. The increased intracranial pressure with associated pulsatile forces produces thinning of bone most evident in the weakest areas of the skull base. Herniation of meninges and brain, often with CSF leakage, most often occurs at:

- **Lateral recess of a well-pneumatized sphenoid**
- **Lateral lamella of the cribriform plate**
- **Ethmoid roof**

These may represent multiple sites of weakness of the skull base. Spontaneous leaks carry the highest recurrence rate: 21% overall, rising to 46% when radiological signs of raised intracranial pressure are present, compared to only 2% for non-spontaneous leaks.

### 3. Congenital CSF Leaks

Congenital CSF leaks in association with encephalocele or meningoencephalocele are uncommon. These are well diagnosed on MRI, which is recommended when sinus opacity is demonstrated adjacent to a skull base defect. Brain tissue contained within the encephalocele is invariably non-functioning and can be removed as part of the surgical procedure. Congenital abnormalities of the inner ear such as the Mondini dysplasia may present with substantial CSF leaks where the CSF has briefly traversed the perilymphatic space.

### 4. Tumour-Related CSF Leaks

Tumours causing substantial erosion of the skull base may present with CSF rhinorrhea. Where tumour shrinkage occurs (e.g., during induction chemotherapy), CSF leaks may also develop. For closure to be successful, the margins must be clear of tumour, particularly in malignant disease.

| Type | Common Cause | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Post-traumatic | Head injury, [[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]] | Most common; 2% of head injuries |
| Spontaneous/BIH | Raised ICP, obesity | 21-46% recurrence rate |
| Congenital | Encephalocele | Non-functioning brain tissue |
| Tumour-related | Skull base erosion | Needs tumour-free margins |

## PART B: CLINICAL FEATURES AND DIAGNOSIS

### Clinical Presentation

The typical presentation is clear, often salty rhinorrhea that is usually unilateral and posturally provoked. A "halo sign" may be seen on tissue where CSF diffuses further than mucus, creating a ring. A "reservoir sign" describes a gush of fluid after bending forward. However, as compelling as such a history appears, patients do not always end up having CSF leaks. In one series, 9 patients with highly suggestive histories were shown not to have CSF leaks, making further investigation essential.

### Investigation Algorithm

**Step 1 - Clinical Assessment:**
- Detailed history and endoscopic examination of the nose
- Endoscopic examination may be unrevealing but is part of the initial work-up

**Step 2 - Fluid Collection and Biochemical Testing:**
- Ideally obtained at the office visit with postural provocation
- If unsuccessful, patients may strain on a closed glottis or be given vials to take home
- **Beta-2 transferrin test:** Provides excellent sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictability. A concomitant serum value enhances its utility. False positives reported in certain liver diseases or hereditary disorders of protein metabolism. Downside is that results may take a few days
- **Beta-trace protein:** Suggested in some European studies to be an even better test than beta-2-transferrin
- **Glucose testing (paper strip):** Unreliable due to high false-positive rate; no longer recommended as sole test

**Step 3 - Imaging:**
- **High-resolution CT** (ultra-fine cut, coronal reformats): Most helpful for examining the ethmoid roof. May show bony defect, sinus opacification, or air-fluid level
- **MRI:** Recommended when sinus opacity is adjacent to a skull base defect to diagnose encephalocele or meningoencephalocele. Also demonstrates radiological features of raised ICP
- **CT temporal bones:** Should be considered if CSF oto-rhinorrhea is suspected

**Step 4 - Contrast Studies (if diagnosis remains unclear):**
- **CT cisternography** with intrathecal iohexol
- **Radionuclide cisternography** with strategically placed nasal pledgets
- **Gadolinium-enhanced MRI cisternography** (off-label use of gadolinium; Aydin et al. found this useful in 43 of 51 patients with no neurological sequelae)

**Step 5 - Intrathecal Fluorescein:**
- Both a diagnostic and intraoperative localization tool (detailed in Part E)

## PART C: EVALUATION OF POST-TRAUMATIC CSF RHINORRHEA

### History

A thorough history should establish:
- Temporal relationship to head injury and mechanism of trauma
- Nature and force of trauma (blunt vs. penetrating)
- History of meningitis episodes
- Duration, intermittency, and laterality of the leak
- Associated symptoms (headache, visual changes, anosmia)
- Previous sinus surgery or neurosurgical procedures

### Examination

- **Neurological examination:** GCS, cranial nerve assessment
- **Anterior rhinoscopy and nasal endoscopy:** Look for clear fluid at middle meatus, olfactory cleft, or sphenoethmoidal recess
- **Signs of skull base fracture:** Battle's sign, raccoon eyes, haemotympanum

### Investigation

- Fluid collection with positional provocation
- Beta-2 transferrin testing (gold standard)
- High-resolution CT of sinuses and skull base
- CT temporal bones if CSF otorrhea suspected
- MRI to exclude encephalocele

### Management of Post-Traumatic CSF Leaks

**Conservative Management:** Most traumatic CSF leaks will heal with conservative measures including bedrest, head elevation, avoidance of nose blowing, straining, and sneezing with mouth closed, and avoidance of CPAP. A lumbar drain may sometimes be required. However, the resulting closure is often only a "mono-layer of protection" (mucosal only, as dura does not regenerate), leaving a fragile barrier with a continuing risk of meningitis. With the widespread availability of endoscopic closure performed with statistically small risks, this conservative paradigm might be challenged towards a more active interventionist policy.

**Indications for Surgical Repair:**
- Conservative management fails after 7-10 days
- Recurrent meningitis episodes
- Persistent or high-flow leak
- Complex comminuted fractures requiring combined approach

**Special Situations:**
- Massive comminuted fractures may require open approaches, facilitated by co-existing intracranial injuries
- Free tissue transfer reported in carefully selected groups
- Sphenoid "egg-shell" fractures may be managed by fat obliteration with secondary mucocele treatment
- Intraoperative CSF leak during sinus surgery should be repaired under the same anaesthetic using local intranasal tissue

## PART D: SPONTANEOUS CSF LEAKS AND BIH

### Benign Intracranial Hypertension (BIH/IIH)

The typical patient is an obese, middle-aged woman with elevated intracranial pressure due to impaired CSF resorption by the arachnoid villi. Clinical presentations include headache, visual disturbances, papilloedema, pulsatile [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], and/or CSF rhinorrhea. CSF rhinorrhea is now recognized as a presenting feature of BIH.

It is not possible to measure intracranial pressure pre-operatively as the persistent leak reduces the pressure. Diagnosis of elevated ICP is made on lumbar puncture after surgical repair.

### Radiological Features of BIH

Imaging may demonstrate features of raised intracranial pressure even while the leak is active:
- **Empty sella** (partially or completely)
- **Enlarged ventricles**
- **Diffuse thinning/erosion of skull base**
- **Multiple areas of skull base dehiscence or weakness**
- Tortuous optic nerve sheath
- Prominent perioptic CSF spaces

### Why Spontaneous Leaks Differ from Other Types

- **Highest recurrence rate:** 21% overall; 46% with radiological signs of raised ICP (compared to 2% for non-spontaneous)
- **Multiple potential leak sites:** Sealing one defect may lead to leakage at another weak point of the skull base
- **Underlying elevated ICP must be addressed** to prevent recurrence
- **Pre-operative counselling** is essential: patients must be informed of the higher recurrence risk

### Management of BIH-Related CSF Leaks

**Surgical Repair:**
- Endoscopic closure of the defect (same principles as other CSF leaks)
- Areas of substantial skull base dehiscence may benefit from reinforcement with bone or cartilage graft
- Lateral sphenoid recess leaks: transantral transpterygoid approach (described by Bolger)

**Post-operative ICP Management:**
- **Oral acetazolamide** to reduce CSF production and lower ICP
- **Weight loss program** (reduces ICP)
- **VP shunt or lumboperitoneal shunt** in selected patients
- **Gastric bypass** for morbid obesity has been described
- Bio-absorbable transparent plates recommended by Potter et al. for patients with elevated BMI

## PART E: ENDOSCOPIC SURGICAL REPAIR

### Success Rates

Endoscopic closure achieves 90% success at first attempt, up to 97% with revision. This compares favourably with some open neurosurgical series reporting success rates as low as 60%.

### Pre-operative Planning

**Intrathecal Fluorescein Protocol:**
- 10 cc of patient's CSF withdrawn via lumbar puncture
- Mixed with 0.1 cc of 10% fluorescein suitable for injection
- Re-injected slowly over 10 minutes, timed by the clock
- Patient placed slightly head-down to allow fluorescein to equilibrate throughout the subarachnoid space
- Leak site disclosed by bright green colour under endoscopic visualization
- Fluorescein is useful in three ways: (1) diagnosing a CSF leak, (2) disclosing its exact location, and (3) by its absence, providing evidence of successful repair
- Complications (seizures) are generally associated with wrong formulation, excessive dose, or rapid bolus injection
- Topical 5% fluorescein applied intraoperatively is an alternative described by Saafan et al.

### Graft Materials and Reconstruction

| Defect Size | Preferred Graft | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Small defects | Free grafts (fascia, mucosa) | Easy manipulation |
| Medium defects | Bone/cartilage grafts | Reinforces dehiscent areas |
| Large defects | Vascularized [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|pedicled flap]]s | Post skull base surgery |

**Free Grafts:** Fascia lata, temporalis fascia, free mucosal grafts, acellular dermis. Preferred for smaller defects due to technical ease.

**Fat Grafts:** Useful for larger defects and obliteration.

**Vascularized Flaps (for large defects):**
- **Hadad-Bassagasteguy (posterior nasoseptal) flap:** Utilizes septal mucoperiosteum and mucoperichondrium based on the nasoseptal artery. Kassam et al. reported its utility in 75 patients undergoing endonasal skull base surgery
- **Inferior turbinate flap**
- **Nasal floor flap**

**Multi-layer skull base reconstruction:** Harvey et al. reported on 30 patients using a variety of flaps with multi-layer reconstruction initially supported by an inflated Foley catheter balloon.

**Fibrin glue:** Almeida et al. showed in a pig model that fibrin glue enhanced closures had superior graft adherence and higher burst pressures.

**Onlay vs. Underlay:** No literature exists favouring one technique over the other; choice is based on surgeon preference.

### Lumbar Drains

The routine use of lumbar drains is controversial. They are helpful when intrathecal fluorescein is employed and provide reduction in intracranial pressure as added insurance for the repair. However, successful repair without lumbar drains has been described. Lumbar drains can be associated with important complications and require specialist nursing management.

### Post-operative Care

- **No nose blowing**, straining, or sneezing with mouth closed
- **No CPAP** (risk of pneumocephalus)
- No positive pressure mask ventilation after extubation
- Antibiotics as appropriate
- Stool softeners to avoid straining
- **Pneumococcal vaccination** (23-valent polysaccharide or 7-valent conjugate) if antibody titres are low
- Gelfoam "break-layer" placed under the repair, then Merocel pack; the Gelfoam isolates the repair from shearing forces during pack removal

## PART F: COMPLICATIONS AND PROGNOSIS

**Meningitis:** The incidence with an active CSF leak has been quoted as 10% per year (historical) and possibly as low as 0.3% per year per active leak per person (Daudia et al.). In their study, 11 patients with 190 years of active leaks developed 21 episodes of meningitis (19% overall), mostly in the first year.

**Pneumocephalus:** A significant risk if positive pressure ventilation is used post-operatively. This has been specifically documented with CPAP use.

**Recurrence:**
- Non-spontaneous leaks: approximately 2%
- Spontaneous leaks: 21% overall; 46% with radiological signs of raised ICP
- A publication from the Mayo Clinic identified recurrence in a group of 21 patients at an average of 50.8 months after surgery
- The longest interval to recurrence was 28 years

**Image-guided surgery:** While indisputably helpful in individual cases, has not reduced the likelihood of complications in large case series. Powered instrumentation has changed the scale of complications, making brain parenchymal injury more likely than CSF leaks.

| Parameter | Non-Spontaneous | Spontaneous (BIH) |
|---|---|---|
| Recurrence rate | ~2% | 21-46% |
| Multiple defects | Uncommon | Common |
| ICP management | Not needed | Essential |
| Long-term follow-up | Standard | Mandatory |

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. CSF rhinorrhea carries a risk of meningitis (10% per year historically, possibly 0.3% per year per recent data); repair should be performed at the earliest reasonable opportunity though it is not an absolute emergency.
> 2. Post-traumatic leaks are the most common overall, occurring in 2% of head injuries and 12-30% of skull base fractures, mostly from blunt trauma.
> 3. Spontaneous CSF leaks represent a variant of BIH, typically seen in obese middle-aged women with elevated ICP due to poor arachnoid villi CSF resorption.
> 4. Beta-2 transferrin provides excellent sensitivity and specificity and is the standard diagnostic test for CSF; beta-trace protein may be even better per European studies.
> 5. Radiological features of BIH include empty sella, enlarged ventricles, and diffuse skull base erosion; these predict higher recurrence (46% vs. 21%).
> 6. Endoscopic repair achieves 90% success at first attempt, up to 97% with revision, compared to 60% in some open neurosurgical series.
> 7. Spontaneous leaks have the highest recurrence rate (21-46%) because of underlying elevated ICP and multiple potential skull base weak points.
> 8. Intrathecal fluorescein (0.1 cc of 10% mixed with 10 cc patient's CSF, re-injected over 10 minutes) localizes the leak intraoperatively by bright green colour.
> 9. The Hadad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap, based on the nasoseptal artery, is the workhorse vascularized flap for large skull base defects.
> 10. Post-operative management of BIH-related leaks requires oral acetazolamide, weight loss, and consideration of VP shunting to address the underlying elevated ICP.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Conservative management of traumatic CSF leaks produces only a "mono-layer of protection" as the dura does not regenerate; this leaves a fragile closure with continuing meningitis risk, challenging the traditional conservative paradigm.
> - Patients must be told: no nose blowing, sneeze with mouth open, no CPAP -- these are critical post-operative instructions, as positive pressure ventilation can cause pneumocephalus.
> - Multiple skull base defects in spontaneous leaks mean that repairing one site may unmask leakage at another weak area; the entire skull base must be imaged and the patient counselled about recurrence risk.
> - A Gelfoam "break-layer" placed under the nasal packing isolates the repair from shearing forces during pack removal -- a practical surgical refinement that improves outcomes.
> - Powered instruments in FESS have changed the scale of complications, making brain parenchymal injury more likely than eminently treatable CSF leaks.
> - Long-term follow-up is essential for all repaired CSF leaks; the longest reported interval to recurrence was 28 years after initial repair (Mayo Clinic series), and ICP cannot be measured pre-operatively when an active leak is present.
