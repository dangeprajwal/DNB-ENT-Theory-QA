# Nasal Obstruction in Neonates and Charge Syndrome

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Discuss the causes, diagnosis, and management of nasal obstruction in neonates. Describe CHARGE syndrome and its otorhinolaryngological implications.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 23 (Neonatal Nasal Obstruction) & Chapter 6 (The Child with a Syndrome)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The aetiology of neonatal nasal obstruction is diverse. Neonates are generally obligate nasal breathers for the first few months of life, and therefore they can present as acute respiratory emergencies, classically with cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying. The extent of their problems will depend on the neonate's ability to breathe orally, which is dependent on maturity and neurological development. An oral airway is often sufficient to relieve respiratory distress until definitive treatment can be undertaken.

## PART B: CHOANAL ATRESIA

### Incidence and Pathology

- Rare condition: 1 in 7000 live births
- Complete obstruction of posterior choanae on one or both sides
- Mixed bony-membranous picture in 70% of cases; purely bony in remainder
- Believed to be secondary to persistence of the nasobuccal membrane
- CT scan demonstrates expansion of the posterior vomer and medial maxillary walls

### Clinical Features

**Bilateral choanal atresia:**
- Acute respiratory distress in neonates (obligate nasal breathers)
- Cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying
- Oral airway resolves distress
- Difficulty with feeding (loss of the nasopharyngeal respiratory channel)
- McGovern nipples beneficial for feeding difficulties

**Unilateral choanal atresia:**
- May present later in life
- Can be detected when inability to pass nasogastric tube through one nasal passageway

### Diagnosis

- **Misting test:** Placement of metal spatula below external nasal aperture; absence of misting suggests atresia
- **Flexible nasendoscopy:** Confirms diagnosis
- **CT scanning:** Determines extent and nature; nose should be suctioned and 0.5% ephedrine drops applied 30 minutes prior to scan

### Surgical Management

**Approaches:**

| Approach | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Endoscopic transnasal (120°) | Endoscope in mouth, instruments through nose; largest series: 161 patients (Great Ormond Street) |
| Endoscopic transnasal (0°) | Serial dilatation with urethral sounds or powered instruments; neo-unichoana via posterior septal window |
| Transpalatal | Less common now; useful in craniofacial anomalies with limited nasal dimensions |
| Balloon dilatation | High success rates but small series |

**Post-operative considerations:**

- **Stenting:** Nasal stents from ivory Portex endotracheal tubes with bridging piece; secured by circumseptal prolene suture for up to 6 weeks. Systematic review shows similar success rates with and without stents; stents may be associated with more complications
- **Suctioning and washing:** Regular suctioning and daily washing with sodium chloride solution results in successful outcomes
- **Mitomycin C:** No definitive benefit in terms of outcomes
- **KTP laser:** Helpful for post-operative granulation tissue

### Success Rates

- Bilateral choanal atresia: 68-80% success
- Unilateral choanal atresia: 82-93% success
- Vomer resection and early (1 week post-repair) examination for granulation removal and dilatation is important for those who do not use stents

## PART C: PYRIFORM APERTURE STENOSIS

### Pathology

- First described in 1988; very rare condition
- Bony overgrowth of the nasal process of the maxilla
- The pyriform aperture is the narrowest part of the nasal airway - even minimal reduction causes significant obstruction

### Clinical Features

- Symptoms similar to bilateral choanal atresia
- Epiphora secondary to bony involvement of nasolacrimal ducts
- **Single central incisor** (megaincisor) association: absent upper frenulum and arch-shaped lower lip
- In the subgroup with megaincisor, ~50% association with holoprosencephaly (failure of forebrain division to form cerebral hemispheres)

### Diagnosis

- Inability to pass narrow gauge nasogastric tube or 2.2 mm endoscope through anterior nasal vestibule
- **CT scan:** Aperture width <11 mm measured on axial CT at level of inferior meatus (in term neonate)
- Aperture <5 mm: almost always requires surgical intervention
- MRI for hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis assessment if megaincisor present

### Management

**Conservative (first-line):**
- Nasal steroid drops or decongestants (up to 2 weeks)
- Saline irrigation

**Surgical (for severe obstruction, respiratory distress, failure to thrive):**
- Transnasal approach with alar-releasing incision, OR
- Sublabial approach with gingival-buccal sulcus incision
- Periosteum elevated, abnormal bone drilled with diamond burr
- Post-operative nasal stents for up to 4 weeks (recent studies suggest stenting not necessary)
- Complications: adhesions, [[Septal Perforation Repair|septal perforation]]s, septal ulceration

## PART D: CHARGE SYNDROME

### Definition

CHARGE syndrome is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder typically caused by mutations in the **chromodomain helicase DNA-binding protein-7 (CHD7) gene** on chromosome 8. The acronym denotes the non-random association of:

- **C** - Coloboma
- **H** - Heart anomalies
- **A** - Atresia choanae
- **R** - Retardation of growth and development
- **G** - Genital anomalies
- **E** - Ear anomalies

### Neonatal Presentation

- Small for gestational age
- Dysmorphic features
- Respiratory distress/cyanosis
- Swallowing/feeding difficulty
- Failed newborn hearing screen
- Inability to pass nasogastric tube

### Otorhinolaryngological Manifestations

**Choanal atresia:**
- Membranous or bony; bilateral in >50% of cases
- When bilateral, it is the most easily ascertained feature and requires early surgical correction
- Of all features, bilateral choanal atresia is the most life-threatening (cannot establish mouth breathing)
- When associated with cyanotic heart disease, tracheoesophageal fistula/atresia, prognosis is poor

**Ear anomalies:**
- External ear malformations in 90-100% of patients
- Ears may be small, simple, low-set, and/or cup-shaped
- Protruding helix may be unravelled
- Sensorineural deafness from vestibular or cochlear defects
- Middle ear problems: ossicular malformations, stapedius tendon abnormality, serous effusion
- CT temporal bone: partial or complete semicircular canal hypoplasia (characteristic finding)

**Unilateral choanal atresia:** May present as persistent nasal discharge in early childhood

### Minimum Investigations in a Child with Choanal Atresia

To exclude CHARGE syndrome:
1. Nasal CT scan (extent of atresia)
2. Cardiac echocardiography
3. Renal ultrasound scan
4. Ophthalmology review (coloboma)
5. Audiology review

## PART E: INVESTIGATION OF NEONATAL NASAL OBSTRUCTION

### Clinical Assessment

- Observation of breathing pattern (cyclical cyanosis, stertor, feeding difficulties)
- Assessment of ability to breathe orally (maturity-dependent)
- Passage of nasogastric tube or suction catheter
- Misting test with metal spatula

### Imaging

- **Flexible nasendoscopy:** Particularly useful first-line investigation
- **CT scanning:** Delineates both nasal and post-nasal lesions; determines nature (bony vs membranous)
- **MRI:** Valuable for soft tissue characterization, especially congenital masses (meningoencephalocoele vs glioma vs dermoid)

### Emergency Management

- Oral airway: often sufficient in neonates and well tolerated
- Endotracheal intubation: only if oral airway insufficient
- McGovern nipple: for feeding difficulties in bilateral choanal atresia

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Neonates are obligate nasal breathers; bilateral nasal obstruction presents as cyclical cyanosis relieved by crying
> 2. Choanal atresia: 1 in 7000 live births; 70% mixed bony-membranous; persistence of nasobuccal membrane
> 3. CHARGE syndrome: CHD7 gene mutation on chromosome 8; choanal atresia (bilateral in >50%) is the most easily ascertained and most life-threatening feature
> 4. Minimum investigations in choanal atresia: nasal CT, cardiac echo, renal USS, ophthalmology, audiology (to exclude CHARGE)
> 5. Pyriform aperture stenosis: CT aperture <11 mm diagnostic; <5 mm almost always requires surgery; association with megaincisor and holoprosencephaly (~50%)
> 6. External ear malformations in 90-100% of CHARGE patients; semicircular canal hypoplasia on CT is characteristic
> 7. Endoscopic transnasal repair of choanal atresia: success rates 68-80% bilateral, 82-93% unilateral
> 8. Stenting vs no stenting for choanal atresia: systematic review shows similar outcomes; stenting may increase complications
> 9. Mitomycin C: no proven benefit in choanal atresia outcomes
> 10. Great Ormond Street Hospital series (161 patients) is the largest reported experience using endoscopic transnasal approach with 120-degree endoscope in the mouth


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - An oral airway is the simplest and most effective emergency measure for bilateral choanal atresia
> - Always exclude CHARGE syndrome in any child with choanal atresia - the acronym is a helpful screening checklist
> - CT scan nose: ensure suctioning and 0.5% ephedrine drops 30 minutes before scanning to clear secretions
> - Neo-unichoana technique (posterior septal window) allows endoscope through one nostril and instruments through the other when nasal cavity is too small
> - Pyriform aperture stenosis with a single central incisor mandates MRI brain to exclude holoprosencephaly
> - Vomer resection is key to success in choanal atresia repair - prevents re-stenosis
> - Unilateral choanal atresia may be an incidental finding; surgical repair can often be deferred
