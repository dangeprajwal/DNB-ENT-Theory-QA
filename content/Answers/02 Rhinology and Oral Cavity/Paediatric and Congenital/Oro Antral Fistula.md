# Oro-antral Fistula

**Question:** *Describe oro-antral fistula along with its surgical management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1 (Chapters 88, 94, 114) & Volume 3

**Note:** Scott-Brown's does not contain a dedicated chapter on oro-antral fistula. The following content is compiled from relevant mentions across the textbook including chapters on rhinosinusitis, fungal sinusitis, sinonasal tumours, and maxillofacial surgery.

---

## INTRODUCTION

An oro-antral fistula (OAF) is an abnormal epithelialized communication between the oral cavity and the maxillary sinus (antrum). It develops when an oro-antral communication (OAC) fails to heal spontaneously and becomes lined by epithelium. The maxillary sinus floor is in close proximity to the roots of the premolar and molar teeth, making this region vulnerable to fistula formation following dental procedures, trauma or pathology.

---

## PART A: AETIOLOGY

### Iatrogenic (Most Common)

An oro-antral fistula is created iatrogenically most commonly during dental extraction, particularly of upper premolar and molar teeth whose roots are in close proximity to or projecting into the maxillary sinus floor. Scott-Brown's specifically mentions the iatrogenic pathway when an oro-antral fistula is created during endodontic treatment with overfilling of the dental canal, which can promote fungal colonization of the sinuses.

### Other Causes

- **Odontogenic infections:** Dental infections can erode the maxillary sinus floor. In approximately 10% of unilateral [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|chronic rhinosinusitis]] cases, the cause is odontogenic. All unilateral cases should include an oral examination and careful review of the maxillary sinus floor on CT to look for signs of an oro-antral fistula.
- **Sinonasal tumours:** Oroantral fistula can result from erosion of the maxillary sinus floor by tumour and is listed as a concerning clinical finding suggesting invasion of the alveolar process.
- **Trauma:** Maxillofacial fractures involving the maxillary sinus floor
- **Post-surgical:** Following maxillectomy, Caldwell-Luc procedure or other maxillary surgery. Caldwell-Luc surgery is now rarely undertaken but carries risks including infraorbital nerve neuropraxia.

---

## PART B: PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

When a communication between the oral cavity and maxillary sinus occurs, if it does not close spontaneously within 24-48 hours, epithelial migration from both the oral and sinus mucosa lines the tract, creating a true fistula. Once epithelialized, spontaneous closure cannot occur.

The persistence of the fistula leads to:

- Contamination of the maxillary sinus with oral flora and food debris
- Secondary maxillary sinusitis
- Fungal ball formation - commonly used endodontic sealers may promote the growth of fungus; zinc oxide found in sealers promotes the growth of Aspergillus, while the liquid component eugenol may initially inhibit growth but this effect fades over time, allowing fungal growth many years after the initial dental treatment

---

## PART C: CLINICAL FEATURES

**Symptoms:**

- Passage of fluids from mouth to nose during drinking
- Escape of air through the extraction socket during nose blowing
- Foul-tasting nasal discharge
- Unilateral nasal obstruction and discharge
- Pain and swelling over the cheek

**Signs:**

- Visible defect in the alveolar ridge or hard palate
- Passage of air or fluid on Valsalva manoeuvre
- Probe can be passed from oral cavity into the sinus
- Signs of maxillary sinusitis on the affected side

**Investigations:**

- Oral examination is essential in all unilateral sinus disease
- CT scan - careful review of the maxillary sinus floor to look for signs of an oro-antral fistula; assessment of sinus mucosal disease
- Involvement of oral maxillofacial colleagues at an early stage where odontogenic aetiology is suspected

---

## PART D: MANAGEMENT

### Conservative Management

Small acute oro-antral communications (less than 2-3 mm) may heal spontaneously if managed conservatively:

- Avoidance of nose blowing, sneezing with mouth open
- Avoidance of sucking through straws
- Soft diet
- Nasal decongestants
- Antibiotics to prevent sinusitis
- Sinus precautions for 2-3 weeks

### Surgical Management

Surgical closure is indicated for:

- Communications larger than 5 mm
- Established epithelialized fistulae
- Failed conservative management
- Associated maxillary sinusitis

**Principles of surgical closure:**

- Excision of the fistulous tract and surrounding epithelialized tissue
- Treatment of any associated sinus infection (may require [[FESS Steps Complications|functional endoscopic sinus surgery]] or Caldwell-Luc to establish sinus drainage)
- Tension-free, well-vascularized flap closure
- Two-layer closure when possible

### Surgical Techniques

**1. Buccal Advancement Flap (Most Common)**

- Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap raised from the buccal mucosa
- Trapezoidal flap design with broad base for adequate blood supply
- Periosteal releasing incision at the base of the flap to allow tension-free advancement
- Advanced over the defect and sutured to the palatal mucosa
- Advantages: reliable blood supply, straightforward technique
- Disadvantage: reduces the vestibular depth

**2. Palatal Rotation Flap**

- Full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap based on the greater palatine artery
- The greater palatine artery enters the nasal cavity through the incisive canal to supply the anteroinferior septum and descends through the greater palatine canal to supply the hard palate
- Flap rotated to cover the defect
- Advantages: excellent blood supply from the greater palatine artery, thick resilient tissue
- Disadvantage: leaves exposed palatal bone at the donor site (heals by secondary intention)

**3. Buccal Fat Pad Flap**

- The buccal fat pad is a [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|pedicled flap]] that can be used for closure of defects in the posterior maxillary region
- Used for reconstruction following maxillary surgery with split skin graft or alone
- Good blood supply
- Advantages: readily available, good vascularity, pliable
- Useful for defects in the posterior alveolar/tuberosity region

**4. Combined Approaches**

For larger defects or recurrent fistulae:

- Two-layer closure using a combination of buccal and palatal flaps
- Buccal fat pad with buccal advancement flap
- Temporoparietal fascial flap for extensive defects

**5. Endoscopic Sinus Surgery**

If associated maxillary sinusitis is present, functional endoscopic sinus surgery to create a middle meatal antrostomy ensures adequate sinus drainage. This may be performed as a first stage or simultaneously with fistula closure.

**6. Caldwell-Luc Procedure**

Although now rarely undertaken, the Caldwell-Luc approach may be required for removal of diseased sinus mucosa or foreign bodies (dental roots, endodontic filling material). Risks include infraorbital nerve neuropraxia which can occur despite all care being taken.

### Management Algorithm

**Acute OAC (less than 48 hours):**

- Small (less than 2 mm): Conservative management with sinus precautions
- Medium (2-5 mm): Primary closure or figure-of-eight suture over extraction socket
- Large (more than 5 mm): Immediate buccal advancement flap closure

**Established OAF (epithelialized):**

1. Treat any associated sinusitis (antibiotics, FESS if needed)
2. Excise the fistulous tract
3. Close with appropriate flap (buccal advancement, palatal rotation or buccal fat pad)
4. Post-operative sinus precautions

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. OAF is an epithelialized communication between oral cavity and maxillary sinus; most common after dental extraction of upper premolars/molars
> 2. In 10% of unilateral CRS, the cause is odontogenic - always examine the oral cavity and review the maxillary sinus floor on CT
> 3. Iatrogenic OAF during endodontic treatment can promote fungal (Aspergillus) colonization of the maxillary sinus via zinc oxide in dental sealers
> 4. Oroantral fistula is a concerning sign in sinonasal tumours indicating erosion of the sinus floor
> 5. Small acute communications (less than 2 mm) may heal spontaneously with sinus precautions
> 6. Buccal advancement flap is the most commonly used surgical technique for OAF closure
> 7. Palatal rotation flap is based on the greater palatine artery and provides thick, reliable tissue
> 8. Buccal fat pad flap is useful for posterior maxillary defects
> 9. Associated sinusitis must be treated (FESS/Caldwell-Luc) before or with fistula closure
> 10. Two-layer closure reduces recurrence in larger or recurrent fistulae


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always consider odontogenic aetiology in unilateral maxillary sinusitis and look for oro-antral fistula on CT
> - Fungal ball formation in the maxillary sinus may be a late consequence of an iatrogenic OAF created during dental treatment
> - The greater palatine artery is the key vascular pedicle for the palatal rotation flap
> - Caldwell-Luc procedure is now rarely performed but may still be needed for removal of diseased mucosa or foreign bodies
> - Involvement of oral maxillofacial colleagues early is beneficial when odontogenic aetiology is suspected
> - Tension-free closure with well-vascularized tissue is the key to successful repair
