# Paediatric Nasal Masses, Rhinosinusitis, and Silent Sinus Syndrome

> [!question] Questions
> "Describe nasal dermoids — embryology, features, classification, and management. Classify midline nasal masses and discuss nasal glioma management. Discuss paediatric rhinosinusitis etiopathogenesis and management. Describe silent sinus syndrome — etiology, features, and treatment."

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapters 23 (Neonatal Nasal Obstruction), 24 (Paediatric Rhinosinusitis), 41 (Cysts and Sinuses of the Head and Neck); Volume 1, Chapter 111 (Disorders of the Orbit)

## Introduction

Midline nasal masses in children — dermoids, gliomas, and encephaloceles — are uncommon but important differential diagnoses that require careful evaluation to rule out intracranial connections before any surgical intervention. Paediatric rhinosinusitis is extremely common, typically following viral upper respiratory tract infections, but differs from the adult form in etiology, pathophysiology, and management. Silent sinus syndrome is a rare condition of chronic maxillary sinus atelectasis that may present incidentally with enophthalmos.

## Part A — Nasal Dermoids

### Embryology

Nasal dermoids arise from a failure of regression of the **dural diverticulum** that normally extends through the foramen cecum during embryological development. This neuroectodermal connection between the intracranial cavity and the nasal skin should completely regress. Persistence of any part of this tract results in a dermoid cyst, sinus, or fistula containing ectodermal elements (skin, hair follicles, sebaceous glands).

Nasal dermoids are the **most common midline nasal mass**, accounting for 1–3% of all dermoids.

### Clinical Features

- Slowly growing **midline cystic mass** over the nasal dorsum
- **Pit** at any position from nasal tip to glabella
- **Hair** may be present at the opening of the pit
- Non-transilluminant
- **No change with crying or straining** (distinguishes from encephalocele)
- Negative Furstenberg test
- May present as an abscess if secondarily infected
- Between 4% and 45% have an **intracranial component**

### Hartley Classification

*(Hartley et al. — series of 103 cases; cited in Scott-Brown Vol 2, Chapter 41, Figure 41.11)*

A pragmatic staging system based on depth of extension of the dermoid tract:

| Stage | Name | Extent | Surgical Approach |
|-------|------|--------|-------------------|
| 1 | **Superficial** | Within soft tissue only; most common | Simple excision |
| 2 | **Intraosseous** | Extends into frontal and nasal bones | Drill-out required |
| 3 | **Intracranial Extradural** | Breaches anterior cranial vault; outside dura | Frontonasal osteotomy; peeled from dura |
| 4 | **Intracranial Intradural** | Most complex; penetrates dura | Bicoronal flap + frontal craniotomy; endoscopic transnasal approach possible in selected cases |

### Investigation

- **CT scan:** Delineates bony anatomy, foramen cecum widening, bifid crista galli
- **MRI:** Essential — delineates any connection to the central nervous system and soft-tissue extent
- **Biopsy is safe** (unlike encephalocele) as dermoids have no CSF communication

### Management

- **Surgical excision** is the treatment of choice
- **External rhinoplasty approach** — for lesions in the lower nasal dorsum
- **Median rhinotomy** — for more extensive lesions
- **Combined with frontal craniotomy** — if intracranial extension is confirmed on MRI
- Endoscopic excision has been reported with image guidance for intracranial nasal dermoids
- Complete excision of the entire tract is essential to prevent recurrence

## Part B — Midline Nasal Masses — Classification and Differential Diagnosis

### The Three Main Congenital Midline Nasal Masses

| Feature | Dermoid | Glioma | Encephalocele |
|---------|---------|--------|---------------|
| Composition | Skin, hair, sebaceous | Glial cells, fibrous tissue | Meninges ± brain |
| Most common | Yes (1–3% dermoids) | Rarer | Rarest (1 in 4000) |
| Consistency | Cystic/firm | Firm, non-compressible | Soft, compressible |
| Transillumination | Negative | Negative | Positive |
| Change with crying | No | No | Enlarges |
| Furstenberg test | Negative | Negative | Positive |
| Intracranial connect. | 4–45% | 15% (fibrous stalk) | Always |
| Biopsy | Safe | Safe | CONTRAINDICATED |

### Nasal Glioma

Nasal gliomas are benign masses containing glial cells and fibrous tissue — a better term is "**glial heterotopia**" (choristoma) as they are not true neoplasms. They represent glial tissue that has become separated from the intracranial structures. Around 15% retain a **fibrous stalk connection** to the brain. Presentation is as a firm, non-compressible, reddish swelling, usually early in life.

**Differentiation from encephalocele:**
- A probe passes **laterally but not medially** to an intranasal encephalocele
- Intranasal glioma can arise from the **lateral nasal wall**
- **Furstenberg's test** (compress IJV) — encephalocele enlarges, glioma does not
- **MRI** is the most effective investigation — encephalocele shows brain in continuity with skull base defect; glioma is discontinuous

**Glioma Management:**
- **Complete surgical excision** — external rhinoplasty, lateral rhinotomy, or endoscopic approach
- Endoscopic approach with image guidance has shown successful outcomes
- If intracranial connection present, combined transnasal and neurosurgical approach

### Encephalocele

- Contains meninges with or without brain tissue; always has an intracranial connection
- **Frontoethmoidal** type associated with craniofacial deformity
- **Basal** type presents intranasally through skull base defects
- Management: combined transnasal and neurosurgical approach; ventriculoperitoneal shunting may be needed pre-operatively
- Endoscopic repair of defect with temporalis fascia, mucosa, or composite turbinate grafts

## Part C — Paediatric Rhinosinusitis

### Differences from Adult CRS

Key differences between paediatric and adult rhinosinusitis as summarized in EPOS:

- **Immunity:** Immature in children — defective response to polysaccharide antigens (IgG2, IgA); improves spontaneously after age 6–8 years
- **Microbiology:** Higher rates of H. influenzae (40%), M. catarrhalis (24%), S. pneumoniae (50%) in children vs adults
- **History:** Self-limited in children (improves after 6–8 years); no spontaneous improvement in adults
- **Histology:** Mainly neutrophilic in children (vs eosinophilic in adults)
- **Endoscopy:** Polyps rare in children (except CF); frequently present in adults
- **CT:** More diffuse sinusitis in younger children

### Aetiology and Predisposing Factors

- **Viral URTI:** Most common precipitant — 8% of URTIs complicated by acute RS in children aged 6–35 months
- **Adenoid hypertrophy and biofilms:** Key role in children
- **Allergy:** Atopy is a significant predisposing factor
- **Immunodeficiency:** Including IgA and IgG subclass deficiency
- **Cystic fibrosis and primary ciliary dyskinesia:** Must be excluded in refractory cases
- **Gastro-oesophageal reflux**
- **Day care attendance:** Markedly higher rates of RS

### EPOS Definition (Paediatric)

Two or more symptoms (one must be nasal blockage OR nasal discharge):
- ± facial pain/pressure
- ± cough (replaces anosmia in the adult definition)

For <12 weeks = acute; ≥12 weeks = chronic.

### Management

**Acute Paediatric Rhinosinusitis:**
- Most cases resolve spontaneously — supportive care (saline irrigation, analgesia)
- Antibiotics reserved for persistent symptoms >10 days, worsening after initial improvement, or severe onset (high fever >39°C with purulent discharge >3 days)
- First-line: amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate
- Intranasal corticosteroids as adjunctive therapy

**Chronic Paediatric Rhinosinusitis:**
- **Medical:** Prolonged courses of antibiotics, intranasal steroids, saline irrigations
- **Adenoidectomy:** First-line surgical intervention — effective in 50–75% of children with CRS
- **[[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]]:** Reserved for failure of adenoidectomy and maximal medical therapy; conservative approach in children (minimal tissue removal)
- Investigations: CT only when surgical planning; screen for immunodeficiency, CF, and PCD in refractory cases

## Part D — Silent Sinus Syndrome

### Definition

Silent sinus syndrome (also called "**imploding antrum**") is chronic maxillary sinus atelectasis resulting from obstruction of the osteomeatal complex, leading to painless **enophthalmos** and facial asymmetry.

### Aetiology

The condition originates from obstruction of the maxillary sinus ostium leading to **hypoventilation** of the maxillary sinus. In the enclosed cavity, air resorption creates a **subatmospheric negative pressure** (vacuum effect). This results in:

- Accumulation of fluid and mucosal thickening
- Progressive inward collapse of the sinus walls
- Retraction of the orbital floor (inferior orbital wall) → enophthalmos and hypoglobus

### Clinical Features

- **Painless enophthalmos** — often the presenting complaint
- **Hypoglobus** — inferior displacement of the globe
- **Facial asymmetry** — malar flattening on affected side
- Often **incidental finding** on imaging for unrelated symptoms
- Usually **unilateral**
- No history of trauma or sinusitis (hence "silent")

### Imaging

- **CT scan:** Contracted, opacified maxillary sinus with inward bowing of all sinus walls, especially the orbital floor; uncinate process lateralized against the infundibulum; reduction in sinus volume

### Treatment

1. **Endoscopic uncinectomy and middle meatal antrostomy:** Restores ventilation and equalizes pressure — essential first step
2. **Orbital floor reconstruction:** If significant enophthalmos persists after sinus re-aeration; titanium mesh or resorbable plate via subciliary or transconjunctival approach
3. Some patients show spontaneous improvement in globe position after sinus re-aeration alone

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Nasal dermoid is the most common midline nasal mass (1–3% of all dermoids); 4–45% have intracranial extension
> 2. The three congenital midline nasal masses are dermoid, glioma, and encephalocele — differentiated by Furstenberg test, transillumination, and response to crying
> 3. Biopsy is CONTRAINDICATED in suspected encephalocele — MRI is essential before any surgical intervention
> 4. Nasal glioma is better termed "glial heterotopia" — 15% retain a fibrous stalk to the brain
> 5. Paediatric rhinosinusitis is mainly neutrophilic (vs eosinophilic in adults) and is self-limiting after age 6–8 years
> 6. Adenoidectomy is the first-line surgical intervention for paediatric CRS
> 7. FESS in children should be conservative — minimal tissue removal
> 8. Viral URTI is the most common precipitant of acute paediatric RS — 8% of URTIs complicated by ARS
> 9. Silent sinus syndrome results from chronic maxillary sinus ostium obstruction with negative pressure causing wall collapse
> 10. Silent sinus syndrome presents with painless enophthalmos — treatment is endoscopic antrostomy ± orbital floor reconstruction


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Always obtain MRI before any intervention** on a midline nasal mass in a child — to rule out intracranial connection
> - **Furstenberg test positive** (mass enlarges with IJV compression) = encephalocele until proven otherwise
> - **Exclude CF and PCD** in any child with refractory rhinosinusitis or nasal polyps
> - **Adenoidectomy before FESS** — resolves CRS in 50–75% of children without the need for sinus surgery
> - **Silent sinus syndrome is painless** — patients typically present to ophthalmology, not ENT; the key is to recognize the maxillary sinus pathology on CT
