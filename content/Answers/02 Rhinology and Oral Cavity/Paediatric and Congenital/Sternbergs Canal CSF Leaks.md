# Sternberg's Canal and Spontaneous CSF Leaks

**Question:** *Describe Sternberg's canal and its clinical significance in the context of spontaneous [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]].*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapters 54 (Pituitary Fossa) & 108 (CSF Leaks)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Sternberg's canal (lateral craniopharyngeal canal) represents a persistent embryological defect in the lateral wall of the sphenoid sinus at the junction of the body and greater wing. This area corresponds to the lateral recess of a well-pneumatized sphenoid sinus and is a recognized site of spontaneous CSF leak. Scott-Brown's discusses spontaneous CSF leaks extensively and identifies the lateral recess of the sphenoid as a key site of herniation and leakage, directly related to the anatomical deficiency that Sternberg's canal represents.

---

## PART A: EMBRYOLOGY AND ANATOMY

### Embryological Basis

During fetal development, the sphenoid bone forms from multiple ossification centres. The junction between the body of the sphenoid (derived from cartilaginous precursors) and the greater wing (derived from membranous ossification) may fail to completely fuse, leaving a persistent bony defect - Sternberg's canal (lateral craniopharyngeal canal).

### Anatomical Location

The canal is located at the junction of:
- The body of the sphenoid
- The greater wing of the sphenoid
- The pterygoid process

This corresponds to the **lateral recess of the sphenoid sinus** when pneumatization extends laterally. The area lies in close proximity to:

- The foramen rotundum (transmitting V2)
- The vidian canal (transmitting the vidian nerve)
- The pterygopalatine fossa
- The floor of the middle cranial fossa

### Relationship to Sphenoid Pneumatization

Scott-Brown's (Chapter 54) classifies sphenoid pneumatization into sellar (~80%), pre-sellar (~20%), and conchal (~2%) types. In well-pneumatized sphenoid sinuses (sellar and post-sellar types), the lateral recess extends into the greater wing of the sphenoid, making the bone at Sternberg's canal location extremely thin or deficient - creating a potential site for meningeal herniation and CSF leakage.

---

## PART B: SPONTANEOUS CSF LEAKS - PATHOGENESIS

Scott-Brown's (Chapter 108) describes the current understanding of spontaneous CSF leaks:

### Association with Benign Intracranial Hypertension

Spontaneous CSF leaks are associated with middle-aged women with raised body mass index and represent a variant of **benign intracranial hypertension (BIH)**. These patients have generally elevated intracranial pressure due to poor CSF resorption by the arachnoid villi.

### Mechanism

The increased intracranial pressure with associated pulsatile forces produces thinning of bone most evident in the weakest areas of the skull base. Herniation of meninges and brain, often with CSF leakage, most often occurs at:

1. **Lateral recess of a well-pneumatized sphenoid** (site of Sternberg's canal)
2. Area of the **lateral lamella of the cribriform plate**
3. **Ethmoid roof**

These may be multiple sites of weakness of the skull base.

### Radiological Features of Raised Intracranial Pressure

- Empty sella
- Enlarged ventricles
- Diffuse erosion of the skull base

---

## PART C: CLINICAL FEATURES AND DIAGNOSIS

### Presentation

- Unilateral clear watery rhinorrhoea, often positional
- May present with recurrent meningitis
- Risk of meningitis: quoted as 10% per year (older data) to 0.3% per year per active leak (more recent data)
- In a study of 11 patients with 190 years of active leaks, 21 episodes of meningitis occurred (19% overall risk), mostly in the first year

### Investigation

As described in Scott-Brown's Chapter 108:

- **CT scanning:** High-resolution CT identifies bony defects in the skull base; in spontaneous leaks, may show multiple areas of thinning
- **MRI:** Recommended when sinus opacity is adjacent to a skull base defect; identifies meningoencephalocele
- **Intrathecal fluorescein:** Helpful in localizing the exact location of active leakage, particularly when multiple cranial base defects are present
- **Beta-trace protein assay:** Can detect occult CSF fistulae; 3% incidence of occult leaks found in one study of post-surgical patients
- **Lumbar puncture:** Performed after surgical repair to confirm elevated intracranial pressure (cannot be measured pre-operatively as persistent leak reduces pressure)

---

## PART D: MANAGEMENT

### General Principles

- Repair of a newly diagnosed CSF leak is not an absolute surgical emergency
- Time can be taken to properly investigate the patient
- Patient advised not to blow their nose, to sneeze with mouth open, and not to use CPAP
- Surgical repair should be performed at earliest reasonable opportunity due to meningitis risk

### Endoscopic Repair

Endoscopic closure achieves remarkable results:
- **90% success rate** at initial attempt
- Up to **97% with revision**
- This compares favourably with open approaches (some series as low as 60% success)

### Repair of Lateral Sphenoid Recess Leaks

CSF leaks in the lateral recess of well-pneumatized sphenoid sinuses may be best approached by the **transantral transpterygoid approach** (described by Bolger). This approach provides access to the lateral recess which may be difficult to reach through a standard transsphenoidal route.

### Reconstruction Techniques

- Multi-layer skull base reconstruction
- Areas of substantial skull base dehiscence may benefit from reinforcement with bone or cartilage graft
- Initially supported by an inflated Foley catheter balloon
- Vascularized flaps: posterior septal (nasoseptal) flap, inferior turbinate and nasal floor flaps

### Post-operative Management

- Oral **acetazolamide** for elevated intracranial pressure
- Selected patients may benefit from **VP shunting**
- **Gastric bypass** for morbid obesity has been described
- Pneumococcal vaccination (23-valent polysaccharide or 7-valent conjugate) if antibody titres are low

### Prognosis

- Spontaneous leaks are most likely to recur compared to other aetiologies
- **21% recurrence** for spontaneous leaks overall
- **46% recurrence** if radiological signs of raised intracranial pressure are present
- Only **2% recurrence** for non-spontaneous leaks
- Re-leakage may occur at the repair site or at other weak areas of the skull base

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Sternberg's canal is a persistent embryological defect at the junction of the body and greater wing of the sphenoid, corresponding to the lateral recess of a well-pneumatized sphenoid sinus
> 2. Spontaneous CSF leaks are associated with middle-aged obese women and represent a variant of benign intracranial hypertension
> 3. Three commonest sites of spontaneous leak: lateral sphenoid recess, lateral lamella of cribriform plate, and ethmoid roof
> 4. Elevated intracranial pressure causes pulsatile bone erosion at the weakest skull base areas
> 5. Endoscopic repair achieves 90% success at first attempt, up to 97% with revision
> 6. Transantral transpterygoid approach (Bolger) is best for lateral sphenoid recess leaks
> 7. Spontaneous leaks have the highest recurrence rate (21% overall, 46% with raised ICP signs)
> 8. Intrathecal fluorescein helps localize exact leak site when multiple defects are present
> 9. Post-repair lumbar puncture is needed to diagnose underlying raised intracranial pressure
> 10. Meningitis risk is the principal motivation for CSF leak repair; even today, meningitis carries a definite mortality rate


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Multiple sites of skull base weakness may coexist in spontaneous leaks - always image the entire skull base
> - Cannot measure intracranial pressure pre-operatively when there is an active CSF leak as the leak reduces the pressure
> - Oral acetazolamide post-repair helps address the underlying elevated ICP and reduce recurrence risk
> - Long-term follow-up is essential; the longest reported interval to recurrence after repair was 28 years (Mayo Clinic study)
> - Even occult CSF leaks (detected by beta-trace protein) may occur in 3% of routine sinus surgery cases
