# Anterior Ethmoidal Artery Anatomy, Preoperative Embolization and Cavernous Sinus

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - - *Q114 (Oct 2024): Describe the anatomy and course of the anterior ethmoidal artery. Discuss the role of preoperative embolization in sinonasal tumours.
> - 2014-8: Describe the relations of the cavernous sinus. Discuss the applied surgical anatomy of the cavernous sinus.
> - Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 87 (Anatomy of the Nose), Chapter 106 ([[Epistaxis Management|Epistaxis]]); Volume 2, Chapter 98 (Anatomy of the Skull Base), Chapter 100 (Vascular Assessment and Management)
> - ## INTRODUCTION
> - ## PART A: ANTERIOR ETHMOIDAL ARTERY — ANATOMY AND COURSE
> - (itself from the internal carotid artery). Its course is as follows:
> - 3. Enters the **anterior ethmoidal canal** at the frontoethmoidal suture line
> - A **meningeal branch** (supplies anterior cranial fossa dura)
> - A larger branch to the **nasal roof, olfactory cleft and superior turbinate
> - Smaller** than the AEA
> - Present in only **80% of individuals
> - Runs **above** the superior oblique muscle (unlike the AEA which runs under it)
> - Enters the posterior ethmoidal foramen, which lies **5 mm anterior to the optic canal** and **10–15 mm behind the AEA foramen
> - Accompanied by the **sphenoethmoidal nerve** (branch of nasociliary nerve)
> - Divides into a meningeal branch and a branch to the posterosuperior nasal cavity, olfactory sulcus and sphenoethmoidal recess
> - in the head and neck. Shaheen demonstrated that the ethmoidal artery territory is much smaller than previously thought:
> - Sphenopalatine artery branches supply the superior meatus and superior turbinate
> - Ethmoidal arteries are relatively narrow calibre
> - The AEA (the larger of the two) is absent in some individuals
> - ## PART B: PREOPERATIVE EMBOLIZATION OF SINONASAL TUMOURS
> - due to its origin from the ophthalmic artery — embolization carries unacceptable risk of blindness. For tumours receiving AEA supply, **intraoperative ligation at the medial orbital wall** is the alternative approach.
> - Particles >150 μm recommended** — particles <80 μm can traverse vasa nervorum and cause cranial nerve palsies
> - Non-visualized anastomoses exist at 50–80 μm diameter
> - Common agents: PVA (particulate), Embosphere (calibrated microspheres), NBCA (liquid glue), Onyx (liquid polymer)
> - Perform embolization **24–48 hours before surgery** — beyond 48 hours, revascularization occurs
> - Blindness is the **most feared complication** (ophthalmic artery territory)
> - Other complications: skin necrosis (pinna particularly susceptible), cranial nerve palsy, stroke
> - ## PART C: CAVERNOUS SINUS — ANATOMY AND RELATIONS
> - anteriorly to the **apex of the petrous temporal bone** posteriorly. Its walls are formed as follows:
> - Medial and inferior walls**: deep grooves into the body and base of the greater wing of the sphenoid
> - Medial, upper and lateral walls**: fold of the inner layer of dura, medially continuous with the **diaphragma sellae
> - Posterolateral wall**: medial to the anterior parts of the trigeminal (Meckel's) cave, containing the trigeminal ganglion
> - of the sinus are the ICA and CN VI — these lie **within** the sinus (not in the wall), making CN VI the most vulnerable nerve in cavernous sinus pathology.
> - (a small posterior extension of the lesser wing of the sphenoid). Here, just posterior to the optic canals, the ICA perforates the dura to enter the middle cranial fossa and divides into terminal branches.
> - Receives blood from the orbit and cerebral hemispheres
> - Drains via the **superior petrosal sinus** → sigmoid sinus
> - Drains via the **inferior petrosal sinus** → internal jugular vein
> - ## PART D: SURGICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF THE CAVERNOUS SINUS
> - to the cavernous sinus, causing cavernous sinus thrombosis (CST).
> - Presents with: proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, CN VI palsy (most common — nerve lies within the sinus)
> - May be bilateral (intracavernous connections)
> - Management: broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + anticoagulation
> - NPC invasion**: [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]] characteristically invades through the foramen lacerum into the cavernous sinus — CN VI palsy is an important presenting feature
> - Transphenoidal surgery**: the cavernous sinus lies lateral to the sphenoid sinus — dehiscence of the lateral sphenoid wall exposes the ICA within the cavernous sinus
> - ICA injury during ESS**: catastrophic complication; management options include the Valentine-Wormald crushed muscle patch technique and endovascular stenting
> - Embolization access**: the cavernous sinus is accessible for diagnostic and therapeutic catheterization via the inferior petrosal sinus
> - > [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> - > [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Embolization for JNA reduces blood loss from ~2000 mL to <1000 mL — direct percutaneous Onyx injection is superior to transarterial embolization for this tumour
> - Particles <80 μm must be avoided during embolization as they traverse vasa nervorum causing cranial nerve palsies — particles >150 μm are recommended
> - NPC characteristically invades through the foramen lacerum into the cavernous sinus — CN VI palsy is an important presenting sign of advanced NPC
> - The AEA may hang freely in a mesentery across a well-pneumatized suprabulla recess during FESS, increasing the risk of intraoperative injury and retraction of the cut artery into the orbit
> - Cavernous sinus thrombosis from sinonasal infection remains a life-threatening emergency — bilateral involvement suggests intracavernous communication and carries a worse prognosis
