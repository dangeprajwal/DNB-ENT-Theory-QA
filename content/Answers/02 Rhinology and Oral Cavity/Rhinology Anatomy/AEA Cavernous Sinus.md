# Anterior Ethmoidal Artery Anatomy, Preoperative Embolization and Cavernous Sinus

**Questions:**

- Q114 (Oct 2024): Describe the anatomy and course of the anterior ethmoidal artery. Discuss the role of preoperative embolization in sinonasal tumours.
- 2014-8: Describe the relations of the cavernous sinus. Discuss the applied surgical anatomy of the cavernous sinus.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 87 (Anatomy of the Nose), Chapter 106 ([[Epistaxis Management|Epistaxis]]); Volume 2, Chapter 98 (Anatomy of the Skull Base), Chapter 100 (Vascular Assessment and Management)

## INTRODUCTION

The anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) is a critical landmark in endoscopic sinus and skull base surgery. Its course through the anterior skull base makes it vulnerable during [[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]], while its origin from the internal carotid system precludes safe embolization. The cavernous sinus is a dural venous sinus containing vital neurovascular structures, making it surgically significant in skull base and transphenoidal approaches. Understanding the detailed anatomy of both structures is essential for safe rhinological and skull base surgery.

---

## PART A: ANTERIOR ETHMOIDAL ARTERY — ANATOMY AND COURSE

The AEA is a branch of the **ophthalmic artery** (itself from the internal carotid artery). Its course is as follows:

1. Arises within the orbit
2. Runs **under the superior oblique muscle**
3. Enters the **anterior ethmoidal canal** at the frontoethmoidal suture line
4. Traverses the ethmoid labyrinth and nasal cavity
5. Terminates in the region of the **ethmoid fovea**, dividing into:
   - A **meningeal branch** (supplies anterior cranial fossa dura)
   - A larger branch to the **nasal roof, olfactory cleft and superior turbinate**

### The 24-12-6 Rule

| Landmark | Distance | From |
|----------|----------|------|
| AEA | 24 mm | Anterior lacrimal crest |
| PEA | 12 mm | Behind AEA |
| Optic nerve | 6 mm | Behind PEA |

This rule provides a reliable intraoperative guide to the position of the ethmoidal arteries relative to the anterior lacrimal crest, measured along the frontoethmoidal suture line.

### Posterior Ethmoidal Artery (PEA)

- **Smaller** than the AEA
- Present in only **80% of individuals**
- Runs **above** the superior oblique muscle (unlike the AEA which runs under it)
- Enters the posterior ethmoidal foramen, which lies **5 mm anterior to the optic canal** and **10–15 mm behind the AEA foramen**
- Accompanied by the **sphenoethmoidal nerve** (branch of nasociliary nerve)
- Divides into a meningeal branch and a branch to the posterosuperior nasal cavity, olfactory sulcus and sphenoethmoidal recess

### ICA–ECA Anastomosis in the Nasal Cavity

The nasal cavity is the **principal site of ICA–ECA anastomosis** in the head and neck. Shaheen demonstrated that the ethmoidal artery territory is much smaller than previously thought:

- Sphenopalatine artery branches supply the superior meatus and superior turbinate
- Ethmoidal arteries are relatively narrow calibre
- The AEA (the larger of the two) is absent in some individuals

## PART B: PREOPERATIVE EMBOLIZATION OF SINONASAL TUMOURS

### Principles

The fundamental aim of embolization is **complete obliteration of the vascular bed**, not just proximal feeding artery occlusion. Proximal occlusion alone induces collateral recruitment, rendering embolization ineffective and making subsequent embolization more difficult.

### Indications and Common Tumour Types

| Tumour | Blood Supply | Key Points |
|--------|-------------|------------|
| [[Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma|JNA]] | Internal maxillary artery | Blood loss reduced from ~2000 mL to <1000 mL; percutaneous Onyx superior |
| [[Glomus Tumour|Paraganglioma]] | Ascending pharyngeal a. | PVA for tumours ≥3 cm; AICA/PICA supply = intradural, contraindicated |
| Meningioma | Dual ICA + ECA supply | Most effective when meningeal supply is exclusive |

### AEA and Embolization

The AEA **cannot be safely embolized** due to its origin from the ophthalmic artery — embolization carries unacceptable risk of blindness. For tumours receiving AEA supply, **intraoperative ligation at the medial orbital wall** is the alternative approach.

### Embolic Materials

- **Particles >150 μm recommended** — particles <80 μm can traverse vasa nervorum and cause cranial nerve palsies
- Non-visualized anastomoses exist at 50–80 μm diameter
- Common agents: PVA (particulate), Embosphere (calibrated microspheres), NBCA (liquid glue), Onyx (liquid polymer)

### Timing and Complications

- Perform embolization **24–48 hours before surgery** — beyond 48 hours, revascularization occurs
- Blindness is the **most feared complication** (ophthalmic artery territory)
- Other complications: skin necrosis (pinna particularly susceptible), cranial nerve palsy, stroke

## PART C: CAVERNOUS SINUS — ANATOMY AND RELATIONS

The cavernous sinus extends from the **apex of the orbit** anteriorly to the **apex of the petrous temporal bone** posteriorly. Its walls are formed as follows:

- **Medial and inferior walls**: deep grooves into the body and base of the greater wing of the sphenoid
- **Medial, upper and lateral walls**: fold of the inner layer of dura, medially continuous with the **diaphragma sellae**
- **Posterolateral wall**: medial to the anterior parts of the trigeminal (Meckel's) cave, containing the trigeminal ganglion

### Contents and Relations

| Location | Structure |
|----------|-----------|
| Lateral wall (embedded) | CN III, CN IV, CN V1, CN V2 |
| Within the sinus | CN VI + internal carotid artery |
| Medial wall | Continuous with diaphragma sellae; pituitary gland medially |
| Posterolateral | Meckel's cave (trigeminal ganglion) |

The **main contents** of the sinus are the ICA and CN VI — these lie **within** the sinus (not in the wall), making CN VI the most vulnerable nerve in cavernous sinus pathology.

### Course of the ICA Through the Cavernous Sinus

The ICA emerges from the petrous apex through the **superior aspect of the foramen lacerum**, then projects upwards, forwards and medially to the **anterior clinoid process** (a small posterior extension of the lesser wing of the sphenoid). Here, just posterior to the optic canals, the ICA perforates the dura to enter the middle cranial fossa and divides into terminal branches.

### Venous Drainage

- Receives blood from the orbit and cerebral hemispheres
- Drains via the **superior petrosal sinus** → sigmoid sinus
- Drains via the **inferior petrosal sinus** → internal jugular vein

## PART D: SURGICAL SIGNIFICANCE OF THE CAVERNOUS SINUS

### Danger Area of the Face

The upper lip and nose are the danger area of the face — infections may be transmitted in a **retrograde fashion through a valveless venous system** to the cavernous sinus, causing cavernous sinus thrombosis (CST).

### Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis

- Presents with: proptosis, chemosis, ophthalmoplegia, CN VI palsy (most common — nerve lies within the sinus)
- May be bilateral (intracavernous connections)
- Management: broad-spectrum IV antibiotics + anticoagulation

### Relevance to Sinonasal and Skull Base Surgery

- **NPC invasion**: [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]] characteristically invades through the foramen lacerum into the cavernous sinus — CN VI palsy is an important presenting feature
- **Transphenoidal surgery**: the cavernous sinus lies lateral to the sphenoid sinus — dehiscence of the lateral sphenoid wall exposes the ICA within the cavernous sinus
- **ICA injury during ESS**: catastrophic complication; management options include the Valentine-Wormald crushed muscle patch technique and endovascular stenting
- **Embolization access**: the cavernous sinus is accessible for diagnostic and therapeutic catheterization via the inferior petrosal sinus

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The AEA arises from the ophthalmic artery, runs under the superior oblique muscle, and traverses the anterior ethmoidal canal — the 24-12-6 rule locates it 24 mm behind the anterior lacrimal crest
> 2. The PEA is smaller than the AEA, present in only 80% of individuals, and lies 5 mm anterior to the optic canal
> 3. The nasal cavity is the principal site of ICA–ECA anastomosis in the head and neck — the ethmoidal artery territory is smaller than traditionally taught
> 4. Preoperative embolization aims for complete vascular bed obliteration — proximal feeding artery occlusion alone induces collateral recruitment and is inadequate
> 5. The AEA cannot be safely embolized due to its proximity to the ophthalmic artery — intraoperative ligation at the medial orbital wall is the alternative
> 6. The cavernous sinus extends from the orbital apex to the petrous apex — CN III, IV, V1 and V2 are embedded in the lateral wall while CN VI and ICA lie within the sinus
> 7. CN VI is the most commonly affected nerve in cavernous sinus pathology because it traverses within the sinus itself, not within the protective lateral wall
> 8. The ICA enters the cavernous sinus through the superior aspect of the foramen lacerum and exits at the anterior clinoid process to enter the middle cranial fossa
> 9. Cavernous sinus drains via superior and inferior petrosal sinuses — the posterolateral wall is medial to Meckel's cave containing the trigeminal ganglion
> 10. Upper lip and nose constitute the danger area of the face — valveless venous connections allow retrograde spread of infection to the cavernous sinus


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Embolization for JNA reduces blood loss from ~2000 mL to <1000 mL — direct percutaneous Onyx injection is superior to transarterial embolization for this tumour
> - Particles <80 μm must be avoided during embolization as they traverse vasa nervorum causing cranial nerve palsies — particles >150 μm are recommended
> - NPC characteristically invades through the foramen lacerum into the cavernous sinus — CN VI palsy is an important presenting sign of advanced NPC
> - The AEA may hang freely in a mesentery across a well-pneumatized suprabulla recess during FESS, increasing the risk of intraoperative injury and retraction of the cut artery into the orbit
> - Cavernous sinus thrombosis from sinonasal infection remains a life-threatening emergency — bilateral involvement suggests intracavernous communication and carries a worse prognosis
