# Anatomy of the Orbit

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the anatomy of the orbit with diagrams.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 111 (Disorders of the Orbit) & Chapter 87 (Anatomy of the Nose and Paranasal Sinuses)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Given the intimate relationship of the orbit to the paranasal sinuses, a thorough understanding of orbital anatomy is essential for the otorhinolaryngologist. The orbit serves as a surgical corridor through the sinuses and is commonly involved in rhinosinusitis complications, thyroid eye disease, optic nerve compression, and orbital neoplasms. The orbit is a quadrilateral pyramid with its base facing forwards, laterally and slightly inferiorly. It contains the globe, extraocular muscles, nerves, vessels and other associated structures including the lacrimal apparatus. The average volume in the adult Caucasian orbit is 30 mL.

---

## PART A: BONY ORBIT

### Contributing Bones

The orbit receives contribution from 7 distinct bones, each making unique contributions to the orbital walls:

- Frontal bone
- Zygomatic bone
- Maxillary bone
- Ethmoid bone
- Lacrimal bone
- Sphenoid bone (greater and lesser wings)
- Palatine bone

### Medial Wall

The medial wall is most relevant to otorhinolaryngologists due to the proximity of the paranasal sinuses. From anterior to posterior:

- **Anterior lacrimal crest** - Originates at the frontal process of the maxilla
- **Lacrimal bone** - Makes up the second half of the lacrimal sac fossa and the posterior lacrimal crest
- **Lamina papyracea** - Arises from the ethmoid bone; makes up the majority of the medial orbital wall; paper-thin bone overlying the ethmoid sinuses
- **Sphenoid body** - Thick bone forming the most posterior portion of the medial wall, adjoining the optic canal

**Key landmarks on the medial wall:**

- **Anterior ethmoid foramen** - 20-25 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest
- **Posterior ethmoid foramen** - 30-35 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest
- Both foramina are found in the superior aspect of the orbit along the fronto-ethmoidal suture line

### Superior Wall (Roof)

The roof of the orbit is formed primarily by the frontal bone. Key structures include:

- **Supraorbital notch or foramen** - Transmits supraorbital nerves and vessels
- **Trochlear fovea** - A small depression lying close to the superomedial orbital margin; anchors the connective tissue sling (trochlea) for the tendinous portion of the superior oblique muscle
- Posteriorly, the lesser wing of the sphenoid contributes to the roof

### Inferior Wall (Floor)

The floor is formed by:

- **Orbital surface of the maxilla** (major contribution)
- **Zygomatic bone**
- **Palatine bone** (orbital process)

**Key features:**

- **Infraorbital groove** - Runs approximately at the midpoint of the orbit floor, containing the maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve (V2), leading to the infraorbital foramen
- Medial to the infraorbital nerve, the orbital floor is relatively thin and fractures more easily than the lateral aspect

### Lateral Wall

Formed by:

- **Zygomatic process of the frontal bone**
- **Orbital surface of the zygoma**
- **Greater wing of the sphenoid**

These bones join at the zygomaticosphenoid suture line, creating the thickest bony wall of the orbit. The lateral tendons all attach to the lateral tubercle (Whitnall's tubercle), found 3-4 mm posterior to the lateral orbital rim.

---

## PART B: ORBITAL FISSURES AND FORAMINA

### Optic Canal

Located at the orbital apex, formed by the lesser wing of the sphenoid. Transmits the optic nerve (CN II) and ophthalmic artery.

### Superior Orbital Fissure

Located between the greater and lesser wings of the sphenoid. Transmits:

- Oculomotor nerve (CN III)
- Trochlear nerve (CN IV)
- Abducens nerve (CN VI)
- Ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve (V1) - lacrimal, frontal and nasociliary branches
- Superior ophthalmic vein

### Inferior Orbital Fissure

Located between the floor and lateral wall. Communicates with the pterygopalatine fossa medially and infratemporal fossa laterally. Transmits:

- Infraorbital nerve (branch of V2)
- Zygomatic nerve
- Infraorbital artery
- Inferior ophthalmic vein

### Other Foramina

- **Anterior ethmoid foramen** - Transmits anterior ethmoid artery, vein and nerve
- **Posterior ethmoid foramen** - Transmits posterior ethmoid artery, vein and nerve
- **Supraorbital foramen/notch** - Transmits supraorbital nerve and vessels
- **Infraorbital foramen** - Exit of infraorbital nerve and vessels onto the face
- **Nasolacrimal canal** - Drains the lacrimal sac to the inferior meatus

---

## PART C: ORBITAL CONTENTS

### Periorbita

The orbit is lined by the fibrous periorbita (periosteum). It is firmly attached to bone at the orbital margin, suture lines, the trochlear fossa, fissures, the lateral tubercle, the anterior lacrimal crest, and perforating vascular foramina. Other than these locations, the periorbita is loosely attached and easily dissected.

### Orbital Fat

The globe and other orbital tissue structures are surrounded by orbital fat, which cushions the globe and facilitates orbital movement. This is further aided by orbital connective tissue septae that slide over each other for smooth movement.

### Globe

The eye occupies the anterior portion of the orbit, supported by the suspensory ligament of Lockwood inferiorly.

### Extraocular Muscles

Six extraocular muscles control eye movement:

- **Superior rectus** - Elevates, adducts, intorts (CN III)
- **Inferior rectus** - Depresses, adducts, extorts (CN III)
- **Medial rectus** - Adducts (CN III)
- **Lateral rectus** - Abducts (CN VI)
- **Superior oblique** - Depresses, abducts, intorts (CN IV); passes through the trochlea
- **Inferior oblique** - Elevates, abducts, extorts (CN III)

All recti muscles originate from the annulus of Zinn (common tendinous ring) at the orbital apex.

### Lacrimal Gland

Situated in the lacrimal fossa in the superolateral orbit. Divided into orbital and palpebral lobes by the lateral horn of the levator aponeurosis.

---

## PART D: NASOLACRIMAL SYSTEM

The nasolacrimal drainage system consists of:

- **Superior and inferior canaliculi** - Begin at the punctum of each eyelid; initially run at 90 degrees to the lid margin for 1-2 mm before running parallel with the lid margin
- **Common canaliculus** - Formed after 10 mm by the joining of the two canaliculi; angles inferiorly and forward for 3-5 mm before entering the lacrimal sac
- **Lacrimal sac** - Protected in the concavity of the lacrimal fossa; fossa formed anteriorly by the harder frontal process of maxilla and posteriorly by the softer lacrimal bone
- **Nasolacrimal duct** - Exits from the inferior portion of the lacrimal sac; continues inferiorly for approximately 12 mm; enters the nose 10-15 mm behind the anterior end of the inferior turbinate and 16 mm above the floor of the nasal cavity at Hasner's valve

---

## PART E: NEUROVASCULAR SUPPLY

### Arterial Supply

- **Ophthalmic artery** - Branch of the internal carotid artery; enters through the optic canal; gives rise to:
  - Central retinal artery
  - Anterior and posterior ethmoid arteries
  - Lacrimal artery
  - Supraorbital and supratrochlear arteries
  - Dorsal nasal artery
- **Infraorbital artery** - Branch of the maxillary artery; enters via the inferior orbital fissure

### Venous Drainage

- **Superior ophthalmic vein** - Drains to the cavernous sinus
- **Inferior ophthalmic vein** - Drains to the cavernous sinus and pterygoid plexus via the inferior orbital fissure

### Nerve Supply

**Motor nerves:**

- Oculomotor nerve (CN III) - Superior and inferior divisions; supplies all extraocular muscles except lateral rectus and superior oblique; also supplies levator palpebrae superioris and carries parasympathetic fibres to the pupil
- Trochlear nerve (CN IV) - Supplies superior oblique
- Abducens nerve (CN VI) - Supplies lateral rectus

**Sensory nerves:**

- Ophthalmic nerve (V1) - Lacrimal, frontal and nasociliary branches
- Maxillary nerve (V2) - Infraorbital and zygomatic branches

**Autonomic nerves:**

- Parasympathetic fibres from CN III synapse in the ciliary ganglion and supply the sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle
- Sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion supply the dilator pupillae and Mueller's muscle

---

## PART F: RELATIONS TO PARANASAL SINUSES

- **Medial wall (lamina papyracea):** Separates orbit from ethmoid sinuses; paper-thin and easily breached during surgery or by infection
- **Floor:** Separates orbit from maxillary sinus; contains infraorbital nerve canal
- **Roof:** Separates orbit from [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] (anteriorly) and anterior cranial fossa (posteriorly)
- **Apex:** Relates to the sphenoid sinus and optic canal

These relationships explain why orbital complications (preseptal cellulitis, orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis) are the most common complications of acute rhinosinusitis.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Orbit is a quadrilateral pyramid formed by 7 bones with average volume of 30 mL
> 2. Lamina papyracea (ethmoid bone) forms majority of the medial wall; paper-thin, most vulnerable to sinusitis complications
> 3. Anterior ethmoid foramen at 20-25 mm, posterior ethmoid foramen at 30-35 mm from anterior lacrimal crest
> 4. The lateral wall (zygomaticosphenoid suture) is the thickest wall; the medial wall and floor are the thinnest
> 5. Infraorbital nerve runs in the floor; floor fractures more easily medial to this nerve
> 6. Superior orbital fissure transmits CN III, IV, VI, V1 and superior ophthalmic vein
> 7. Inferior orbital fissure communicates with the pterygopalatine fossa
> 8. All recti muscles originate from the annulus of Zinn at the orbital apex
> 9. Nasolacrimal duct opens into the inferior meatus 10-15 mm behind the anterior end of the inferior turbinate
> 10. The lacrimal fossa is formed by the hard frontal process of maxilla (anterior) and soft lacrimal bone (posterior)


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The lamina papyracea is the thinnest wall and the most common route for spread of ethmoid sinusitis into the orbit
> - The anterior ethmoid artery is a critical landmark during endoscopic sinus surgery - injury causes retro-orbital haematoma
> - The orbital floor fractures more easily medial to the infraorbital nerve than laterally
> - Knowledge of the fronto-ethmoidal suture line is essential - it indicates the level of the skull base during surgery
> - The periorbita is loosely attached except at suture lines, foramina and the orbital margin - this allows subperiosteal abscess formation
> - Hasner's valve at the nasolacrimal duct opening is a common site of congenital obstruction
