# Embryological Development of the Nose and Pns, Surgical Anatomy of the Uncinate Process and Lateral Wall, Intraoperative CSF Leak

## INTRODUCTION

The nose and paranasal sinuses develop from a complex series of mesenchymal processes during early embryogenesis. An understanding of this development is essential for appreciating congenital anomalies such as choanal atresia and cleft palate. The surgical anatomy of the lateral nasal wall, and the uncinate process in particular, forms the foundation of [[FESS Steps Complications|functional endoscopic sinus surgery]] (FESS). The uncinate process is the first structure encountered and removed during FESS, and its variable posterosuperior attachment determines the [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] drainage pathway. Intraoperative CSF leak is a recognized complication of FESS, occurring in approximately 0.5% of cases, and requires prompt recognition and repair.

## PART A: EMBRYOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENT OF THE NOSE

### External Nose and Nasal Cavity

The nose develops from several mesenchymal processes around the primitive mouth during the **4th week of gestation**. The sequence of events is as follows:

1. Collections of neural crest cells undergo proliferation and form the **nasal placodes** on the ventrolateral aspect of the frontonasal process
2. Sinking of the nasal placodes leads to formation of the **nasal pits**, which further deepen to form the **nasal sac**
3. Adjacent mesoderm cells proliferate to give rise to the **medial and lateral nasal prominences** of the frontonasal process, which surround the nasal pit and sac to eventually become the nares
4. The **maxillary processes** grow anteriorly and medially to fuse with the medial nasal prominences and frontonasal process, closing off the nasal pits to form separate nasal cavities
5. The primitive nasal cavity and mouth are initially separated by the **bucconasal membrane**
6. This membrane gradually thins as the nasal sacs extend posteriorly and eventually breaks down to form the **choanae**

**Failure of the bucconasal membrane to break down results in choanal atresia.**

### Derivatives of the Nasal Prominences

| Structure | Derivative |
|-----------|-----------|
| Frontal + maxillary processes | Lateral 2/3 upper lip, superior alveolar ridges, palatal shelves |
| Medial nasal prominences + maxillary process | Philtrum, medial crus of LLC |
| Lateral nasal prominences | Nasal bones, ULC, lateral crus of LLC |

## PART B: DEVELOPMENT OF THE PALATE AND PARANASAL SINUSES

### Palate Development

The **nasal septum** develops from the posterior midline growth of the frontonasal process in the root of the oral cavity, extending posteriorly to the opening of Rathke's pouch.

The **primary palate** begins to form anteriorly with fusion of the maxillary and frontonasal processes. As the nasal cavities enlarge, the **palatal processes** (derived from lateral maxillary mesoderm) grow medially towards each other and the nasal septum:

- Initially the palatal processes are **vertically oriented and lateral to the tongue**
- Further growth of the jaw and oral cavity enables the palatal processes to **migrate medially** towards the midline and fuse
- The fusion begins along the posterior margin of the primitive palate and progresses from **anterior to posterior** to form the **secondary palate**
- A midline dehiscence in the fusion of primary and secondary palate forms the **incisive foramen**
- Partial notching of the posterior hard palate represents incomplete fusion and may indicate a **submucous cleft**

### Paranasal Sinus Development

| Sinus | Development Timeline | Key Features |
|-------|---------------------|-------------|
| Maxillary | 7-10 weeks gestation; present at birth | Rapid growth until age 7; adult size 17-18 yrs; hypoplasia in up to 10% of CT scans |
| Ethmoid | 9-10 weeks; present at birth | All structures present at birth; continues to enlarge until puberty |
| Sphenoid | 12 weeks gestation | Pneumatization begins age 3; variable patterns (sellar 90%, pre-sellar 9%, conchal 1%) |
| Frontal | 16 weeks gestation | Absent at birth; visible radiologically by age 8; adult size by late teens |

### Maxillary Sinus

The maxillary sinus is the first sinus to appear (7th-10th weeks of gestation) as a shallow groove expanding from the primitive ethmoidal infundibulum into the mass of the maxilla. A small sinus cavity is present at birth. Rapid growth occurs during childhood until age 7, followed by gradual enlargement reaching final size by age 17-18 years. Maxillary sinus hypoplasia is present in up to 10% of CT scans. Radiographic diagnostic criteria for hypoplasia include enlargement of the vertical orbit, lateral position of the infraorbital neurovascular canal, elevated canine fossa, enlargement of the superior orbital fissure, and enlargement of the pterygopalatine fissure.

### Ethmoid Sinus

During the 9th and 10th weeks of gestation, a series of folds called **ethmoturbinals** separated by corresponding grooves appear in the lateral wall of the nasal capsule. Fusion of these folds leads to the development of crests, each with an ascending and descending portion. All permanent ethmoidal structures are present at birth. As a result, acute sinusitis in children often involves the ethmoid cavity, which can extend laterally through the lamina papyracea causing orbital complications.

Understanding the basic embryology of the four or five ethmoturbinals defines a series of lamellae that must be removed to pass from the anterior sinonasal cavity to the sphenoid sinus. In order from anterior to posterior:
- **FIRST:** Agger nasi (ascending portion) and uncinate process (descending portion)
- **SECOND:** Bulla ethmoidalis
- **THIRD:** Basal lamella of the middle turbinate
- **FOURTH:** Superior turbinate
- **FIFTH:** Supreme turbinate (if present)

### Sphenoid Sinus

The sphenoid sinus begins to develop in the 12th week of gestation as an evagination from the sphenoethmoidal recess. A small sphenoid sinus is present at birth with progressive enlargement starting at age 3 during pneumatization of the sphenoid bone. Three pneumatization patterns are described with reference to the sella turcica:

- **Sellar type (90%):** Pneumatization posterior to the sella turcica (most common)
- **Pre-sellar type (9%):** Pneumatization up to the anterior sella
- **Conchal type (1%):** Shallow bowl with minimal pneumatization and trabecular bone between sinus and sella

The sphenoid sinuses can also pneumatize laterally into the pterygoid root, resulting in a **lateral sphenoid recess** that exposes surrounding neurovascular structures.

### Frontal Sinus

The frontal sinus is the most variable sinus in terms of size and shape. Pneumatization of the frontal bone begins during the 16th week of gestation, originating from the anterior ethmoid complex. At birth, the frontal sinuses appear only as a small blind pocket indistinguishable from the anterior ethmoid air cells on imaging. The frontal sinuses are seen on most radiological studies by the age of 8 years. Significant frontal pneumatization does not occur until early adolescence and continues until 18 years of age. Relative proportions reach adult ratios by age 10-12 years.

## PART C: SURGICAL ANATOMY OF THE UNCINATE PROCESS

### Structure and Attachments

The uncinate process is a **sickle-shaped bone** that is the first structure removed during endoscopic sinus surgery. Its attachments are as follows:

- **Inferior attachment:** To the inferior turbinate and palatine bone
- **Anterosuperior attachment:** To the lacrimal bone
- **Posterosuperior attachment:** VARIABLE (discussed below)

### Posterosuperior Attachment and Frontal Drainage

The uncinate process can insert into the medial orbital wall, skull base or middle turbinate. Recent studies have demonstrated that the uncinate has **multiple attachments in more than 50% of cases** rather than a single attachment pattern. Classic teaching that describes three distinct attachments of the uncinate process which determines the direction of the frontal sinus drainage pathway is **neither surgically relevant nor accurate**.

- The uncinate process inserts onto the **medial orbital wall in 85% of cases**
- Thus, the frontal recess drainage pathway is **medial to the uncinate process in 85%** of cases
- An uncinate process with an isolated attachment to either the skull base or middle turbinate (without attachment to the medial orbital wall) occurs in **only 15%** of cases
- This 15% attachment pattern leads to a surgically obvious frontal drainage pathway located **lateral to the uncinate** that is easily identified at the time of surgery
- The surgical rule: the frontal recess is medial to the remnant uncinate process or 'vertical bar' in 85% of cases, with other attachments representing easy surgical arrangements

### Uncinate Process and Maxillary Sinus

- The true maxillary ostial opening is **covered by the uncinate process** and cannot be viewed endoscopically in a sinus cavity that has not been previously opened surgically
- The uncinate, together with a fold of mucosa called the **anterior and posterior fontanelle**, covers the opening to the maxillary sinus
- **Accessory ostia** may be present in the fontanelle that can be mistaken for the true maxillary ostium
- Failure to correctly identify the true ostium and connect it with the common sinus cavity results in **mucous recirculation** (mucous directed towards the natural opening along the mucociliary drainage pathway re-enters the sinus through the accessory ostium)

## PART D: LATERAL WALL ANATOMY - THE PARANASAL SURGICAL BOX

### Concept of the Surgical Box

Endoscopic sinus surgery is an exercise of anatomical dissection around fixed anatomical landmarks. The surgeon must identify certain key landmarks to delineate the limits of dissection. These landmarks are based on fixed anatomical structures rather than variable anatomy that can be distorted by disease or prior surgery.

The three key landmarks are:
1. **Maxillary sinus** (roof/orbital floor = first landmark)
2. **Medial orbital wall** (lamina papyracea = second landmark, identified after bulla removal)
3. **Sphenoid sinus/posterior skull base** (third landmark)

### Horizontal Component (Ethmoid Bulla to Sphenoid)

Boundaries of the horizontal portion of the paranasal surgical box:
- **Medial:** Middle turbinate
- **Lateral:** Medial orbital wall (lamina papyracea)
- **Inferior:** Floor of nose
- **Superior:** Skull base

The **surgical rule**: staying below or at the level of the maxillary sinus roof/orbital floor as dissection proceeds posteriorly will avoid the skull base. The maxillary sinus roof was below the level of the skull base in 100% of cases (mean distance 10.1 +/- 2.7 mm).

### Vertical Component (Frontal Recess)

Boundaries of the vertical portion of the paranasal surgical box:
- **Medial:** Intersinus septum and middle turbinate
- **Lateral:** Lamina papyracea
- **Anterior:** Nasofrontal beak
- **Posterior:** Skull base

### Ethmoid Bulla

The ethmoid bulla is the **largest and most consistent anterior ethmoid air cell**. It attaches to the lamina papyracea laterally and has variable attachments to the skull base and basal lamella. Complete removal of the ethmoid bulla is critical to define the medial orbital wall as a landmark.

### Middle Turbinate

The complex shape of the middle turbinate is divided into three segments:

| Segment | Plane | Attachment | Significance |
|---------|-------|-----------|-------------|
| Sagittal | Sagittal | Skull base at lateral lamella | Vertically oriented anterior portion |
| Coronal | Coronal | Creates basal lamella | Separates anterior from posterior ethmoid |
| Axial | Axial | Lateral nasal wall | Entry point of sphenopalatine artery branch |

- The **basal lamella** (coronal segment) separates the anterior ethmoid (draining via middle meatus) and posterior ethmoid (draining via superior meatus) cavities
- There is **no natural connection** between these two cavities
- The safe working distance from the skull base established by the maxillary sinus roof/orbital floor is used as a reference point to proceed through the basal lamella and enter the posterior ethmoid cavity

### Agger Nasi Cell

The agger nasi is the **anterior-most ethmoid air cell** and its medial border is formed by the uncinate process. The degree of pneumatization of the agger nasi influences the position of the superior uncinate process and thickness of the bony nasofrontal beak. It is the key to all approaches to the frontal sinus.

## PART E: INTRAOPERATIVE CSF LEAK DURING FESS - RECOGNITION AND MANAGEMENT

### Incidence and Sites of Vulnerability

A rate of serious complications in the order of 0.5% or less is generally quoted for endoscopic sinus surgery. CSF leaks diagnosed intraoperatively or postoperatively are included in this category.

The most likely anatomical sites for CSF leaks complicating sinus surgery are:
- **Lateral lamella of the cribriform plate** (thinnest point of the anterior skull base)
- **Anterior skull base** where weakened by the anterior ethmoid neurovascular bundle
- **Posterior ethmoid** where there may be confusion regarding the relationship between the last posterior ethmoid cell and the sphenoid sinus

### Keros Classification of Olfactory Fossa Depth

| Keros Type | Depth | Lateral Lamella | Risk Level |
|------------|-------|----------------|-----------|
| Type I | 1-3 mm | Short, thick | Low risk |
| Type II | 4-7 mm | Intermediate | Moderate risk |
| Type III | 8-16 mm | Long, thin | High risk |

The lateral lamella of the cribriform plate is the thinnest bone of the anterior skull base. A deep olfactory fossa (Keros Type III) means a longer, thinner lateral lamella that is more vulnerable to injury during FESS. The anterior ethmoid artery traverses intracranially into the olfactory fossa through the lateral lamella; when the artery lies in a mucosal mesentery and the olfactory fossa is deep, the risk of skull base injury is increased.

### Recognition of CSF Leak

- **Intraoperative signs:** Clear watery fluid in the surgical field that does not match the expected bleeding pattern; may see pulsatile flow
- **Posturally provoked:** Leaning forward may increase drainage
- **Confirmatory test:** Beta-2 transferrin assay (excellent sensitivity, specificity and positive/negative predictive values); the beta-2 transferrin isoform is present only in CSF, perilymph and aqueous humour
- **Alternative test:** Beta-trace protein (considered even better than beta-2 transferrin in some European studies)
- **Intrathecal fluorescein:** Bright green coloration confirms active leak site and can confirm adequacy of repair

### Management Protocol

**General Principle:** CSF leaks complicating sinus surgery diagnosed intraoperatively should be repaired under the same anaesthetic using local intranasal tissue.

**Immediate Steps:**
1. Identify the exact site of leakage
2. Assess the size of the defect
3. Perform multilayer repair

**Size-Based Repair Protocol:**

- **Small defect (<1 cm):** Free tissue grafts (fat, fascia) using **Bath Plug technique** (fat plug as inlay + overlay mucosal graft); technically straightforward with excellent results
- **Medium defect (1-3 cm):** **Underlay + overlay graft** with or without bone/cartilage buttress; bone or cartilage grafts are helpful for medium-sized defects to provide structural rigidity
- **Large defect (>3 cm):** **Vascularized [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|pedicled flap]]** (Hadad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap based on nasoseptal artery) with rigid buttress (semirigid bioabsorbable plate in patients with elevated BMI); multilayer skull base reconstruction supported by inflated Foley catheter balloon

**Adjuncts:**
- Gelfoam placed as a 'break-layer' under the repair to isolate from shearing forces during pack removal
- Merocel pack for support
- Fibrin glue to reinforce closure (shown to enhance graft adherence and higher burst pressures in animal models)

**Postoperative Management:**
- Head elevation
- Lumbar drain: controversial for routine use; employed when intrathecal fluorescein is used and in high-flow or large defects
- Avoid positive pressure mask ventilation (risk of pneumocephalus)
- Avoid nose blowing, straining, sneezing with mouth closed, CPAP
- Pneumococcal vaccination if antibody titres are low
- Prophylactic antibiotics: no strong evidence base

**Prognosis:**
- 90% success rate at initial endoscopic repair attempt
- Up to 97% success with revision surgery
- Spontaneous leaks have highest recurrence rate (21%, up to 46% with raised intracranial pressure)

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The frontal sinus is the last paranasal sinus to develop and is absent at birth; it is visible radiologically by age 8 but significant pneumatization does not occur until early adolescence, continuing until 18 years.
>
> - Mucous recirculation occurs when the true maxillary ostium is not connected with the surgically created antrostomy; the true ostium lies hidden behind the uncinate process and must be identified during FESS.
>
> - The sphenoid sinus has variable pneumatization described with reference to the sella turcica (sellar 90%, pre-sellar 9%, conchal 1%); the conchal type has trabecular bone between sinus and sella, making transphenoidal approaches more difficult.
>
> - The agger nasi cell is the anterior-most ethmoid cell and its degree of pneumatization influences the position of the superior uncinate process and the thickness of the nasofrontal beak; it is the key landmark for all frontal recess approaches.
>
> - Beta-2 transferrin is the confirmatory test for [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]]; it is present only in CSF, perilymph and aqueous humour, providing excellent sensitivity and specificity for the diagnosis of CSF leaks.
>
> - The five ethmoturbinal lamellae (agger nasi/uncinate, bulla ethmoidalis, basal lamella, superior turbinate, supreme turbinate) are the embryological basis of all ethmoid air cells and define the sequential landmarks encountered during complete sphenoethmoidectomy.
