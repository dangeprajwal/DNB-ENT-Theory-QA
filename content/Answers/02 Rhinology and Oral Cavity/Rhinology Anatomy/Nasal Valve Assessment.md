# Nasal Valve Anatomy, Assessment and Nasal Resistance

## INTRODUCTION

The nasal valves are the flow-limiting segments of the nasal airway and are critical to nasal airflow. The internal nasal valve represents the narrowest portion of the nasal cavity and is the primary determinant of nasal resistance. Understanding nasal valve anatomy is essential for evaluating nasal obstruction, planning septorhinoplasty, and interpreting objective nasal function tests. Nasal resistance contributes up to 50% of total airway resistance, and its measurement by rhinomanometry, acoustic rhinometry, or peak nasal inspiratory flow provides objective data to guide surgical and medical management.

**Note on Sternberg's canal:** This topic is covered in detail in the separate answer file `Sternbergs_Canal_CSF_Leaks.md`. Briefly, Sternberg's canal (lateral craniopharyngeal canal) is a persistent embryological defect at the junction of the lateral wall of the sphenoid sinus with the pterygoid process, representing failure of fusion of the basisphenoid and presphenoid ossification centres. It is a recognized pathway for spontaneous [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]] and skull base encephaloceles.

## PART A: EXTERNAL NASAL VALVE

### Definition

The external nasal valve is **not a true valve**. It is the area bounded by the alar cartilages, septum and columella at the entrance to the nasal vestibule.

### Boundaries

| Boundary | Structure |
|----------|-----------|
| Medial | Nasal septum |
| Lateral | Alar rim (lateral crus of LLC + sesamoid complex + fibrofatty tissue) |
| Inferior | Nasal sill |

### Nasal Cartilages

The nasal cartilages are hyaline cartilages that attach to the bones of the anterior nasal aperture:

- **Lower lateral cartilage (LLC):** Divided into medial, intermediate and lateral crus that form the natural arch of the nasal ala. The LLC determines tip shape, projection and alar rim contour.
- **Upper lateral cartilage (ULC):** Trapezoid-shaped cartilage that attaches to the dorsal septum in the midline, nasal bones cranially at the rhinion, and the LLC caudally via the scroll area.

The relationship and architecture of these cartilages form the external and internal nasal valves, which are critical to nasal airflow.

### Clinical Significance

Anatomical abnormalities or compromise in the structural integrity of external valve components can cause external valve narrowing, stenosis, or dynamic valve collapse that is exacerbated during inspiration.

- **Alar collapse** is a measure of external nasal valve collapse and must be identified pre-operatively
- Dynamic narrowing of the alar rim on forced inspiration should be assessed, noting that some degree of dynamic narrowing is normal
- Loss of lateral crus support (post-rhinoplasty, trauma, ageing) is a common cause of external valve compromise

### Muscles of the External Nose

The extension of the facial SMAS layer continues over the nose as numerous muscles that compress, dilate, depress or elevate the nostrils. These are all supplied by the facial nerve:
- **Elevators:** Procerus, levator labii superioris alaeque nasi, anomalous nasi
- **Depressors:** Alar nasalis, depressor septi nasi
- **Compressors:** Transverse nasalis, compressor narium minor
- **Dilator:** Dilator naris anterior (minor dilator)

The ala nasi muscle contracts during inspiration to prevent vestibular collapse; its paralysis (e.g., in [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]]) causes the nasal vestibule to collapse and increases nasal resistance.

## PART B: INTERNAL NASAL VALVE

### Definition

The internal nasal valve, in normal development, is the **narrowest portion of the nasal cavity**. It is the flow-limiting segment of the upper airway and is the primary determinant of nasal airflow resistance.

### Boundaries

| Boundary | Structure |
|----------|-----------|
| Medial | Nasal septum |
| Lateral (superior) | Caudal edge of upper lateral cartilage |
| Lateral (posterior) | Head of inferior turbinate |
| Inferior | Nasal floor |

### Apex Angle

- Approximately **10-15 degrees** in Caucasians
- **Wider** in non-Caucasian populations
- More acute angles (less than 15 degrees) are associated with nasal blockage and may result in dynamic external valve collapse
- A shallow valve height with an obtuse angle (more than 15 degrees) can also cause nasal restriction

### Dimensions

- Located approximately 2 cm posterior to the anterior nares
- Cross-sectional area ranges between 55-83 mm squared
- During quiet respiration, the velocity of airflow at the anterior valve ranges from 12 to 18 m/sec

### Pathophysiology

Changes in the relationship of any structure within this space can cause symptoms of nasal obstruction. Causes include:
- **Septal deviation** at the valve level
- **ULC weakness or medialization** (congenital, post-rhinoplasty, or traumatic)
- **Inferior turbinate hypertrophy** (even 1-2 mm enlargement significantly reduces airflow velocity from 0.89 to 0.42 m/s)
- **Cicatricial narrowing** (post-surgical or post-traumatic scarring)

The **Bernoulli effect** explains dynamic internal valve collapse: during forceful inspiration, the negative intraluminal pressure causes the compliant ULC to collapse towards the septum, narrowing the valve angle.

### Clinical Tests

**Cottle's manoeuvre:**
- Involves pulling on the soft tissues of the cheek to open the internal valve
- This is **non-specific** as it improves all nasal airways (affects both internal and external valve via their attachments)
- Not advocated as a usefully discriminant test

**Jobson Horne probe test (preferred):**
- A Jobson Horne probe is placed in the internal nasal valve to prevent collapse of the upper lateral cartilage
- Detects the specific effect of ULC support on inspiration
- More specific than Cottle's manoeuvre for identifying internal valve collapse

**Modified Cottle's test / Splinting tests:**
- Trial of Breathe Right strips or nasal vestibule stents (France's alar dilators, AirMax nasal splints, Max-Air sinuscones)
- Can have a positive effect on both valve areas and confirm anatomical deficiency

**Important:** Internal nasal valve assessment should be carried out during normal quiet respiration at rest; exaggerated effortful breathing is likely to precipitate transient internal valve collapse in the normal individual.

### Management of Internal Nasal Valve Narrowing

**Non-surgical:**
- External nasal valve dilators (Breathe Right strips, nasal stents)
- Nasal steroid medications to reduce mucosal congestion at the valve

**Surgical options:**
- **Spreader grafts:** Placed between the ULC and dorsal septum to widen the internal valve angle and increase cross-sectional area; considered the gold standard for internal valve repair
- **Butterfly grafts:** Cartilage grafts placed across the dorsum to open the internal valve
- **Flaring sutures:** Mattress sutures to open the internal valve angle
- **Alar batten grafts:** Strengthen the lateral nasal wall and external valve
- **Alar expansion sutures:** Address external valve collapse
- **Z-plasty of internal valve:** For narrowing caused by inappropriate scar contracture
- **Septoplasty:** Addressing septal deviation at the valve level
- **Turbinate reduction:** Reducing the head of the inferior turbinate to improve the valve cross-sectional area

Most nasal valve procedures will have an aesthetic effect on the nose; the patient should be warned of this prior to surgery.

## PART C: NASOFACIAL ANGLES AND RHINOPLASTY ASSESSMENT

### Powell and Humphrey's Facial Aesthetic Triangle

Powell and Humphrey described the ideal angles of the facial aesthetic triangle, based on dimensions first described by Leonardo da Vinci and extended by Albrecht Duerer:

| Angle | Ideal Range |
|-------|-------------|
| Nasofrontal (NF) | 115-135 degrees |
| Nasofacial (NFc) | 30-40 degrees |
| Nasomental (NM) | 120-132 degrees |
| Mentocervical (MC) | 80-95 degrees |

These facial proportions act as a guide and are helpful in planning rhinoplasty procedures, but should not be taken as absolute. Each rhinoplasty should respect the individual's wishes, gender and character, and these ideal measurements vary between ethnicities.

### Nasolabial Angle

The nasolabial angle determines nasal **tip rotation** along an arc:
- **Men:** 90-95 degrees
- **Women:** 95-105 degrees
- An acute nasolabial angle indicates an under-rotated (droopy) tip
- An excessively open nasolabial angle indicates an over-rotated tip

### Tip Projection

Ideal tip projection is determined using **Goode's ratio**: a line drawn from the alar-facial groove to the nasal tip should measure **0.55-0.60** of the distance from the nasion to the nasal tip.
- Ratio less than 0.55 = underprojected nose
- Ratio greater than 0.60 = overprojected nose

### Nasal Length

Nasal length is measured from nasion to tip, which equals the distance from stomium to menton. It can also be calculated as: **NT = TS x 1.6** (nasal tip to stomium distance multiplied by 1.6).

### Rule of Thirds and Fifths

- **Rule of thirds:** The face is divided into three approximately equal horizontal segments: trichion-glabella, glabella-subnasale, subnasale-menton
- **Rule of fifths:** The face is divided into five equal vertical widths, each approximately one eye width. The alar base should equal the intercanthal distance. The nose ideally occupies one-third of the facial length and one-fifth of its width.

### Columellar Show

- Ideally **3-5 mm** visible caudal septum in lateral view
- Greater columellar show may indicate a hanging columella or alar margin notching/retraction

### Key Facial Landmarks

| Landmark | Definition |
|----------|-----------|
| Trichion | Anterior hairline in the midline |
| Glabella | Most prominent point of forehead in midsagittal plane |
| Nasion | Deepest point of frontonasal angle |
| Rhinion | Midline junction of nasal bones and upper lateral cartilages |
| Subnasale | Junction of columella and upper lip in midsagittal plane |
| Pogonion | Deepest point on outer cortex of mandible |
| Gnathion | Most inferior point of the chin |
| Menton | Lowest point of mandibular symphysis |

### Additional Assessment Parameters

- **Fitzpatrick classification:** Used to assess skin type based on colour and reaction to sun exposure (Types I-VI)
- **Tip configurations:** Normal, boxy, bifid, bulbous, amorphous
- **Lip-chin relationship:** Horizontal distance from surface of upper lip to lower lip is normally around 2 mm; upper and lower lips rest on the nasomental line in an aesthetic face
- **Brow-tip line:** Should reveal a smooth curvilinear line from eyebrow to nasal tip; irregularities highlight sources of deformity

### Computer Imaging

Computer morphing of pre-operative photographs enhances communication with the patient and is associated with higher patient satisfaction. However, it must be clarified as a means of communication only and does not imply a specific guaranteed outcome.

## PART D: NASAL RESISTANCE AND ITS MEASUREMENT

### Nasal Resistance

Nasal resistance plays a crucial role in preventing collapse of the lower respiratory tract. It contributes up to **50% of total airway resistance**. As nasal resistance increases, it causes an increase in negative intrathoracic pressure, which in turn enhances pulmonary ventilation.

Mathematically: **R = delta-P / V** (resistance = transnasal pressure / nasal airflow)

### Factors Affecting Nasal Resistance

**Structural (fixed) factors:**
- Septal deviation (especially at the nasal valve level)
- Turbinate bony hypertrophy
- Nasal valve collapse or stenosis (internal or external)
- Nasal polyps
- Skeletal stenosis of the bony piriform aperture

**Mucosal (reversible) factors:**
- Nasal cycle (spontaneous congestion/decongestion of nasal venous sinusoids)
- [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|Allergic rhinitis]] and vasomotor rhinitis
- Rhinitis medicamentosa (decongestant overuse)
- Drug-induced congestion (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, oral contraceptives)
- Hormonal changes (puberty, pregnancy)

**Dynamic factors:**
- Inspiration-related valve collapse (Bernoulli effect at the internal nasal valve)
- Ala nasi muscle function (paralysis in facial nerve palsy increases resistance)
- Exercise-induced dilation (ala nasi contraction increases nasal cavity radius)

### Three Key Components of Nasal Resistance

1. **Nasal vestibule:** Compliant wall that can collapse during inspiration at flow rates above 30 L/min
2. **Nasal valve (internal):** Narrowest part of the nasal airway; produces the most turbulent airflow; cross-sectional area 55-83 mm squared
3. **Inferior turbinates:** Erectile tissue that swells and recedes; even 1-2 mm enlargement significantly increases resistance

### Methods of Measurement

**1. Rhinomanometry**

Measures transnasal pressure and nasal airflow to calculate nasal resistance. The two parameters are measured using differential pressure transducers (manometers).

- **Active anterior rhinomanometry:** Most commonly used clinical method; recommended by standardization committees. Pressure-sensing tube taped into one nasal passage; airflow measured from one nostril at a time.
- **Active posterior rhinomanometry:** Pressure-sensing tube held in the mouth to detect posterior nares pressure. Measures total nasal airflow from both passages simultaneously. Not all subjects can achieve an airway around the soft palate.
- **Passive rhinomanometry:** External airflow source drives air through the nose; useful for separating upper and lower airways in experimental work.
- Normal total nasal resistance: approximately 0.23 Pa/cm3/s (upper limit of normal ~0.30 Pa/cm3/s)

**2. Acoustic Rhinometry**

Uses sound waves to measure cross-sectional area and volume of the nasal cavity. An acoustic pulse is generated and transmitted along a tube into the nose; reflected sound is processed into an area-distance graph.

- Identifies the **minimum cross-sectional area** (corresponds to the internal nasal valve)
- Normal minimum cross-sectional area: 0.7 cm squared (range 0.3-1.2 cm squared), increasing to 0.9 cm squared after decongestion
- Can distinguish valve stenosis, septal deviation, turbinate hypertrophy, and tumour masses
- Major advantage: provides cross-sectional area measurements along the entire length of the nasal passage (unlike rhinomanometry which only measures the effect of the narrowest point)
- Accuracy is unreliable in the posterior nose, especially when congested

**3. Nasal Inspiratory Peak Flow (NIPF/PNIF)**

- Simple, cheap, and portable
- Measures peak inspiratory flow rate using a face mask and flow meter
- Effort-dependent and less sensitive than rhinomanometry or acoustic rhinometry for detecting small changes
- Normal values: mean PNIF in adult males 143 L/min, females 122 L/min
- Normal adult nasal patency is more than 100 L/min
- Useful for assessing large changes in nasal conductance (nasal challenge, decongestion)

**4. Decongestion Testing**

Measurement before and after topical nasal decongestant differentiates mucosal from structural causes of nasal obstruction:
- **Structural causes:** No significant improvement after decongestion (cartilage, bone, valve stenosis)
- **Mucosal causes:** Significant improvement after decongestion (turbinate congestion, rhinitis)
- Eliminates physiological variation from the nasal cycle and allows assessment of anatomical factors

### Objective vs Subjective Correlation

The correlation between objective nasal function tests and subjective sensation of nasal patency remains uncertain. Objective measurements are mainly determined by the cross-sectional area of the nasal valve region, whereas subjective perception is influenced by cold receptor stimulation, menthol sensitivity, ethmoid congestion, and mood. Studies of nasal function are not performed routinely in rhinoplasty assessment and are mainly confined to the research environment.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The internal nasal valve is the narrowest portion of the nasal cavity, bounded by the septum medially, caudal edge of the upper lateral cartilage and head of the inferior turbinate laterally, and the nasal floor inferiorly, with an apex angle of 10-15 degrees in Caucasians.
> 2. The external nasal valve is not a true valve; it is bounded by the septum medially, alar rim (lateral crus + sesamoid complex + fibrofatty tissue) laterally, and the nasal sill inferiorly.
> 3. Cottle's manoeuvre (lateral cheek traction) for internal valve assessment is non-specific as it opens all nasal airways; a Jobson Horne probe placed in the internal valve to prevent ULC collapse is a better, more specific test.
> 4. Powell and Humphrey's facial aesthetic triangle defines the nasofrontal angle (115-135 degrees), nasofacial angle (30-40 degrees), nasomental angle (120-132 degrees), and mentocervical angle (80-95 degrees).
> 5. The nasolabial angle is 90-95 degrees in men and 95-105 degrees in women; it determines nasal tip rotation along an arc centred on this angle.
> 6. Goode's ratio for tip projection is 0.55-0.60 (distance from alar-facial groove to nasal tip divided by nasion-to-tip distance).
> 7. Nasal resistance accounts for approximately 50% of total airway resistance; rhinomanometry, acoustic rhinometry, and PNIF are the key measurement tools.
> 8. Acoustic rhinometry identifies the minimum cross-sectional area (normal 0.7 cm squared), which corresponds to the internal nasal valve; it can distinguish valve stenosis, septal deviation, and turbinate hypertrophy.
> 9. Decongestion testing differentiates structural from mucosal causes of nasal obstruction: structural causes show no improvement after topical decongestant.
> 10. The alar base should equal the intercanthal distance (rule of fifths), and the nose should occupy one-third the facial length and one-fifth its width (rules of thirds and fifths).

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Dynamic internal valve collapse** during forceful inspiration is caused by the Bernoulli effect (negative intraluminal pressure causes the ULC to collapse towards the septum). Spreader grafts address this by physically widening the valve angle and preventing ULC medialization.
> - **Internal nasal valve assessment** should be carried out during normal quiet respiration at rest. Exaggerated effortful breathing can precipitate transient valve collapse even in normal individuals, leading to false-positive findings.
> - **The correlation between objective nasal function tests and subjective nasal patency remains uncertain.** Functional studies (rhinomanometry, acoustic rhinometry) are mainly confined to the research environment, and perception of nasal obstruction is influenced by factors unrelated to airflow resistance (cold receptors, mood, menthol sensitivity).
> - **The upper lip and nose are the "danger area" of the face** because infections may be transmitted in a retrograde fashion through a valveless venous system to the cavernous sinus.
> - **Computer morphing of pre-operative photographs** improves communication and patient satisfaction in rhinoplasty assessment, but must be clarified as a communication tool only and does not imply a specific guaranteed surgical outcome.
> - **Even a 1-2 mm enlargement of the inferior turbinate** has been shown to significantly reduce nasal airflow velocity (from 0.89 to 0.42 m/s), highlighting the critical importance of turbinate size in nasal valve function and resistance.
