# Orbit — Vision in ENT, Orbital Anatomy, Proptosis, and ESS Complications

> [!question] Questions
> "Enumerate causes of vision affection in ENT practice and describe AEA retraction during [[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]]. Discuss orbital anatomy in relation to nose and PNS. Enumerate causes of proptosis. What causes sudden proptosis during ESS and how to manage?"

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 1, Chapters 98 (Surgical Management of Rhinosinusitis), 111 (Disorders of the Orbit)

---

## Introduction

The orbit shares intimate anatomical relationships with the paranasal sinuses — the medial wall (lamina papyracea) separates the orbit from the ethmoid sinuses, the floor is the roof of the maxillary sinus, and the posterior relations include the sphenoid sinus. This close proximity means that sinonasal disease and sinus surgery can both affect orbital and visual function. The orbit receives contribution from 7 bones and contains the globe, extraocular muscles, cranial nerves, and vessels within a volume of approximately 30 mL. Understanding orbital anatomy is essential for safe FESS and for managing orbital complications of sinusitis.

---

## Part A — Causes of Vision Affection in ENT Practice

### Classification of Causes

**Infective:**
- Orbital complications of acute rhinosinusitis (Chandler classification — preseptal cellulitis, orbital cellulitis, subperiosteal abscess, orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis)
- [[Invasive Fungal Rhinosinusitis|Mucormycosis]] / invasive fungal sinusitis with orbital invasion
- Skull base osteomyelitis affecting cranial nerves II–VI

**Iatrogenic (FESS complications):**
- Retrobulbar haemorrhage from AEA or orbital vessel injury
- Direct optic nerve injury (especially in Onodi cell)
- Medial rectus muscle damage (microdebrider-associated)
- Orbital fat herniation with secondary complications

**Traumatic:**
- Traumatic optic neuropathy (indirect injury from facial fractures)
- Orbital blow-out fractures with muscle entrapment
- Nasoethmoidal fractures

**Neoplastic:**
- Sinonasal malignancies with orbital invasion (SCC, adenoid cystic carcinoma)
- Olfactory neuroblastoma with orbital extension
- Lymphoma, rhabdomyosarcoma (paediatric)

**Inflammatory:**
- Thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy) — compressive optic neuropathy
- Orbital pseudotumour
- Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (Wegener's)

**Vascular:**
- Cavernous sinus thrombosis
- Carotid-cavernous fistula

### AEA Retraction During FESS

The anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) is identified at the fronto-ethmoidal suture line, 20–25 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest. It may run on a mesentery across the ethmoid roof, making it vulnerable during surgery. Strategies to avoid AEA injury:

- Pre-operative CT assessment for mesentery-suspended AEA
- Identification at the skull base during bulla ethmoidalis elevation
- If bleeding occurs, the artery retracts into the orbit causing retrobulbar haemorrhage
- The AEA **cannot be safely embolized** pre-operatively (branch of ophthalmic artery)
- Bipolar cauterization before division can prevent retraction and haemorrhage

## Part B — Orbital Anatomy in Relation to Nose and PNS

### Bony Walls

The orbit is a quadrilateral pyramid with its base facing forwards, laterally and slightly inferiorly:

| Wall | Bones | Sinus Relation |
|------|-------|---------------|
| Medial | Maxilla (frontal process), lacrimal, lamina papyracea, sphenoid body | Ethmoid sinuses |
| Superior | Frontal bone (primarily) | [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|Frontal sinus]] |
| Inferior | Maxilla, zygomatic, palatine | Maxillary sinus roof |
| Lateral | Zygomatic, greater wing sphenoid | Thickest wall; no sinus |

### Key Landmarks

- **Anterior ethmoid foramen:** 20–25 mm posterior to anterior lacrimal crest — contains AEA and anterior ethmoidal nerve
- **Posterior ethmoid foramen:** 30–35 mm posterior to anterior lacrimal crest
- **Optic canal:** Most posterior, transmits CN II and ophthalmic artery; at risk from posterior ethmoid/sphenoid surgery
- **Superior orbital fissure:** Transmits CN III, IV, V1, VI, and ophthalmic veins
- **Inferior orbital fissure:** Transmits infraorbital nerve (V2), zygomatic nerve, inferior ophthalmic vein

### Relation to Sinuses

- **Lamina papyracea** (paper-thin ethmoid bone) forms the majority of the medial wall — the most relevant wall for otorhinolaryngologists; dehiscent in a significant percentage of patients
- **Orbital floor** is thin medial to the infraorbital nerve and fractures more easily than laterally
- **Periorbita** is loosely attached to bone (except at orbital margin, suture lines, trochlear fovea, and vascular foramina) and easily dissected

## Part C — Proptosis — Causes and Classification

Proptosis (exophthalmos) is anterior displacement of the globe. It may be axial (along the visual axis) or non-axial (suggesting a mass displacing the globe).

### Causes of Proptosis in ENT Practice

**Infective:**
- Orbital cellulitis / subperiosteal abscess (Chandler III–IV)
- Invasive fungal sinusitis (mucormycosis, aspergillosis)

**Inflammatory:**
- Thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy) — most common cause of bilateral proptosis in adults; infiltration of T-lymphocytes and macrophages with glycosaminoglycan deposition → enlargement of orbital fat and extraocular muscles → impaired venous drainage → chemosis, periorbital oedema, proptosis
- Orbital pseudotumour (idiopathic orbital inflammation)
- Granulomatous disease (GPA, sarcoidosis)

**Neoplastic:**
- Sinonasal malignancies invading orbit (SCC, ACC, adenocarcinoma)
- Lymphoma
- Lacrimal gland tumours
- Rhabdomyosarcoma (paediatric)
- Fibro-osseous lesions (fibrous dysplasia, ossifying fibroma)

**Mucocele:**
- Frontoethmoidal mucocele — displaces globe laterally and inferiorly

**Vascular:**
- Cavernous sinus thrombosis (pulsatile proptosis)
- Carotid-cavernous fistula
- Retrobulbar haemorrhage (acute)

**Iatrogenic:**
- Retrobulbar haemorrhage during FESS

## Part D — Sudden Proptosis During ESS — Cause and Management

### Cause

Sudden proptosis during endoscopic sinus surgery is caused by **retrobulbar haemorrhage** from injury to orbital vessels, most commonly the anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA). The haemorrhage collects within the confined orbital space, rapidly increasing intraorbital pressure.

The breach of the lamina papyracea allows instruments to enter the orbit. The AEA, running on a mesentery at the skull base level, may be severed and retract into the orbit. Use of the microdebrider is associated with increased risk of medial rectus muscle damage.

### Clinical Signs

- **Proptosis** — progressive, tense globe
- **Subconjunctival haemorrhage** and periorbital ecchymosis
- **Limited extraocular movements** (ophthalmoplegia)
- **Raised intraocular pressure**
- **Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)** — indicates optic nerve compromise
- **Decreased visual acuity** — urgent sign requiring immediate intervention
- **Tense orbit** on palpation

### Emergency Management Protocol

1. **Stop the surgery immediately**
2. **Remove nasal packing** if any
3. **Ophthalmology consultation** — urgent
4. **Assess visual acuity and pupillary reflex** — RAPD indicates optic nerve compromise
5. **Decompress the orbit:**
   - **Remove lamina papyracea** widely and **incise the periorbita** endonasally
   - **Lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis** — releases orbital septum, reduces compartment pressure
6. **Medical measures:**
   - IV dexamethasone / methylprednisolone (reduce oedema)
   - IV mannitol 20% (reduce intraocular pressure)
   - Timolol eye drops (reduce aqueous production)
   - Head-up positioning
7. **Monitor closely** — serial visual acuity, pupillary function, and IOP measurements
8. If damage to ocular muscles is suspected, late oculoplastic surgery may be required — transected muscles usually cannot be repaired acutely

### Prevention

- Pre-operative CT review for lamina papyracea dehiscence and AEA mesentery
- Identify the AEA at the skull base in every case where bulla is elevated
- Avoid aggressive use of microdebrider near the lamina papyracea
- Maintain a clear surgical field — poor visualization due to bleeding increases complication risk
- Consider intra-operative image guidance for revision cases and high-risk anatomy

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The orbit receives contributions from 7 bones: maxilla, zygomatic, ethmoid, lacrimal, sphenoid, frontal, and palatine
> 2. The lamina papyracea (ethmoid bone) forms the majority of the medial orbital wall and is the thinnest wall
> 3. The AEA foramen is located 20–25 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest at the fronto-ethmoidal suture
> 4. Retrobulbar haemorrhage is the most serious immediate orbital complication of FESS — caused primarily by AEA injury
> 5. Any proptosis during FESS should be regarded as significant and requires immediate orbital decompression
> 6. Lateral canthotomy and cantholysis is the emergency procedure for compartment syndrome of the orbit
> 7. Thyroid eye disease (Graves') is the most common cause of bilateral proptosis in adults
> 8. The optic nerve is most at risk from an Onodi cell during posterior ethmoid/sphenoid surgery
> 9. Microdebrider use is associated with increased risk of medial rectus muscle damage
> 10. Prevalence of [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]] during sinus surgery is approximately 0.5%


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **RAPD is the most important sign** of optic nerve compromise during FESS — check pupils regularly intra-operatively in at-risk cases
> - **Lateral canthotomy is a life-of-eye-saving procedure** that all ENT surgeons must be able to perform
> - **Never continue surgery without proper visualization** — bloody surgical field increases the risk of all orbital complications
> - **The AEA cannot be embolized** (ophthalmic artery branch) — unlike the SPA, preventive cauterization at the skull base is the safest strategy
> - **Dehiscence of the lamina papyracea** must be identified on pre-operative CT — it is present in a significant proportion of patients
