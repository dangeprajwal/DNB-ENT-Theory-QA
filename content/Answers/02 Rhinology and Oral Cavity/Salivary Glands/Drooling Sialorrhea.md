# Drooling (Sialorrhoea) — Causes and Management

> [!question] Questions
> Drooling in children — causes and management. Sialorrhoea — aetiology, assessment and treatment options.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 46 (Non-Neoplastic Salivary Gland Diseases)

---

## Introduction

Drooling is the unintentional loss of saliva from the mouth. It is common in normally developed babies but is considered abnormal after the age of 4 years. Drooling may arise as a consequence of impaired neuromuscular function, though less frequently it can be due to an increased production of saliva (idiopathic or drug-induced). True excess salivation (hypersalivation) is extremely unusual — the majority of patients with excess salivation have difficulties in salivary control rather than hypersalivation. In children, the most common cause is cerebral palsy, which persists in 37–58% of these individuals. In adults, drooling is observed in 45% of patients affected by Parkinson's disease. Drooling is known to be difficult to treat, and management ranges from conservative measures to surgical intervention.

---

## PART A: Aetiology

### Causes of Drooling

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| Neuromuscular (children) | Cerebral palsy (37–58%), intellectual disability, Down syndrome |
| Neuromuscular (adults) | Parkinson's disease (45%), ALS, stroke, traumatic brain injury |
| [[Facial Nerve|Facial nerve palsy]] | Impaired lip seal and oral incompetence |
| Drug-induced | Clozapine, cholinesterase inhibitors, pilocarpine |
| Idiopathic | True hypersalivation; rare |

### Pathophysiology

In most cases, drooling results from impaired neuromuscular control of the swallowing mechanism rather than excessive saliva production. Impaired oral-motor function leads to reduced frequency of swallowing, poor lip seal, tongue thrust, and inability to manage normal volumes of saliva. The distinction between true hypersalivation and impaired salivary control is important as it guides treatment selection — most patients benefit from interventions that reduce saliva volume or redirect its flow rather than attempting to improve neuromuscular coordination alone.

### Complications

Drooling can give rise to a wide spectrum of clinical and functional complications:
- Impaired speech and eating
- Perioral skin breakdown and excoriation
- Bad odour and secondary infection
- Angular cheilitis
- Wetting of clothing
- Social embarrassment and reduced quality of life
- Aspiration-related and pulmonary complications

---

## PART B: Assessment

There are no specific investigations for sialorrhoea — the cause is often established from the clinical history. Assessment should include:

- **History:** Duration, severity, aggravating factors, underlying neurological diagnosis, medication review
- **Examination:** Oral-motor function, lip seal competence, tongue control, dental status, perioral skin condition
- **Swallowing assessment:** Important to evaluate aspiration risk before planning interventions that reduce saliva volume
- **Severity grading:** Various drooling severity scales exist to quantify baseline severity and monitor treatment response

---

## PART C: Conservative Management

### Behavioural and Oral-Motor Therapy

Behaviour modification to promote swallowing has been advocated for many decades but results are inconsistent. It requires motivation and is time-consuming. Oral-motor exercises and intra-oral devices such as palatal training devices may be used as adjuncts. These approaches are most suitable for patients with adequate cognitive function and motivation.

### Changes in Diet and Positioning

Dietary modifications (avoiding foods that stimulate salivation) and postural adjustments (upright positioning, chin tuck) can reduce drooling severity as part of a multidisciplinary approach.

---

## PART D: Pharmacological Management

### Anticholinergic Agents

Anticholinergic agents decrease saliva secretion through the parasympathetic autonomic nervous system. A systematic review concluded that although effective, no one drug was preferable. Commonly used agents include:

| Drug | Route | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Glycopyrrolate | Oral | Quaternary amine; does not cross BBB; fewer CNS side effects |
| Scopolamine | Transdermal | Long-acting patch; convenient but systemic side effects |
| Benztropine | Oral | Suggested for long-term benefit |

Side effects include dry mouth (paradoxically desired), urinary retention, constipation, blurred vision, tachycardia, behavioural changes and drowsiness.

### Botulinum Toxin

Botulinum toxin has emerged as a significant treatment for drooling. It is a neurotoxin that prevents the secretion of saliva by inhibiting the release of presynaptic acetylcholine. It has been shown to be effective in both adult and paediatric populations.

A recent meta-analysis (2013) concluded that botulinum toxin decreases the severity of drooling with statistical significance in both adult and paediatric populations. The injection is typically directed into the submandibular and/or parotid glands under ultrasound guidance.

- **Duration of effect:** 6 weeks to 6 months
- **Need for repeat injections:** Required at regular intervals
- **Complications:** Increased saliva thickness (3.9%), dysphagia (3.3%), xerostomia (3.3%), pneumonia (2.2%)
- The ideal dose and form of application remain to be established

## PART E: Surgical Management

Various surgical procedures have been described for drooling, all encompassing an alteration in the anatomy of the salivary glands.

### Surgical Options

| Procedure | Details |
|---|---|
| SMG duct relocation | Most common procedure; ducts redirected posteriorly to tonsillar fossa |
| Sublingual gland excision | Often combined with SMG duct relocation |
| Parotid duct relocation | Ducts relocated to tonsillar fossa |
| Salivary gland excision | SMG excision; reduces saliva volume; risk of xerostomia |
| Denervation | Tympanic neurectomy; high recurrence rate |
| Duct ligation | Parotid duct ligation; risk of sialocoele |

### Submandibular Duct Relocation

This is the most common surgical approach for drooling. The submandibular ducts are relocated posteriorly so that saliva is directed into the oropharynx where it can be swallowed more easily. It is often combined with removal of the sublingual glands. Ranula formation and submandibular duct obstruction have been reported in 8% of patients after submandibular duct relocation. The procedure is most suitable for young adults with likely long-term illness such as cerebral palsy.

### Gland Excision

Resection of the major salivary glands is effective but carries side effects including xerostomia, visible scar and facial nerve weakness. It is reserved for patients in whom other measures have failed.

### Radiation Therapy

A low dose of [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] (8–12.5 Gy) has been suggested for excess salivation in patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Given the often shortened lifespan of affected individuals, this may be a useful treatment. Radiation is typically reserved for elderly patients who are not candidates for surgery and cannot tolerate medical therapies, due to the risks of long-term xerostomia, loss of taste and development of malignancy.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The majority of patients with drooling have impaired salivary control rather than true hypersalivation — behaviour modification alone has inconsistent results and requires patient motivation and cognitive function
> - Glycopyrrolate is preferred over other anticholinergics in children as it is a quaternary amine that does not cross the blood-brain barrier, resulting in fewer central nervous system side effects
> - Botulinum toxin injection requires ultrasound guidance for accurate placement and needs to be repeated every 6 weeks to 6 months — the ideal dose and application protocol remain to be established
> - Gland excision for drooling carries the most significant side effects including xerostomia, visible scarring and facial nerve weakness — it should be reserved as a last resort after conservative and minimally invasive options have been exhausted
> - Aspiration risk should be assessed before any intervention that reduces saliva volume — patients with impaired swallowing may paradoxically worsen with reduced but thicker saliva
