# [[Parapharyngeal Space|Parapharyngeal Space]] Tumours, FNAC and Imaging of Salivary Glands

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Discuss salivary gland tumours arising de-novo in the parapharyngeal space with their clinical features and management. Describe the indications for FNAC in salivary gland disease and the role of radiology in salivary gland evaluation. Outline the cervico-parotid approach for PPS adenoma, complications of parotid surgery and first bite syndrome.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 9 (Benign Salivary Gland Tumours), Chapter 10 (Malignant Tumours of the Salivary Glands), Chapter 45 (Imaging of Salivary Glands)

---

## Introduction

Parapharyngeal space (PPS) tumours are uncommon lesions accounting for only 0.5% of all head and neck tumours. Despite their rarity, they present unique diagnostic and surgical challenges due to the anatomical complexity of the PPS and its proximity to the great vessels, cranial nerves and skull base. Among PPS tumours, 40-50% are of salivary gland origin, predominantly [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|pleomorphic adenoma]]s arising from the deep lobe of the parotid gland. The evaluation of salivary gland tumours relies heavily on fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) as the initial tissue diagnosis tool and on multimodal imaging for surgical planning. MRI is the superior modality for PPS tumours, while ultrasound remains the primary imaging tool for superficial salivary gland lesions. The cervico-parotid approach is the most commonly employed surgical technique for excision of salivary PPS tumours, and the surgeon must be cognisant of complications including facial nerve injury, Frey's syndrome and first bite syndrome.

## PART A: Parapharyngeal Space Tumours

### Anatomy and Tumour Types

The parapharyngeal space is a potential space shaped like an inverted pyramid extending from the skull base superiorly to the hyoid bone inferiorly. It is divided by the tensor-vascular-styloid fascia into two functionally important compartments:

- **Pre-styloid compartment:** contains fat, minor salivary glands and the deep lobe of the parotid gland; tumours arising here are predominantly salivary in origin
- **Post-styloid compartment:** contains the carotid artery, internal jugular vein, cranial nerves IX-XII and sympathetic chain; tumours arising here are predominantly [[Glomus Tumour|paraganglioma]]s and neurogenic tumours (schwannomas, neurofibromas)

| Feature | Pre-styloid PPS | Post-styloid PPS |
|---|---|---|
| Common tumours | Pleomorphic adenoma, MEC | Paraganglioma, schwannoma |
| Vessel displacement | ICA displaced posteriorly | ICA displaced anteromedially |
| Typical origin | Deep lobe parotid, minor SG | CN IX-XII, sympathetic chain |

Parapharyngeal space tumours account for 0.5% of all head and neck tumours. Of these, 40-50% are of salivary gland origin, with pleomorphic adenoma from the deep lobe of the parotid being the most common. Other PPS tumours include paragangliomas, schwannomas, neurofibromas, lymph node enlargement and branchial cysts. The so-called dumbbell tumour is a characteristic presentation where a deep lobe parotid tumour extends through the stylomandibular tunnel, creating a waisted mass bridging the parotid and the parapharyngeal space.

### Clinical Features

The classic presentation of a PPS tumour is medial displacement of the tonsil and soft palate, visible on intraoral examination. On palpation, the mass is felt bimanually with one finger intraorally and the other hand palpating the neck. Many PPS tumours remain asymptomatic until they reach a significant size due to the compliance of the parapharyngeal fat. Symptoms when present include:

- Painless progressive oropharyngeal swelling
- Dysphagia and muffled voice (large tumours)
- Unilateral serous otitis media from Eustachian tube compression
- Trismus (if extension to masticator space)
- Cranial nerve palsies (IX-XII) in post-styloid tumours
- Pulsatile [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] (paragangliomas)

Malignant salivary PPS tumours may present with pain, rapid growth and cranial nerve deficits.

## PART B: Surgical Approaches to PPS Tumours

### Cervico-Parotid Approach

The cervico-parotid approach is the most commonly used technique for salivary PPS tumours and is considered the approach of choice for pleomorphic adenomas arising from the deep lobe of the parotid. Key steps include:

- A modified Blair incision is made with neck extension to allow adequate access
- The facial nerve is identified at the stylomastoid foramen using standard landmarks (tympanomastoid suture, tragal pointer) and is formally dissected and mobilized
- Access to the PPS is achieved by separating the tumour from the parotid gland and retracting the mandible anteriorly
- The space between mobilized facial nerve branches is opened up to deliver the tumour
- Deep lobe parotid tumours displace the facial nerve laterally, placing it at risk during initial dissection
- This approach can be combined with transoral robotic surgery (TORS) if additional access is necessary

The cervico-parotid approach provides excellent access to the pre-styloid compartment while preserving the facial nerve under direct vision. It allows en-bloc removal of the tumour along with the originating deep lobe tissue.

### Transmandibulotomy/Transpharyngeal Approach

This approach is reserved for large or complex PPS tumours requiring wider exposure, particularly those with significant medial or superior extension:

- A skin crease incision with lip split is used
- Dissection is performed deep to the submandibular gland onto the digastric and hyoglossus muscles
- The hypoglossal nerve is identified and preserved
- A paramedian mandibulotomy is performed with pre-plating using two plates to ensure accurate reduction
- The mandible is retracted laterally and the incision extended through the floor of mouth to the anterior faucial pillar
- The lingual and hypoglossal nerves are identified and displaced medially
- This approach provides excellent exposure of the medial and superior aspects of the tumour

Patients must be warned pre-operatively of the probability of hemilingual anaesthesia, as the lingual nerve is frequently damaged during this approach.

### Other Approaches

- **Transoral approach:** has a limited role; risk of incomplete excision and difficulty controlling haemorrhage; may be appropriate for small pre-styloid tumours
- **Infratemporal fossa approach:** indicated for tumours with significant superior extension towards the skull base

## PART C: FNAC in Salivary Gland Disease

Fine-needle aspiration cytology is the initial tissue diagnosis tool for all suspected salivary gland tumours. It is minimally invasive, inexpensive and provides rapid results that guide surgical planning. Ultrasound-guided FNAC (UgFNAC) is the recommended technique as it simultaneously allows staging of the neck and ensures accurate targeting of the lesion.

### Indications

- All new palpable salivary gland masses
- Deep lobe or parapharyngeal tumours (under ultrasound or CT guidance)
- Suspected salivary lymphoma
- Suspected metastatic disease within intraparotid lymph nodes
- Recurrent salivary gland tumours
- Assessment of diffuse salivary gland enlargement (to exclude lymphoma or Sjogren's)

### Diagnostic Performance

FNAC has an accuracy of approximately 79% for differentiating benign from malignant salivary gland tumours. A large French series of 1355 FNACs reported: 80.5% true positive, 4.6% suspicious, 11.9% false negative and 3% uninterpretable results. On-site cytological evaluation significantly improves diagnostic yield; if the initial aspirate is unsatisfactory, the procedure can be repeated immediately.

### Biopsy Considerations

| Method | Indication | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| UgFNAC | All salivary masses (1st line) | ~79% accuracy benign vs malignant |
| Core needle biopsy | Equivocal/non-diagnostic FNAC | Provides architectural info |
| Incisional biopsy | Minor salivary gland tumours only | Accessible mucosal location |
| Open biopsy | Contraindicated | Risk of tumour seeding |

A critical principle is that even if FNAC suggests a benign diagnosis, removal for histopathological examination remains mandatory for all major salivary gland tumours. Incisional biopsy is NOT recommended for major salivary gland tumours due to the risk of tumour spillage and facial nerve damage, except in the rare circumstance of a large tumour with skin ulceration. For minor salivary gland tumours, incisional biopsy IS appropriate as the mucosa is readily accessible. Core needle biopsy is increasingly used as it provides architectural information that FNAC cannot, which is helpful for grading mucoepidermoid carcinoma and distinguishing certain tumour types.

## PART D: Imaging of Salivary Glands

### Ultrasound

Ultrasound is the primary and initial imaging modality for salivary gland tumours. It is readily available, inexpensive, non-ionising and can guide FNAC simultaneously. Ultrasound assesses intrinsic tumour characteristics including size, echogenicity, vascularity and margins. Benign tumours typically appear as well-defined hypoechoic masses, while malignant tumours show irregular margins and heterogeneous echogenicity. However, ultrasound has limitations for deep lobe and parapharyngeal tumours that are not accessible to the transducer.

### MRI

MRI is the superior cross-sectional imaging modality for salivary gland tumours. Its indications include:

- Deep lobe and parapharyngeal space tumour evaluation
- Suspected malignancy (assessment of facial nerve invasion, perineural spread)
- Assessment of the stylomastoid foramen
- Pre-operative surgical planning for PPS tumours (relationship to great vessels, ICA, parotid)
- Recurrent tumour evaluation

Diffusion-weighted MRI (DW-MRI) has demonstrated superior diagnostic efficacy for distinguishing benign from malignant salivary gland tumours based on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. Malignant tumours generally show restricted diffusion (lower ADC values) compared to benign lesions.

### CT and Other Modalities

- **CT:** indicated when bone invasion is suspected; also useful for identifying calcification within tumours (e.g., pleomorphic adenoma)
- **[[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]]:** has high false-positive rates in salivary gland assessment because Warthin's tumour and pleomorphic adenoma both show increased FDG uptake; its main utility is for excluding distant metastases in confirmed malignancy
- **Sialography:** involves contrast injection into the salivary duct and demonstrates filling defects (stones), duct strictures and sialectasis; contraindicated in acute infection; largely replaced by non-invasive alternatives
- **MR sialography:** non-invasive alternative to conventional sialography; requires no contrast injection; excellent for demonstrating ductal pathology

| Modality | Primary Role | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Ultrasound | Initial imaging; guides FNAC | Cannot assess deep lobe/PPS |
| MRI | Deep lobe, PPS, nerve invasion | Cost; availability |
| CT | Bone invasion, calcification | Inferior soft tissue contrast |
| PET-CT | Distant metastasis screening | High false-positive rate |

## PART E: Parotid Surgery Complications and First Bite Syndrome

### Complications of Parotidectomy

**Facial nerve weakness** is the most significant complication. Temporary neuropraxia is common and recovers within 4-6 weeks. The risk of permanent [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]] is 1-2% for benign disease surgery. The risk is higher in revision surgery, malignant disease and total parotidectomy.

**Frey's syndrome (gustatory sweating)** results from aberrant reinnervation of sweat glands by parasympathetic secretomotor fibres that misdirect along damaged sympathetic pathways to cutaneous sweat glands. It presents as flushing and sweating over the pre-auricular skin during eating. Diagnosis is confirmed by the Minor's starch-iodine test. Treatment includes subdermal botulinum toxin injections, which require repeat at 6-12 monthly intervals. Prevention strategies include SMAS layer restoration and sternomastoid muscle interposition flap.

**Greater auricular nerve sensory loss** affects the angle of the mandible and the inferior two-thirds of the pinna. This is largely unavoidable but the area of numbness decreases over 12 months. Preservation of the posterior branch of the greater auricular nerve reduces the sensory deficit. A stump neuroma of the greater auricular nerve may develop as a painful tender nodule months post-operatively, managed by local excision and burying the nerve end in muscle.

**Salivary fistula and sialocoele** occur from the cut surface of residual parotid tissue leaking saliva. A sialocoele presents as a fluctuant swelling within days of surgery and is treated by aspiration. A salivary fistula presents as cutaneous leakage and is managed with pressure dressings. Persistent cases are treated with botulinum toxin injection into residual parotid tissue under ultrasound guidance. Hyoscine patches may also be used to reduce salivary output.

**Cosmetic defect** results from loss of bulk behind the ramus of the mandible, creating a visible contour depression. This can be mitigated by fat transfer.

### First Bite Syndrome

First bite syndrome is characterised by intense pain in the parotid region at the onset of eating that quickly passes once the patient begins to chew. It is caused by sympathetic denervation of remaining parotid tissue. Loss of sympathetic innervation results in supersensitivity of myoepithelial cells to parasympathetic stimulation, producing an exaggerated contractile response on the first stimulus of eating.

Treatment options include:

- Botulinum toxin injection into the parotid gland
- High-dose gabapentin or pregabalin for neuropathic pain control
- The condition is generally self-limiting

There is a theoretical argument for performing a total parotidectomy during the cervico-parotid approach for PPS tumours to avoid first bite syndrome altogether, though this comes at the expense of poorer cosmesis and loss of salivary function.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A dumbbell tumour on MRI (waisted mass extending through the stylomandibular tunnel) is virtually pathognomonic of a deep lobe parotid pleomorphic adenoma extending into the parapharyngeal space
> - Open biopsy of a major salivary gland tumour is contraindicated due to risk of tumour seeding; core needle biopsy is the preferred escalation when FNAC is non-diagnostic
> - PET-CT has limited diagnostic utility in salivary gland tumours due to high false-positive rates (both Warthin's tumour and pleomorphic adenoma show increased FDG uptake); its role is restricted to distant metastasis screening
> - During the cervico-parotid approach for PPS tumours, the facial nerve is displaced laterally by the deep lobe tumour mass and is therefore at risk during the initial superficial dissection before formal nerve identification
> - Preservation of the posterior branch of the greater auricular nerve during parotid surgery significantly reduces the area of post-operative sensory loss over the pinna and angle of mandible
> - The transmandibulotomy approach requires pre-plating the mandibulotomy with two plates before performing the osteotomy to ensure accurate anatomical reduction at closure
