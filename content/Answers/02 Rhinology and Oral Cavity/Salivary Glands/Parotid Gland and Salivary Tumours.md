# Parotid Gland Anatomy, Salivary Gland Tumours and Facial Nerve in Parotid Surgery

> [!question] Questions
> Parotid gland anatomy. Neck management in salivary gland carcinoma. Facial nerve landmarks in parotid surgery. House-Brackmann classification. Benign tumours of parotid — features, investigation, management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 43 (Salivary Gland Anatomy), Chapter 9 (Benign Salivary Gland Tumours), Chapter 10 (Malignant Tumours of the Salivary Glands), Chapter 95 (Facial Reanimation Surgery); Volume 2, Chapter 112 (Facial Nerve Disorders)

## Introduction

The parotid gland is the largest salivary gland and the site of most salivary gland neoplasms. Approximately 75% of all salivary gland tumours are benign, with pleomorphic adenoma being the most common. Around 80% of salivary gland tumours arise in the parotid, of which approximately 80% are benign and 20% malignant. The incidence of salivary gland tumours is approximately 6–7 per 100 000 per year. A key principle in salivary gland oncology is the inverse relationship between gland size and malignancy risk — the smaller the gland, the higher the probability that a tumour arising within it is malignant. Understanding parotid anatomy, facial nerve relationships and the principles of parotid surgery is essential for the ENT surgeon.

---

## PART A: Parotid Gland — Clinical Anatomy

The parotid gland is a wedge-shaped predominantly serous gland weighing approximately 25 grams, lying in the retromandibular fossa. It is the last salivary gland to become encapsulated during embryological development and consequently entraps lymphatic tissue within it — explaining why Warthin's tumour (which arises from salivary duct inclusions within lymph nodes) is almost exclusive to the parotid gland, and why intraparotid lymph node metastases from skin malignancies occur.

The gland is arbitrarily divided by the facial nerve into a superficial lobe (containing 80–90% of parotid tissue) and a deep lobe. As 80–90% of glandular tissue is lateral to the facial nerve, a similar proportion of tumours arise in the superficial lobe.

### Parotid Duct (Stensen's Duct)

The parotid duct emerges from the anterior border of the gland, runs superficial to the masseter muscle, pierces the buccinator, and opens into the oral cavity opposite the second upper molar. It is approximately 5 cm long with a luminal calibre of 0.6 mm. Accessory parotid tissue may be found along the line of the duct (1% of parotid tumours arise from accessory tissue).

### Parotid Fascia and Capsule

The parotid is invested by the superficial layer of the deep cervical fascia, which splits to form a capsule. The capsule is firmly adherent to the gland superficially but deficient medially (the stylomandibular tunnel), allowing deep lobe tumours to extend into the [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] — producing the characteristic 'dumbbell' tumour visible on MRI.

### Facial Nerve — Intraparotid Course

The facial nerve exits the stylomastoid foramen and enters the parotid gland, where it divides into an upper (temporozygomatic) and lower (cervicofacial) division. These give rise to five terminal branches:

| Branch | Motor Supply |
|---|---|
| Temporal | Frontalis, orbicularis oculi (upper) |
| Zygomatic | Orbicularis oculi (lower) |
| Buccal | Buccinator, orbicularis oris |
| Marginal mandibular | Lower lip depressors, mentalis |
| Cervical | Platysma |

Cross-innervation is most common between the zygomatic and buccal branches and least common for the marginal mandibular branch — making injury to this branch the most clinically significant. The branching pattern is variable; Katz and Catalano described five different patterns of facial nerve arborization within the parotid.

### Landmarks for Facial Nerve Identification in Surgery

| Landmark | Relationship to Facial Nerve |
|---|---|
| Tragal pointer | Nerve lies 1 cm deep and inferior to tip |
| Tympanomastoid suture | Nerve lies immediately deep and inferior; extremely reliable |
| Posterior belly of digastric | Nerve exits skull immediately anterior to its attachment |
| Retromandibular vein | Lies medial to nerve; surrogate for deep lobe boundary on MRI |
| Styloid process | Lies deep to nerve exit — dissection onto styloid may increase nerve damage risk |

In revision or difficult cases where the main trunk cannot be accessed, the mandibular branch can be identified at the angle of the mandible where it lies superficial to the facial vessels, and dissected in a retrograde fashion.

## PART B: Investigation of Salivary Gland Tumours

### Clinical Assessment

A parotid lump should be considered a salivary gland tumour until proven otherwise. Features suggesting malignancy include rapid growth, pain, facial nerve dysfunction, fixation to skin or deep structures, and cervical lymphadenopathy. Approximately 44% of parotid cancer patients have pain and 25% have facial nerve paresis or paralysis at presentation.

### Imaging

Ultrasound is the preferred initial investigation for palpable salivary gland masses — it is quick, cheap, accessible and allows simultaneous USgFNAC. Cross-sectional imaging (MRI preferred) is indicated for tumours > 3 cm, suspected deep lobe or parapharyngeal extension, suspicion of malignancy, and recurrent disease. MRI provides superior soft tissue definition and is mandatory for evaluation of facial nerve invasion and perineural spread. CT is complementary when bone erosion is suspected.

### Fine-Needle Aspiration Cytology (FNAC)

FNAC should be performed on all suspected salivary gland tumours, preferably under ultrasound guidance (USgFNAC). The accuracy for distinguishing benign from malignant lesions is around 80–90%. Standardized cytological categories are: (i) inadequate; (ii) benign; (iii) atypical cells present; (iv) suspicious for malignancy; (v) overtly malignant. Core biopsy can be used as a second-line investigation when FNAC is equivocal or non-diagnostic.

## PART C: Benign Salivary Gland Tumours

### Pleomorphic Adenoma

Pleomorphic adenoma is the commonest salivary gland tumour, accounting for the majority of parotid tumours. It is derived from intercalated duct and myoepithelial cells that differentiate into epithelial and connective tissue structures. The myxoid stroma is one of its most characteristic features and makes the tumour extremely fragile — rupture during surgery inevitably seeds the operative field and significantly increases recurrence risk.

A critical feature is the capsule — in virtually all pleomorphic adenomas, it is exceptionally thin (< 20 microns) in at least one area and incomplete in approximately 50%, more so in the mucoid subtype. The risk of malignant transformation is approximately 0.15% of tumours showing features of malignant change, but increases with chronicity and is higher in recurrent disease (~3%).

**Choice of operation for parotid pleomorphic adenoma:**

| Approach | Key Arguments |
|---|---|
| Extracapsular dissection (ECD) | Similar recurrence rates to superficial parotidectomy; less cosmetic deformity; lower incidence of Frey's syndrome; virginal tissue planes preserved for revision |
| Partial parotidectomy with cuff | Cuff compensates for incomplete capsule; may achieve microscopic clearance; if unexpected malignancy found, clearance may be adequate |

In practice, most surgeons compromise — taking a narrow cuff (e.g. 1 cm) around the tumour. The most common practice in the UK is partial superficial parotidectomy. Enucleation is an unacceptable historic practice. Overall recurrence rates are around 2%, occurring typically about one decade after surgery. Recurrence is usually multicentric. For recurrent disease, all remaining parotid tissue should be resected and post-operative [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] considered, as there is a higher chance of malignant change (~3%) in recurrent tumours.

### Warthin's Tumour

Warthin's tumour is the second most common parotid tumour, accounting for 20% of benign parotid tumours. It arises from salivary duct inclusions within intraparotid lymph nodes, explaining why it is rare outside the parotid. Peak incidence is in the 7th decade. There is a strong association with smoking (incidence doubled in smokers) and the tumour is comparatively rare in Afro-Caribbeans. Bilaterality occurs in 10–15%. Malignant transformation is exceedingly rare. Management is through partial superficial parotidectomy, but in elderly patients with diagnostic FNAC, conservative observation is acceptable.

### Oncocytoma

Rare tumour predominantly affecting women over middle age. Presents as a slow-growing parotid mass. May rarely be bilateral (10%). Excision is curative.

## PART D: Malignant Salivary Gland Tumours

### WHO 2017 Classification and Clinical Grading

The WHO 2017 classification recognizes 22 malignant salivary gland tumour phenotypes. For clinical use, these are divided by biological behaviour:

| Clinical Grade | Tumour Types |
|---|---|
| Low grade | AcCC, PAC, low-grade MEC |
| Intermediate grade | AdCC, epithelial-myoepithelial carcinoma |
| High grade | High-grade MEC, SDC, carcinoma ex-PA, ACNOS, undifferentiated carcinoma |

### Specific Histotypes

- **Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (MEC):** Most common parotid malignancy; comprised of mucus-secreting, epidermoid and intermediate cells; prognosis strongly influenced by grade
- **Adenoid cystic carcinoma (AdCC):** Most common submandibular and minor salivary gland malignancy; notorious for perineural spread with skip lesions; distant metastasis to lungs in ~70% of patients; 5-year survival can be optimistic — 10- and 20-year rates drop continuously
- **Acinic cell carcinoma (AcCC):** Predominantly parotid; generally low grade but cure rate drops from 76% at 5 years to 55% at 15 years — long-term follow-up mandatory
- **Carcinoma ex-pleomorphic adenoma:** Risk increases with duration of existing pleomorphic adenoma — up to 10% by 15 years; rate is 7–16% in recurrent pleomorphic adenoma; 5-year DSS is 40%

### TNM Staging (AJCC/UICC 8th Edition)

| T Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| T1 | Tumour ≤ 2 cm, no extraparenchymal extension |
| T2 | > 2–4 cm, no extraparenchymal extension |
| T3 | > 4–6 cm, and/or extraparenchymal extension |
| T4a | Invades skin, mandible, ear canal, VII nerve |
| T4b | Skull base, pterygoid plates, encases carotid |

## PART E: Neck Management in Salivary Gland Carcinoma

### N+ Neck

A clinically positive neck requires comprehensive (modified radical) [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] removing levels I–V. In cN+ parotid cancer, pN+ rates are high across all levels: level I (43–52%), level II (77–90%), level III (40–73%), level IV (53–57%), level V (40–43%). Post-operative adjuvant radiotherapy to the parotid and ipsilateral neck doubles locoregional control and improves survival.

### N0 Neck

Risk factors for occult nodal metastasis include tumour size > 4 cm and high-grade histology (20% and 49% risk respectively). Age > 54 years, perilymphatic spread and extraparotid extension together correspond to a 95% risk. Elective neck treatment (surgery or radiotherapy) is indicated for high-risk patients. AdCC rarely causes lymph node metastasis — elective neck dissection is generally not indicated.

For submandibular gland malignancy with cN0 neck, the minimum surgery is gland resection within a level I–II–III neck dissection.

## PART F: Surgery and Complications

### Parotid Surgery Complications

| Complication | Details |
|---|---|
| Facial weakness | Neuropraxia recovers 4–6 weeks; permanent palsy risk 1–2% |
| Sensory loss | Greater auricular nerve territory; posterior branch preservation reduces deficit |
| Frey's syndrome | Gustatory sweating; treated with botulinum toxin; reduced by SMAS restoration or conservative surgery |
| Salivary fistula/sialocoele | Settles within 1–2 weeks; botulinum toxin to gland if refractory |
| First bite syndrome | Pain on initial bite; caused by sympathetic denervation; gabapentin or botulinum toxin |

### House-Brackmann Facial Nerve Grading System

| Grade | Degree | Description |
|---|---|---|
| I | Normal | Normal symmetrical function |
| II | Mild | Slight weakness on close inspection; complete eye closure with minimal effort |
| III | Moderate | Obvious but not disfiguring difference; complete eye closure with effort |
| IV | Moderately severe | Obvious disfiguring weakness; inability to lift brow; incomplete eye closure |
| V | Severe | Motion barely perceptible; incomplete eye closure; slight mouth movement |
| VI | Total paralysis | No movement, loss of tone |

The House-Brackmann system was endorsed by the American Academy of Otolaryngology — Head and Neck Surgery and remains the most widely used facial nerve grading system. Its limitations include inter-observer variability (13–44% disagreement) and the fusion of static and dynamic criteria. The Sunnybrook scale is increasingly used as it allows better assessment of recovery and includes assessment of synkinesis.

### Facial Nerve Management in Malignant Disease

Most pre-operatively intact nerves can be dissected free from the tumour. The nerve should only be resected when pre-operatively paralyzed or intra-operatively invaded/surrounded by tumour. There is consensus that microscopic disease left on a spared nerve can be controlled by post-operative radiotherapy. When nerve resection is performed, frozen section of cut margins is recommended and immediate cable grafting performed — the greater auricular nerve is the preferred donor (good diameter, easy access). The sural nerve is an alternative. Results of cable grafting typically achieve House-Brackmann grade III–IV, with EMG signs of reinnervation from 4.5 months and final function realized by 2 years.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The parotid is the largest salivary gland (~25 g) — it is the last to encapsulate embryologically, entrapping lymphatics (explaining Warthin's tumour and intraparotid nodal metastases from skin cancers)
> 2. The facial nerve divides the parotid into superficial (80–90% of tissue) and deep lobes — five terminal branches arise with cross-innervation most common between zygomatic and buccal branches
> 3. The tympanomastoid suture is an extremely reliable landmark for identifying the facial nerve trunk — the nerve lies immediately deep and inferior to this groove
> 4. Pleomorphic adenoma capsule is exceptionally thin (< 20 microns) and incomplete in ~50% — the myxoid stroma makes the tumour fragile, and intra-operative rupture seeds the field increasing recurrence risk
> 5. Recurrence of pleomorphic adenoma is typically multicentric and occurs around one decade after primary surgery — the rate of malignant change is ~3% in recurrent tumours versus 0.15% in primary
> 6. The inverse relationship between gland size and malignancy risk is a fundamental principle — ~20% of parotid, ~50% of submandibular, and >90% of sublingual tumours are malignant
> 7. AdCC is notorious for perineural spread with skip lesions and distant pulmonary metastasis in ~70% — it is considered a clinically high-grade malignancy despite sometimes optimistic 5-year survival figures
> 8. The N+ neck in salivary gland carcinoma requires comprehensive neck dissection (levels I–V) — risk factors for occult metastasis in the N0 neck include T > 4 cm, high-grade histology, age > 54, and extraparotid extension
> 9. The House-Brackmann system is a 6-grade scale (I = normal, VI = total paralysis) endorsed by the American Academy of Otolaryngology — limitations include 13–44% inter-observer disagreement
> 10. When the facial nerve requires resection for malignancy, immediate cable grafting with the greater auricular nerve achieves House-Brackmann grade III–IV — radiotherapy does not impair grafting outcomes

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A retromandibular fossa or cheek mass should be considered a salivary gland tumour until proven otherwise — USgFNAC is the standard initial investigation
> - Warthin's tumour has a strong association with smoking (incidence doubled) and 10–15% bilaterality — in elderly patients with diagnostic FNAC, conservative observation is acceptable
> - Frey's syndrome is caused by misdirected parasympathetic nerve regeneration along sympathetic fibres to sweat glands — subcutaneous botulinum toxin injection is the current treatment of choice, requiring repeat at 6–12 monthly intervals
> - Post-operative radiotherapy in salivary gland carcinoma can be omitted only for completely resected stage I–II low-grade MEC and AcCC without adverse pathological features — for all other patients, it significantly improves locoregional control
> - First bite syndrome following cervico-parotid approach causes intense pain at the onset of eating that quickly passes — it occurs due to sympathetic denervation of remaining parotid tissue and is generally self-limiting
