# Salivary Physiology, Sialadenosis, Ranula and Non-neoplastic Salivary Diseases

**Question:** *Discuss developmental dental cysts, physiology of salivation, and [[Sjogren Syndrome|Sjogren's syndrome]]. Describe the causes of sialadenosis and management of submandibular [[Sialolithiasis and Sialendoscopy|sialolithiasis]]. Discuss saliva formation, regulation, and function, and electrogustometry. Describe plunging ranula -- definition, clinical features, and management. Classify non-neoplastic salivary diseases, and discuss sialectasis.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 44 (Physiology of the Salivary Glands) and Chapter 46 (Non-Neoplastic Salivary Gland Diseases); Volume 2, Chapter 112 (Facial Nerve Testing)

## INTRODUCTION

The salivary glands are essential for oral health, performing complex secretory functions that maintain lubrication, antimicrobial defence, digestion, buffering, and remineralization of teeth. The three paired major glands -- parotid, submandibular, and sublingual -- together with 600-1000 minor salivary glands produce 1-1.5 litres of saliva daily through a tightly regulated two-phase secretion process under autonomic control. Non-neoplastic diseases of the salivary glands represent a diverse group of disorders ranging from obstructive sialolithiasis to autoimmune Sjogren's syndrome, metabolic sialadenosis, and developmental lesions such as ranula. Understanding salivary physiology is fundamental to diagnosing and managing these conditions. This answer comprehensively addresses salivary physiology and its assessment, electrogustometry, sialadenosis, ranula, sialolithiasis management, Sjogren's syndrome, and the classification of non-neoplastic salivary diseases including sialectasis.

## PART A: SALIVARY PHYSIOLOGY -- FORMATION, REGULATION AND FUNCTION

### Microanatomy of the Salivary Gland Unit

The secretory unit consists of the acinus enveloped by myoepithelial cells, followed distally by the intercalated duct, striated duct, and excretory duct.

| Component | Structure | Function |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| Acinus | Serous, mucous, or mixed cells with secretory granules | Produces primary secretion (isotonic, plasma-like) |
| Myoepithelial cells | Contractile pseudopodia surrounding acini and intercalated ducts | Peristalsis to move saliva distally |
| Intercalated duct | Low cuboidal epithelium; rich in carbonic anhydrase | Secretes HCO3-, absorbs Cl- |
| Striated duct | Cuboidal cells with basal membrane folding + mitochondria | Absorbs Na+, secretes K+ -> hypotonic modification |
| Excretory duct | Cuboidal to pseudostratified columnar epithelium | No modification; conveys saliva |

### Gland Types and Secretory Characteristics

- **Parotid gland:** Purely serous (protein-secreting); rich in amylase granules
- **Submandibular gland:** Mixed, predominantly serous
- **Sublingual gland:** Mixed, predominantly mucinous
- **Minor salivary glands:** 600-1000 glands in oral/oropharyngeal mucosa; mucin-rich; flow independent of stimulation; contribute 7-8% of total output

### Two-Phase Secretion Model

**Phase 1 -- Primary Secretion (Acinar):**
- Active process by acinar cells
- Product is isotonic, similar in composition and osmolality to plasma
- Driven by neurotransmitter stimulation (acetylcholine and noradrenaline binding to basolateral receptors)

**Phase 2 -- Ductal Modification:**
- Intercalated ducts: secrete bicarbonate, absorb chloride (carbonic anhydrase-mediated)
- Striated ducts: absorb sodium from lumen, secrete potassium into lumen
- Net result: progressively hypotonic saliva
- Duct epithelium is impermeable to water, so active ion transport creates the hypotonic composition
- Longer striated ducts (serous glands) = greater modification; short/absent in mucous glands = minimal modification

### Mechanisms of Ion Transport in Salivary Acini

Three concurrent mechanisms produce primary salivary fluid secretion:

1. **Mechanism 1 (NaCl-driven):** Na+/Cl- co-transporter on basolateral membrane + Ca++-activated K+ channel (basolateral) + apical Ca++-activated Cl- channel + basal Na+/K+ ATPase. Parasympathetic stimulation raises intracellular Ca++, opening K+ and Cl- channels. Cl- flows into lumen; Na+ follows electrochemically; water follows osmotically.

2. **Mechanism 2 (Cl-/HCO3- exchange):** Cl-/HCO3- exchanger acts in tandem with Na+/H+ exchanger. Fall in intracellular Cl- (from parasympathetic-stimulated KCl loss) drives entry of more Cl- in exchange for HCO3-.

3. **Mechanism 3 (HCO3- secretion):** CO2 enters basolaterally, converted to HCO3- which exits via apical anion channel. Protons expelled by basolateral Na+/H+ exchanger.

### Salivary Composition

Saliva comprises 99.5% water, plus proteins, glycoproteins, and electrolytes:

- High in K+ (7x plasma)
- Low in Na+ (1/10 plasma)
- Contains calcium, phosphorus, chloride, thiocyanate, and urea
- pH range: 5.6-7.4

### Salivary Flow Rates

- **Total output:** 1-1.5 litres per 24 hours
- **Basal flow rate:** 0.001-0.2 mL/min/gland
- **Stimulated flow rate:** 0.18-1.7 mL/min/gland

| State | Submandibular | Parotid | Sublingual |
|-------|---------------|---------|------------|
| Unstimulated | 69% | 26% | 5% |
| Stimulated | 26% | 69% | 5% |

The relative contributions of parotid and submandibular glands reverse between unstimulated and stimulated states. Most saliva is secreted during mastication. Minor gland output is independent of stimulation.

### Autonomic Regulation

**Parasympathetic (Primary driver -- tonic stimulation):**
- Stimulates exocytosis, vasodilation of gland blood supply, and myoepithelial contraction
- Interruption causes glandular atrophy

**Sympathetic (Intermittent modulation):**
- Acts in opposition to parasympathetic input
- Modulates saliva composition by varying exocytosis from specific duct-lining cells
- Interruption causes no significant change in gland architecture

| Pathway | Parotid Gland | Submandibular/Sublingual |
|---------|---------------|--------------------------|
| Nucleus | Inferior salivatory nucleus | Superior salivatory nucleus |
| Cranial nerve | CN IX (glossopharyngeal) | CN VII (facial) |
| Preganglionic route | Lesser superficial petrosal nerve | Chorda tympani |
| Ganglion | Otic ganglion | Submandibular ganglion |
| Postganglionic nerve | Auriculotemporal nerve | Direct fibres (no true synapse); release ACh near glands |

Sympathetic postganglionic fibres travel from the superior cervical ganglion along the external carotid artery and its branches to reach the glands.

### Functions of Saliva

- **Lubrication:** Mucins, proline-rich glycoproteins, water
- **Antimicrobial defence:** Amylase, lysozyme, lactoferrin, lactoperoxidase, defensins, histatins, cystatins, secretory IgA, complement, statherin
- **Growth factors:** EGF, TGF-alpha, TGF-beta, FGF, IGF-1, IGF-2, NGF
- **Mucosal integrity:** Mucins, electrolytes, water
- **Buffering:** Bicarbonate, phosphate ions, proteins
- **Remineralization:** Calcium, phosphate, statherin, anionic proline-rich proteins
- **Digestion:** Amylase, lipase, ribonuclease, proteases
- **Taste facilitation:** Water, gustin (dissolves food solutes)
- **Speech:** Water, mucins (oral cavity lubrication)
- **Lavage/cleansing:** Water

## PART B: XEROSTOMIA AND SALIVARY FLOW ASSESSMENT

### Xerostomia

Xerostomia (reduced salivary flow) may be subjective or objective. Drugs are the most common cause in clinical practice.

**Drug classes causing xerostomia:**

| Anticholinergic Activity | Sympathomimetic Activity |
|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| Atropine and analogues | Decongestants (ephedrine) |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Bronchodilators |
| MAO inhibitors | Appetite suppressants |
| Phenothiazines/neuroleptics | Amphetamines |
| Anti-Parkinsonian agents | Diethylpropion |
| Antihistamines | |
| Ganglion blockers/clonidine | |
| Antiemetics | |

Other causes include depression/anxiety states, dehydration (haemorrhage, diarrhoea, chronic vomiting, polyuria, diuretics), and specific salivary gland diseases.

### Collection and Measurement of Saliva

- **Methods:** Spitting, drainage, suction, cotton wool rolls
- **Parotid duct collection:** Carlson-Crittenden cup (placed on buccal mucosa over parotid duct ampulla; has collecting chamber separated from outer suction chamber) or polythene catheter cannulation
- **Submandibular/sublingual:** Syringe collection (individual gland measurement technically impossible due to proximity of duct orifices and communicating channels)
- **Stimulation:** Local sialagogues (5% citric acid on tongue dorsum); systemic sialagogues (pilocarpine -- research use)

### Sjogren's Syndrome -- Salivary Diagnostic Criteria

- Unstimulated whole saliva flow rate < 0.1 mL/min = diagnostic of salivary gland hypofunction
- Stimulated saliva < 0.5 mL/min = consistent with Sjogren's syndrome
- 2016 ACR/EULAR unified criteria: scoring system based on labial gland biopsy histopathology (focus score >1 per 4 mm2), anti-Ro/La antibodies, ocular staining score, Schirmer test, and whole saliva flow rate

### Sialochemistry in Disease

| Condition | Sialochemical Changes |
|-----------|-----------------------|
| Sjogren's syndrome | Raised Na+, Cl-, P+ (parotid); raised protein, beta-2 microglobulin |
| Cystic fibrosis | Raised Na+, Ca++, P+; raised proteins, amylase, lysozyme |
| Sialadenitis | Raised Na+, K+, Ca++, P+ |
| Radiation damage | Raised Na+, Ca++, Mg++, Cl- |
| Diabetes mellitus | Raised Ca++; raised protein, IgA, IgG, IgM, glucose |
| Hyperparathyroidism | Raised Ca++; raised total protein |
| Aldosteronism | Depressed Na+, raised K+ |

## PART C: ELECTROGUSTOMETRY

The chorda tympani nerve (branch of CN VII) carries taste sensation from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. It also provides parasympathetic secretomotor fibres to the submandibular and sublingual glands via the submandibular ganglion.

### Principle

Electrogustometry involves electrical stimulation of the tongue surface to produce a metallic taste sensation. The threshold at which taste is perceived is measured and compared between the two sides.

### Clinical Applications

- **Pre-operative assessment:** Establishes baseline chorda tympani function before middle ear surgery
- **Post-operative monitoring:** Documents chorda tympani integrity after [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]], mastoid surgery, or [[Stapes Surgery|stapes surgery]]
- **Facial nerve assessment:** Used alongside other tests of facial nerve branches (Schirmer test for greater petrosal nerve, stapedial reflex for nerve to stapedius)
- **Threshold comparison:** A significant unilateral elevation in threshold suggests chorda tympani dysfunction on that side

### Comparison with Chemical Gustometry

- **Electrogustometry:** Electrical stimulus; produces metallic taste; rapid; quantitative threshold comparison
- **Chemical gustometry:** Uses taste strips or regional application of sweet/sour/salty/bitter solutions; tests specific taste modalities; more physiological but less precise for lateralized comparison

A reduction of 25% in salivary flow from the submandibular gland (measured by cannulating Wharton's duct and applying 6% citric acid stimulus) is considered abnormal and correlates with chorda tympani dysfunction.

## PART D: SIALADENOSIS (SIALOSIS)

### Definition

Sialadenosis is an uncommon non-neoplastic and non-inflammatory disorder giving rise to bilateral painless enlargement of the major salivary glands, typically the parotid glands.

### Epidemiology

- Peak incidence: 5th and 6th decades
- Slight female predominance
- Parotid glands typically affected with often profound enlargement
- Slow onset; may have some decrease in salivary flow, but xerostomia is not a dominant symptom

### Pathogenesis

The underlying mechanism is thought to be an autonomic neuropathy:
- Loss of parasympathetic activity
- Increased sympathetic drive
- Results in unopposed increase in protein content within acinar cells (protein accumulation without adequate secretion)

### Histopathology

- Acinar cell hypertrophy
- Atrophy of striated ducts
- Oedema of interstitial connective tissue
- Ultimately: widespread fatty replacement of acini

### Causes and Associations

| Category | Specific Causes |
|----------|-----------------|
| Endocrine | Diabetes mellitus (unrelated to duration/metabolic control); hypothyroidism |
| Nutritional | Malnutrition |
| Hepatic | Alcoholic and other hepatic cirrhosis |
| Eating disorders | Bulimia nervosa (up to 68% of patients; correlates with bulimic symptom frequency and serum amylase) |
| Hormonal | Puberty; menopause |
| Drug-induced | Antihypertensive medication |

### Management

- **Primary:** Correct the underlying systemic disorder
- **Bulimia nervosa:** Cessation of vomiting is generally associated with resolution of parotid swelling; one report of pilocarpine successfully resolving sialosis in bulimia
- **Endocrine/hepatic causes:** Often resistant to treatment even when underlying disorder is addressed
- **Surgical:** Superficial [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]] as last resort for cosmetically significant enlargement (carries surgical risks)
- No specific pharmacological therapy exists for sialosis itself

## PART E: RANULA

### Definition and Etymology

A ranula is a cystic lesion that develops from extravasation of saliva, possibly following trauma to the sublingual gland or obstruction of its ducts. The term derives from the Latin word for "small frog" -- so named because it causes croaking speech and the oral swelling resembles a frog's belly.

### Pathology

- **Pseudocyst:** Connective tissue-lined (NOT epithelial-lined)
- Filled with extravasated saliva (mucus)
- Prevalence: 0.2 per 1000 people
- Typical presentation: 2nd decade of life; possible female predisposition
- Appears as a soft, blue-tinged swelling of the floor of mouth

### Classification

| Feature | Simple (Intra-oral) Ranula | Plunging (Deep) Ranula |
|---------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Location | Above mylohyoid muscle | Below mylohyoid into submandibular space |
| Presentation | Floor of mouth swelling; blue-tinged | Painless persistent/recurrent neck swelling |
| Extension | Confined to oral cavity | May extend to upper chest wall (rare) |
| Imaging (US) | Simple cystic lesion | Low-attenuation cystic lesion extending below mylohyoid |
| Imaging (MRI) | Low T1, high T2 signal | Similar signal extending into submandibular space |

### Predisposing Factors

- **Congenital predisposition:** Higher occurrence in some ethnic groups and among siblings
- **HIV infection:** Increased occurrence due to blocked salivary ducts from inflammation and peri-ductal fibrosis as part of HIV-associated salivary gland disease
- Rarely, neonatal ranulas may require EXIT (ex utero intrapartum treatment)
- Respiratory obstruction secondary to ranula is rare

### Investigation

- Ultrasound: Simple cystic lesion in floor of mouth
- CT: Solitary, low-attenuation cystic lesion; plunging type shows extension through mylohyoid
- MRI: Low T1 and high T2 signal; best for delineating plunging ranulas
- FNAB: Cytological examination may aid diagnosis

### Management

**Surgical:**
- **Excision of sublingual gland** = lowest rate of recurrence (gold standard)
- Complications of sublingual gland excision:
  - Lingual nerve injury
  - Wharton's duct injury (stenosis, obstructive sialadenitis, ductal laceration)
  - Bleeding and haematoma
- Other approaches: marsupialisation, incision and drainage (higher recurrence)
- Success rates range from 0-100% depending on technique

**Medical:**
- Nickel gluconate (oral administration): At low doses, promotes enzymatic functions and tissue-repairing processes in salivary glands
- Promising results in case series with no recurrence and lower side effects
- Currently experimental

## PART F: CLASSIFICATION OF NON-NEOPLASTIC SALIVARY DISEASES AND SIALECTASIS

### Comprehensive Classification

| Category | Conditions |
|----------|------------|
| **Obstructive** | Sialolithiasis; mucus plugs; ductal strictures (focal, beaded, diffuse) |
| **Infective -- Viral** | Mumps (paramyxovirus); HIV salivary gland disease; HCV sialadenitis; EBV; CMV |
| **Infective -- Bacterial** | Acute suppurative sialadenitis; chronic non-specific sialadenitis; TB; actinomycosis |
| **Autoimmune** | Sjogren's syndrome (primary and secondary); IgG4-related disease (Kuttner tumour/Mikulicz disease) |
| **Granulomatous** | Sarcoidosis (Heerfordt syndrome); Wegener's granulomatosis |
| **Metabolic** | Sialadenosis/sialosis |
| **Drug-related** | Xerostomia (anticholinergics, sympathomimetics); drug-induced swelling (l-asparaginase, clozapine); iodine-induced sialadenitis |
| **Radiation-related** | Post-RT sialadenitis; radioactive iodine-induced sialadenitis |
| **Developmental** | Ranula (simple/plunging); salivary gland agenesis/aplasia; heterotopic salivary gland tissue |
| **Cystic** | Mucoceles; lymphoepithelial cysts (HIV); benign lymphoepithelial lesions |
| **Other** | Pneumoparotitis; recurrent parotitis of childhood; chronic graft-versus-host disease; drooling |

### Sjogren's Syndrome -- Key Features

Sjogren's syndrome is the second most common autoimmune connective tissue disorder, characterized by xerostomia and xerophthalmia from lymphocytic infiltration of salivary and lacrimal glands.

- **Primary:** Only eyes and mouth affected
- **Secondary:** Xerostomia + xerophthalmia + connective tissue disorder (rheumatoid arthritis, SLE)
- **Prevalence:** 0.2-3% of populations; 9x more common in women; detected in 4th-6th decade
- **Oral features:** Profound dryness causing dysarthria, dysphagia, accelerated dental caries (cervical), candidal infections, dysgeusia
- **Malignant potential:** 4.3% risk of malignancy (standardized incidence ratio 18.9); low-grade marginal-zone MALT lymphoma most common
- **Diagnosis:** ACR/EULAR 2016 criteria (labial gland biopsy focus score >1/4mm2 + anti-Ro/La + Schirmer test + salivary flow)
- **Treatment:** Pilocarpine 5 mg QID (muscarinic agonist); cevimeline 30 mg TID; salivary substitutes; sugar-free chewing gum; biological therapies (rituximab) for systemic disease

### Sialolithiasis -- Submandibular Management

Sialoliths preferentially affect the submandibular gland (83% of cases) due to high alkalinity, calcium content of submandibular saliva, and strong parasympathetic secretory drive.

**Management approach:**

- **Small, accessible stones:** Manual expression from submandibular duct by palpation
- **Anterior duct stones:** Transoral surgical removal (risk of stricture formation)
- **Minimally invasive (gland-sparing):** Interventional sialoendoscopy (SE) with/without extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL); success rates up to 93% sialolith removal, 95% symptom relief
- **Posterior duct/intraglandular stones:** Transcervical surgical excision of submandibular gland when minimally invasive methods unavailable or unsuccessful
- **Large stones (>10 mm):** ESWL + SE; may need open surgical approach
- **Adjunctive:** Alpha-1-blocker alfuzosin may reduce symptoms of ductal stenosis

### Sialectasis

Sialectasis refers to dilatation of the salivary duct system, seen in chronic and recurrent sialadenitis. It represents irreversible ductal damage from repeated episodes of infection and inflammation.

**Types on sialography:**

- **Punctate sialectasis:** Small dot-like collections of contrast; follows repeated infections; described as "snowstorm" pattern
- **Globular sialectasis:** Larger rounded areas of ductal dilatation; seen in advanced chronic sialadenitis and Sjogren's syndrome
- **Cavitary sialectasis:** Large cavities representing extensive ductal/parenchymal destruction; associated with benign lymphoepithelial lesions in Sjogren's

**Clinical significance:**
- Characteristic of recurrent parotitis of childhood (non-obstructive sialectasis; may be bilateral even if clinical disease is unilateral)
- Seen in chronic non-specific sialadenitis alongside ductal strictures
- Sjogren's syndrome: progressive from punctate to globular to cavitary, correlating with disease severity (Rubin and Holt grading)
- May develop after repeated episodes of pneumoparotitis
- US, conventional sialography, and MR sialography all demonstrate sialectasis

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Saliva production occurs in two phases: isotonic primary secretion by acinar cells, followed by ductal modification (Na+ absorption, K+ secretion) producing hypotonic final saliva high in K+ (7x plasma) and low in Na+ (1/10 plasma).
>
> 2. The relative contributions of parotid and submandibular glands reverse between unstimulated (submandibular 69%, parotid 26%) and stimulated (parotid 69%, submandibular 26%) states.
>
> 3. Parasympathetic innervation is the primary tonic driver of salivary secretion; the parotid is supplied via CN IX through the otic ganglion, while the submandibular/sublingual are supplied via CN VII through the chorda tympani and submandibular ganglion.
>
> 4. Electrogustometry assesses chorda tympani function by electrically stimulating the tongue to produce a metallic taste, with thresholds compared between sides -- essential before and after middle ear surgery.
>
> 5. Sialadenosis is a non-inflammatory bilateral painless parotid enlargement caused by autonomic neuropathy (loss of parasympathetic activity + increased sympathetic drive), associated with diabetes, hypothyroidism, hepatic cirrhosis, and bulimia nervosa (up to 68%).
>
> 6. Ranula is a connective tissue-lined pseudocyst (NOT epithelium-lined) from extravasated sublingual gland saliva; simple type lies above mylohyoid, plunging type extends below mylohyoid into the submandibular space.
>
> 7. Excision of the sublingual gland is the treatment with the lowest recurrence rate for ranula, though it carries risks of lingual nerve and Wharton's duct injury.
>
> 8. Sjogren's syndrome is the second most common autoimmune connective tissue disorder with a 4.3% risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma (usually MALT type); diagnostic criteria include labial gland biopsy focus score >1/4mm2 and anti-Ro/La antibodies.
>
> 9. Submandibular sialolithiasis management has been transformed by minimally invasive sialoendoscopy with/without extracorporeal lithotripsy, achieving up to 93% sialolith removal rates and preserving gland function.
>
> 10. Sialectasis (punctate, globular, cavitary types on sialography) represents irreversible ductal dilatation from chronic inflammation and is characteristic of recurrent parotitis of childhood, chronic sialadenitis, and Sjogren's syndrome.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Drugs are the most common cause of xerostomia** in clinical practice -- always review medication history (anticholinergics, tricyclics, phenothiazines, antihistamines) before investigating salivary gland pathology.
>
> - **The Carlson-Crittenden cup** is the gold-standard device for isolated parotid gland saliva collection; submandibular and sublingual gland outputs cannot be individually separated due to communicating duct channels.
>
> - **In bulimia nervosa**, parotid enlargement may affect up to 68% of patients and correlates with bulimic symptom frequency and serum amylase; cessation of vomiting generally resolves the swelling.
>
> - **Plunging ranula** should be considered in the differential diagnosis of any painless recurrent neck swelling in a young patient, particularly in HIV-positive individuals where ductal inflammation and fibrosis promote ranula formation.
>
> - **Sjogren's syndrome** risk factors for lymphoma development include splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, persistent salivary gland enlargement, low C4, high beta-2 microglobulin, low CD4 T-cells, and mixed cryoglobulinaemia -- these patients require vigilant surveillance.
>
> - **In sialolithiasis**, 90% of parotid stones are radiolucent while 90% of submandibular stones are radio-opaque -- ultrasound detects both types and is the preferred first-line imaging modality.
