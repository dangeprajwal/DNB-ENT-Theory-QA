# Sialolithiasis — Aetiology, Investigation and Management Including Sialendoscopy

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Submandibular duct stone removal — options. Sialendoscopy — indications and complications.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 46 (Non-Neoplastic Salivary Gland Diseases), Chapter 45 (Imaging of the Salivary Glands)

---

## Introduction

Sialolithiasis is a common disorder characterized by the formation of calculi (sialoliths) within the ductal system of a salivary gland. Sialoliths are significantly more common in the submandibular glands (83%) than the parotid (10%) or sublingual (7%) glands. This distribution is explained by the high alkalinity and calcium content of submandibular saliva together with its strong parasympathetic secretory drive. Necropsy studies reveal that 1% of submandibular glands harbour sialoliths, while sialomicroliths (microscopic concretions) are present in almost all submandibular glands. Sialolithiasis is more common in females, much more likely in adults than children, and does not always produce clinical symptoms — incidental sialoliths on ultrasound scanning of asymptomatic patients are not uncommon. The management of sialolithiasis has been significantly advanced by the introduction of minimally invasive, gland-sparing therapies including interventional sialendoscopy.

---

## PART A: Aetiology and Pathophysiology

### Composition and Formation

The sialoliths are composed of both organic and non-organic material. The central medulla is more organic, while calcium phosphate is the predominant salt of the calculi, laid down as concentric shells of approximately 10 micrometres thickness. The likely cause of sialolith formation remains unknown, although stasis caused by sialomicroliths with resultant inflammation and fibrosis and further stagnation of calcium-rich fluid can drive sialolith formation. Sialomicroliths need not be the origin of all sialoliths.

### Predisposing Factors

- High alkalinity and calcium content of submandibular saliva
- Strong parasympathetic secretory drive of the submandibular gland
- Longer, more tortuous Wharton's duct with antigravity flow
- Possible associations with diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic liver disease and nephrolithiasis — but not water hardness
- Hyperparathyroidism may increase incidence, and these patients have a greater risk of nephrocalcinosis
- The anti-HIV agent atazanavir has been associated with sialolithiasis

### Submandibular Gland Predominance

The submandibular gland accounts for approximately 83% of sialoliths because its saliva is more alkaline, has a higher calcium content than parotid saliva, and Wharton's duct is longer and flows in an antigravity direction, promoting stasis and stone formation.

---

## PART B: Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

### Clinical Features

Obstruction of a major duct causes pain and swelling, typically in the submandibular gland, related to gustation or eating. The swelling is diffuse, develops rapidly, is often associated with a burning-like local pain, and is generally non-tender. It gradually resolves over a few hours. Long-standing sialolithiasis may lead to acute suppurative sialadenitis or chronic non-specific sialadenitis. Minor salivary gland sialoliths present as painless hard swellings that rarely form mucoceles.

### Investigations

| Investigation | Details |
|---|---|
| Plain radiograph | Occlusal/lateral skull views; detects large radiopaque stones; up to 30% are radiolucent (especially parotid) |
| Ultrasound | Detects both radiopaque and radiolucent stones; widely available; first-line in many centres |
| Sialography | Demonstrates ductal anatomy and stone location; technically difficult for SMG |
| CT / MRI | Useful adjuncts; CT superior for calcified stones |
| Sialendoscopy | Diagnostic and therapeutic; first-line where available |

Pan-oral tomograms are not helpful due to their tomographic nature. Where available, sialendoscopy may serve as a first-line investigative tool combining diagnosis with immediate treatment.

### Conservative and Transoral Approaches

When small and accessible, sialoliths may be expressed manually from the submandibular duct by bimanual palpation. Many can also be removed via a transoral approach if within the anterior segment of the submandibular duct. However, transoral removal carries a risk of stricture formation. Transcervical surgical removal of an affected gland may be the only effective treatment for calculi in the posterior aspect of the duct or within the gland itself, when minimally invasive methods are unavailable. A submental approach that lessens post-operative scarring and provides good cosmetic outcomes has also been described.

### Minimally Invasive Approaches

The management of sialolithiasis has been significantly advanced by minimally invasive, gland-sparing therapies that can often be undertaken under local anaesthesia in both children and adults.

**Sialendoscopy (SE):** Interventional sialendoscopy includes endoscopy with possible laser cutting of intraductal fibrosis and basket retrieval of stones. It is particularly useful for small distal sialoliths and when there are multiple stones.

**Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL):** When sialendoscopy alone is unsuccessful for small distal sialoliths, or the sialolith is proximal, mobile or intraparenchymal, ESWL followed by sialendoscopy with basket retrieval of fragments may be required. ESWL is not available in the United States.

**Intracorporeal lithotripsy:** Sialolith fragmentation with XeCl excimer, flash-lamp pulsed dye, Ho:YAG or erbium:YAG lasers is now possible as the laser fibres are of small diameter. However, the lasers are expensive, the procedure is time-consuming, and there is a risk of perforation of the duct and abscess formation necessitating gland removal.

**Size-based approach:** Sialoliths up to 10 mm require ESWL combined with sialendoscopy. Larger stones or those unresponsive to minimally invasive techniques may require an open surgical approach.

### Gland Excision

Transcervical submandibular gland excision remains indicated for:
- Calculi in the posterior duct or within the gland parenchyma when minimally invasive methods fail or are unavailable
- Chronic sialadenitis with irreversible glandular destruction
- Recurrent stones despite minimally invasive treatment

## PART D: Sialendoscopy — Technique and Outcomes

### Indications

- Sialolithiasis (both diagnostic and therapeutic)
- Ductal stenosis
- Mucous plugging
- Recurrent sialadenitis of unclear aetiology
- Juvenile recurrent parotitis

### Outcomes

Surgeon-based results report sialolith removal achieved in up to 93% of patients and relief of symptoms reported by up to 95%. Patient-orientated outcomes report resolution in 86% of patients with sialolithiasis and a significant improvement in quality of life and the Glasgow Benefit Inventory. Minimally invasive procedures are also applicable to the management of ductal obstruction due to stenosis and mucous plugging.

### Complications

| Complication | Details |
|---|---|
| Ductal perforation | Risk with intracorporeal lithotripsy; may necessitate gland excision |
| Abscess formation | Secondary to perforation or stone manipulation |
| Stricture formation | More common with transoral stone removal |
| Ranula / duct obstruction | Reported in 8% after SMG duct relocation |
| Upper airway obstruction | Significant but rare adverse effect |
| Basket impaction | Rare; may require conversion to open surgery |
| Incomplete stone removal | May require repeat procedure or ESWL |

### Additional Therapies

Preliminary data suggest that the alpha-1-blocker alfuzosin may reduce symptoms associated with ductal stenosis of the parotid gland or post-lithotripsy therapy of parotid sialoliths, but prospective studies are needed to confirm these benefits.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Sialolithiasis is most common in the submandibular gland (83%) due to the high alkalinity, calcium content and antigravity flow of Wharton's duct — parotid accounts for 10% and sublingual for 7%
> 2. Up to 30% of sialoliths (particularly parotid) are radiolucent — ultrasound detects both radiopaque and radiolucent stones and is the preferred first-line investigation
> 3. The sialoliths are composed of organic and non-organic material — calcium phosphate is the predominant salt, laid down in concentric shells of ~10 micrometres thickness
> 4. Small anterior duct stones can be removed by manual expression or transoral surgery — posterior or intraparenchymal stones require minimally invasive or open approaches
> 5. Interventional sialendoscopy achieves sialolith removal in up to 93% of patients and symptom relief in up to 95% — it can be performed under local anaesthesia in both adults and children
> 6. ESWL combined with sialendoscopy is indicated for proximal, mobile or intraparenchymal sialoliths and those up to 10 mm — ESWL is not available in the US
> 7. Intracorporeal lithotripsy using laser fibres carries a risk of ductal perforation and abscess formation that may necessitate gland excision
> 8. Long-standing sialolithiasis may cause acute suppurative sialadenitis or chronic non-specific sialadenitis — necropsy studies show sialomicroliths in almost all submandibular glands
> 9. Hyperparathyroidism is associated with increased incidence of sialolithiasis, and these patients have a greater risk of nephrocalcinosis
> 10. Transcervical gland excision remains indicated when minimally invasive methods fail or are unavailable — a submental approach offers improved cosmetic outcomes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Sialolithiasis does not always produce clinical symptoms — incidental sialoliths found on ultrasound in asymptomatic patients are not uncommon, and management should be guided by symptoms rather than imaging alone
> - The characteristic clinical presentation is diffuse, rapidly developing, non-tender submandibular swelling associated with eating that resolves over a few hours — this gustatory relationship is highly suggestive of obstruction
> - Transoral removal of anterior submandibular duct stones, while effective, carries a risk of stricture formation — sialendoscopy has lower morbidity and preserves gland function
> - Sialendoscopy is applicable beyond sialolithiasis — it is also effective for ductal stenosis, mucous plugging, recurrent sialadenitis and juvenile recurrent parotitis
> - The alpha-1-blocker alfuzosin may reduce symptoms of parotid ductal stenosis or following lithotripsy — preliminary data are promising but prospective confirmation is needed
