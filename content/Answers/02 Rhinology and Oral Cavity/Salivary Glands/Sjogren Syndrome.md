# Sjögren's Syndrome — Classification, Head and Neck Manifestations, Diagnosis and Treatment

> [!question] Questions
> Sjögren's syndrome — classification, head and neck manifestations, investigations and treatment.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 46 (Non-Neoplastic Salivary Gland Diseases)

---

## Introduction

Sjögren's syndrome is the second most common autoimmune connective tissue disorder, characterized by xerostomia and xerophthalmia due to profound lymphocytic infiltration into the salivary and lacrimal glands. It occurs in 0.2–3% of populations, has no geographic or ethnic associations, and is approximately nine times more common in women than men. Disease is usually detected in the 4th–6th decade but can arise in younger adults and occasionally in children under 10 years. Sjögren's syndrome probably remains underdiagnosed, possibly reflecting the generally mild nature of clinical signs in some patients. It is a potentially malignant disorder with a 4.3% risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, making awareness of its features essential for the ENT surgeon.

---

## PART A: Classification

### Primary Sjögren's Syndrome

Primary Sjögren's syndrome presents with symptoms and signs affecting only the eyes and mouth (sicca syndrome). However, this classification is somewhat simplistic, as many patients with primary Sjögren's have a spectrum of other systemic and often autoimmune phenomena including:
- Arthralgia (75% of patients)
- Myalgia (45%)
- Xerosis (30%)
- Vasculitic and other cutaneous rashes
- Raynaud's phenomenon (up to 15%)

### Secondary Sjögren's Syndrome

Secondary Sjögren's syndrome has xerostomia and xerophthalmia together with an associated connective tissue disorder, most frequently:
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Systemic sclerosis
- Mixed connective tissue disease

## PART B: Head and Neck Manifestations

### Oral Manifestations

The oral dryness of Sjögren's syndrome (both primary and secondary) can be profound, giving rise to:
- **Dysarthria and dysphagia** — due to mucosal dryness
- **Dental caries** — particularly cervical disease; retention of food on teeth, mucosa and gingiva increases caries frequency
- **Acute gingivitis** — but not periodontitis per se
- **Candidal infection** — notably acute pseudomembranous candidiasis, median rhomboid glossitis, chronic atrophic candidiasis (denture-associated stomatitis) and angular cheilitis
- **Dysgeusia and loss of taste** — patients report most foodstuffs taste 'cardboard-like'
- **Acute suppurative parotitis** — long-standing oral dryness increases liability

### Salivary Gland Manifestations

- Intermittent swelling of the major salivary glands, notably the parotid
- Parotid enlargement often reflects non-specific inflammatory change
- Solitary gland enlargement may represent chronic sialadenitis, acute suppurative parotitis or, importantly, non-Hodgkin lymphoma

### Malignancy Risk

Sjögren's syndrome has a 4.3% risk of malignancy with a standardized incident rate of 18.9. Low-grade marginal-zone lymphoma related to mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) is the most common lymphoma, but others include follicle-centre lymphoma, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and lymphoplasmacytoid lymphoma.

| Risk Factor for Lymphoma | Details |
|---|---|
| Splenomegaly | Associated with systemic disease |
| Lymphadenopathy | Persistent generalized |
| Salivary gland enlargement(s) | Persistent or progressive |
| Low C4 | Complement consumption |
| High beta-2 microglobulin | Marker of lymphoproliferation |
| Low CD4 T-cells | Immune dysregulation |
| Low IgM | B-cell dysfunction |
| Mixed cryoglobulinaemia | Strong predictor |

Treatment is usually CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisolone) and outcomes are extremely good, with 5-year survival rates of 85–100%.

## PART C: Diagnosis

### Diagnostic Criteria

The diagnostic criteria have evolved over the past decade. Until recently, two classification systems were in use — the American European Consensus Group (AECG) criteria and the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria. In 2016, a unified system was agreed — the ACR/EULAR criteria.

### ACR/EULAR 2016 Unified Criteria

The unified criteria are based on a scoring system using objective features:

| Criterion | Score |
|---|---|
| Labial gland biopsy: focus score ≥ 1 per 4 mm² | 3 |
| Anti-SSA/Ro positive | 3 |
| Ocular staining score ≥ 5 | 1 |
| Schirmer test ≤ 5 mm/5 min | 1 |
| Unstimulated whole saliva flow ≤ 0.1 ml/min | 1 |

A total score of ≥ 4 classifies a patient as having Sjögren's syndrome.

### Histopathology

Central to diagnosis is the histopathological detection of pathognomonic features within salivary gland tissue. This comprises an infiltrate of mononuclear lymphocytes scored as foci of 50 lymphocytes, plasma cells and macrophages (focus lymphadenitis). A focus score of number of foci per 4 mm² is determined and must be greater than 1 for Sjögren's syndrome. Unlike IgG4-related disease, germinal centres are present in Sjögren's syndrome. The presence of germinal centres may be a predictor of non-Hodgkin lymphoma development later in life. There is also loss of salivary acini and periductal fibrosis. Studies are typically performed on labial gland biopsy tissue.

### Serological Investigations

- Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) — present in up to 80%
- Rheumatoid factor (RF) — frequently detected
- Anti-SSA (anti-Ro) and anti-SSB (anti-La) antibodies — specific for Sjögren's
- Many asymptomatic people have circulating ANAs relevant to Sjögren's in low titre — these are not indicative of future risk

### Differential Diagnosis

The following conditions may mimic Sjögren's syndrome and must be excluded:
- HIV salivary gland disease (mimics Sjögren's but no anti-Ro/La)
- Hepatitis C virus sialadenitis
- Sarcoidosis
- Chronic graft-versus-host disease
- IgG4-related disease (obliterative phlebitis, storiform fibrosis, no germinal centres — unlike Sjögren's)
- Amyloidosis (rare)

## PART D: Treatment

### Salivary Substitutes

Synthetic salivary substitutes are available but patients generally find them unsatisfactory unless xerostomia is mild. About 40% of patients discontinue salivary substitutes and prefer to sip water regularly. A Cochrane review concluded that no salivary substitute could be recommended for long-standing oral dryness of any cause.

### Sialagogues

Two muscarinic acetylcholine receptor agonists are licensed for Sjögren's:

| Agent | Dose | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pilocarpine | 5 mg four times daily | RCTs: increases whole saliva flow; reduces oral, ocular, nasal, vaginal and skin dryness |
| Cevimeline | 30 mg three times daily | Double-blind studies: reduces xerostomia and dry eyes; not available in Europe |

Both stimulate M1 and M3 muscarinic receptors. Adverse effects of pilocarpine include sweating (43%), increased urinary frequency (10%) and flushing (10%). Cevimeline has similar adverse effects but with increased gastrointestinal symptoms and reduced urinary frequency.

### Biological Therapies

- **Rituximab:** Two small placebo-controlled RCTs (47 patients) showed improvements in sicca features, salivary flow, fatigue and quality of life. However, the large TEARS study (France) showed no sustained substantial alleviation of symptoms. The TRACTISS study (UK) was also undertaken
- **Anti-TNF agents:** Infliximab showed positive results in an open-label study but was not confirmed in a subsequent RCT of 103 patients. Etanercept showed no significant benefit in two studies
- **Interferon-alpha:** A large RCT of 497 patients found IFN-alpha only tended to improve unstimulated salivary flow
- **Belimumab:** Monoclonal antibody targeting soluble BAFF — phase II data suggest promise but further investigation is needed

### Other Pharmacological Agents

- **Corticosteroids:** One randomized study found prednisolone (30 mg alternate days) reduced dry mouth symptoms but failed to enhance flow rate. Corticosteroid irrigation of major glands increased flow. Overall, use is not supported by reliable evidence and is associated with high adverse events
- **Antimalarials:** Hydroxychloroquine may improve salivary function by inhibiting glandular cholinesterase — benefit sustained after 3–9 months; no large RCTs exist
- **Immunosuppressants:** Cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, methotrexate, leflunomide, mycophenolic acid and cyclosporine A have limited benefits for sicca features with an unacceptable rate of adverse events (41–100%)
- **Bromhexine:** 32–48 mg daily may increase salivary and lacrimal flow — limited published data

### Supportive Measures

- Sugar-free chewing gum to stimulate salivary flow
- Meticulous dental hygiene and regular dental review
- Antifungal therapy for oral candidiasis
- Management of associated connective tissue disorders

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Sjögren's syndrome probably remains underdiagnosed — it should be suspected in any middle-to-late-aged female with unexplained oral and ocular dryness, even with mild symptoms
> - The oral dryness can be so profound that patients describe all food as tasting 'cardboard-like' — this dysgeusia significantly impacts quality of life and nutritional intake
> - Solitary enlargement of a major salivary gland in a patient with known Sjögren's syndrome should raise suspicion of non-Hodgkin lymphoma — urgent investigation is warranted
> - IgG4-related disease can mimic Sjögren's syndrome clinically but the serological and histopathological features are distinctly different — IgG4 disease has elevated serum IgG4, obliterative phlebitis and storiform fibrosis, but no germinal centres or anti-Ro/La antibodies
> - No salivary substitute has been shown to be effective for long-term management — approximately 40% of patients discontinue these products and prefer simply sipping water regularly
