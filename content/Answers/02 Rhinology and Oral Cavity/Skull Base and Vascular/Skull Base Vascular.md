## Part A: Carotid Body Tumour and Paragangliomas

*Q16: Shamblin classification carotid body tumour. Class 3 — features, diagnosis, management (2021 Jun)*
*Q50: Carotid body tumour — features, investigations, treatment, surgical technique (2019 Jun)*

### Paragangliomas — Overview

Paraganglia are groups of cells of neural crest origin belonging to the extra-adrenal chromaffin and non-chromaffin cell system. They are believed to play a role in chemoreception. Paragangliomas are tumours that arise from paraganglia and head and neck paragangliomas may occur from paraganglia along the arterial vasculature and cranial nerves from the skull base to the aortic arch.

**Genetics and associations:**
- Approximately 30% of these tumours have a genetic origin related to the Succinate Dehydrogenase (SDH) gene locus
- The SDHA, SDHB, SDHC and SDHD genes are particularly associated with familial head and neck paragangliomas
- Up to 40% of patients with head and neck paragangliomas have an SDH gene mutation
- Head and neck paragangliomas may also be associated with MEN2a and MEN2b syndromes
- Associations described with Von Hippel-Lindau disease, renal cell carcinoma, neurofibromatosis and vestibular schwannoma
- Paragangliomas belong to the APUD (amine precursor uptake and decarboxylation) tumour group

**Terminology:**
- The term chemodectoma should be reserved for tumours originating from the chemoreceptor system (e.g., carotid sinus paraganglia)
- The term [[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]] is also technically inaccurate (glomus refers to arteriolar-venous connections in skin) but is commonly used in older literature
- Paraganglioma is the preferred modern term

### Carotid Body Tumour

**Clinical features:**
- Most present as an asymptomatic neck mass — a slowly growing painless mass in the upper neck at the carotid bifurcation
- Present for months to years before symptom onset; many are incidental diagnoses
- Approximately 10% are multicentric — whole body MRI should be performed to assess other sites (aortic arch, adrenal)
- Approximately 3% may be secretory and present with systemic symptoms associated with catecholamine release (hypertension, flushing, headaches, palpitations)
- All patients should be assessed for catecholamine secretion prior to surgery (24-hour urine collection for catecholamine metabolites or plasma free metanephrines) to avert anaesthetic complications from catecholamine release during surgical manipulation
- Slow-growing benign tumours — the decision to perform surgery needs careful consideration
- Cranial nerve palsies may be present if the tumour has reached significant size (hoarseness from vagal involvement, Horner syndrome from sympathetic chain involvement)

**Examination findings:**
- Pulsatile mass in the anterior triangle at the level of the hyoid bone
- Mobile horizontally but not vertically (Fontaine sign)
- May have an audible bruit
- Cranial nerve assessment (IX, X, XII, sympathetic chain) is essential

### Shamblin Classification of Carotid Body Tumours

The Shamblin classification categorizes carotid body tumours based on their relationship to the carotid vessels and predicts surgical difficulty and risk:

| Class | Description | Surgical Implications |
|---|---|---|
| I | Small, minimally attached to vessels | Easily dissected; low morbidity |
| II | Partially encasing the carotid vessels | Moderate difficulty; may require subadventitial dissection |
| III | Completely encasing the carotid vessels | High risk; may require vessel sacrifice and reconstruction |

**Class III tumours — features, diagnosis and management:**

Class III carotid body tumours are characterized by complete encasement of the internal and external carotid arteries. These tumours represent the highest surgical challenge. They are typically large (>5 cm), may present with cranial nerve deficits due to compression, and have the highest risk of vascular injury during surgical excision.

**Diagnosis:**
- Imaging is crucial — paragangliomas have characteristic imaging features including demonstration of vascularity and the so-called 'salt and pepper' appearance on MRI
- In carotid body tumours, there is displacement of the angle between the internal and external carotid arteries known as the **'Lyre' sign** (widening of the carotid bifurcation on angiography)
- CT neck shows a vascular tumour lying between the internal and external carotid arteries
- MRI with gadolinium shows the characteristic 'salt and pepper' appearance from flow voids (intratumoral haemorrhages)
- Carotid angiography/MR angiography — defines feeding vessels and relationship to carotid vessels
- Balloon occlusion test is performed in patients with lesions that encircle the internal carotid artery to assess the risk of neurovascular complications if vessel sacrifice becomes necessary

**Histology:**
- Both malignant and benign paragangliomas are comprised of the same two major cell types: epithelioid chief cells and sustentacular supporting cells
- Together these two cell types form clusters of cells (**Zellballen**), which are surrounded by extensive vascular sinusoids
- Difficult to discriminate between benign and malignant paraganglioma unless histological assessment is performed
- Histological markers such as central necrosis, vascular invasion, and mitosis or nuclear atypia do not appear to be correlated with metastasis or true invasion

### Investigations for Carotid Body Tumour

1. **CT neck with contrast** — demonstrates vascular tumour, bony detail, calcification, erosion
2. **MRI with gadolinium** — shows soft-tissue extent, intracranial extension, 'salt and pepper' appearance; T1 hypointense/isointense, T2 hyperintense with flow voids
3. **MR angiography/CT angiography** — delineates vasculature and relationship to tumour
4. **Conventional carotid angiography** — gold standard for vascular mapping; allows concurrent therapeutic interventions (embolization, balloon occlusion)
5. **Biochemical screening** — 24-hour urine catecholamine metabolites or plasma free metanephrines (essential pre-operatively)
6. **Genetic screening** — for SDH mutations, especially with family history, bilateral tumours, or multiple paragangliomas
7. **Whole body MRI/PET** — to exclude multicentric paragangliomas (10% multicentric)
8. **Fine-needle aspiration** — generally avoided due to vascularity and risk of haemorrhage; biopsy should not be performed

### Management of Carotid Body Tumours

**Treatment options:**

**1. Wait-and-see policy:**
- Paragangliomas are slow-growing benign tumours — may take years or decades for growth
- Appropriate for elderly patients, those with significant comorbidities, or small asymptomatic tumours
- Serial imaging with CT/MRI to monitor size

**2. Surgery:**
- Surgery is the primary treatment modality when intervention is indicated
- Usual indications include: suspected malignancy (multiple lesions, bone erosion, cranial nerve involvement, lymphadenopathy), increasing tumour size causing pressure effects on vascular and neurological structures
- The surgical complications from lower cranial nerve palsies may be more distressing than the lesion itself

**Pre-operative embolization:**
- Performed 24-48 hours before excision of vascular tumours via branches of the external carotid artery
- Pre-operative embolization significantly reduces blood loss during surgery and facilitates complete removal
- Carries a small risk of stroke
- Some surgeons advocate waiting 4-6 weeks after embolization prior to surgery

**Surgical technique:**
- Transcervical approach with a skin crease incision or doubly modified Blair's incision
- Identification and protection of the carotid vessels (ICA and ECA) and lower cranial nerves (X, XI, XII)
- Subadventitial dissection of the tumour from the carotid vessels — the tumour is dissected off the internal and external carotid arteries
- In Class III tumours — may require vascular reconstruction with saphenous vein graft or synthetic graft if ICA sacrifice becomes necessary
- For lesions extending to the skull base — transmastoid and infratemporal fossa approaches may be required

**3. [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]]:**
- Conventional fractionated radiotherapy and stereotactic radiosurgery have been demonstrated to be efficacious, providing long-term tumour control with a lower risk of iatrogenic cranial nerve damage compared to surgery
- The aim is to destroy the microvascular tissue and bring about sclerosis and fibrosis — the tumour will not disappear but may regress over time
- Appropriate for inoperable tumours, elderly patients, and those with significant comorbidities

### Fisch Classification (Temporal Bone Paragangliomas)

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| A | Limited to the middle ear (mesotympanum) |
| B | In the middle ear and mastoid |
| C | Originates in jugular bulb dome; subdivided by carotid involvement |
| D | Intracranial extension; subdivided by intradural extension |

## Part B: Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma

*Q106: Nasopharyngeal angiofibroma — pathological theories of genesis (2015 Dec)*

### Introduction

Juvenile angiofibroma (JA) is a unique tumour presenting almost exclusively in adolescent males. The tumour is rare, with an incidence of approximately 0.4 cases per 1 million inhabitants per year in Denmark. A higher incidence has been reported in Southern Europe and even more in Asia. Therapeutic options include surgery (therapy of choice), radiotherapy, and in rare cases, chemotherapy.

### Clinical Findings

The tumour usually arises from the lateral wall of the posterior nasal cavity close to the sphenopalatine foramen and the pterygoid base. Due to its early submucosal expansion towards the nasopharynx, it has frequently been termed 'nasopharyngeal angiofibroma'. Nasal obstruction and nasal bleeding are the usual presenting symptoms in adolescents.

**Pattern of spread:**
- JAs spread along natural foramina and fissures
- Bony erosions of the pterygoid, the clivus and the sphenoid sinus floor are common hallmarks
- Bony destruction of the sphenoid sinus floor leads to tumour extension into the sphenoid sinus — the tumour may fill the sinus almost completely without infiltrating the mucosa
- Lateral extension through the sphenopalatine foramen into the pterygopalatine fossa and further into the infratemporal fossa
- **Typical radiological findings**: widened sphenopalatine foramen and anterior displacement of the posterior maxillary sinus wall (Holman-Miller sign)
- The orbit may be reached via the inferior orbital fissure causing proptosis and/or optic nerve compression
- Intracranial extension mainly through the roof of the infratemporal fossa and via the superior orbital fissure
- JAs are mainly located in the extradural space when intracranial; dural infiltration or brain involvement is rare

### Blood Supply

- In early stages — supplied by the sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries
- In advanced stages — further branches of the external carotid artery (e.g., ascending pharyngeal artery), internal carotid arteries, and vertebral arteries
- Careful angiographic evaluation of ipsilateral and contralateral ECAs and ICAs is strongly recommended
- Pre-operative superselective embolization is recommended by most authors
- Some surgeons do not use pre-operative embolization as they close the feeding vessels before tumour removal and find it useful to detect possible tumour remnants by bleeding

### Tissue Architecture

JAs are benign fibrovascular tumours with characteristic irregularly-shaped vessels, which may be lined by endothelial cells only or show an incomplete vascular wall architecture. The surgeon is faced with profuse bleeding when opening these non-contractible pathological vessels during dissection.

**Key structural features:**
- The highest density of vessels, particularly those with characteristic irregular shape, is found below a pseudocapsule at the tumour surface — the surgeon should always dissect along the pseudocapsule to avoid severe bleeding
- Towards the centre of the tumour, the amount of fibrous tissue increases while vessel density decreases
- Based on clinical findings and specific vascular architecture, JAs have been defined as **vascular malformations rather than true neoplasms** — recent genetic and molecular findings support this theory

### Pathological Theories of Genesis

**1. First Branchial Arch Artery Theory (Primary theory):**

It has been proposed that the tumour origin is based on an incomplete regression of the first branchial arch artery. This theory is especially attractive as it explains different aspects of JAs:

- During embryological development, the first branchial arch artery finally recedes close to the pterygoid base and sphenopalatine foramen regions — explaining the proximity of tumour origin to these sites
- Vascular remnants of this artery are incorporated into the sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries themselves — explaining why the main blood supply in early stages is from these arteries
- The fact that this embryological vessel is connected to the C4-segment of the ICA explains the observation of a vascular supply from the ICA despite an anatomical distance between this vessel and the JA
- The 'branchial arch artery' theory elucidates the common finding of residual tumour at the pterygoid base and clivus and explains why it is necessary to drill the bone in this specific location to remove tumour remnants at the site of origin

**2. Vascular Malformation Theory:**
- Based on the characteristic vascular architecture with irregularly-shaped vessels lined by endothelial cells only, JAs have been defined as vascular malformations rather than true neoplasms
- Recent published genetic and molecular findings have supported this theory
- Genome-wide copy number profiling has identified specific disease-related genes (BORIS and TSHZ1) in juvenile angiofibroma

**3. Hormonal Theory:**
- The almost exclusive occurrence in adolescent males suggests hormonal influence
- Presence of androgen and oestrogen receptors within the tumour has been documented
- The tumour may represent an end-organ response to circulating androgens at puberty

**Tumour diagnosis is based on typical signs on CT, MRI and angiography. A biopsy should NOT be performed due to the risk of severe bleeding.**

### Endoscopic Resection — Surgical Considerations

Endoscopic resection has been proven to allow successful treatment of most JAs. The strategy takes the following considerations into account:

1. Opening of the maxillary sinus and exposure of its posterior wall early during surgery; bony posterior wall removed with through-cutting punches or drills
2. Identification and clipping/coagulation of the sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries (early control of main feeding vessel)
3. Resection of the posterior nasal septum to widen access and define tumour margins
4. Removal of the anterior sphenoid sinus wall to expose tumour within the sphenoid sinus
5. Dissection of the tumour off the pterygoid base, pterygoid canal and clivus
6. **Drilling at the pterygoid base and clivus at the end of surgery** is of utmost importance to avoid residual tumour at the site of origin
7. Endoscopic dissection should always follow the pseudocapsule to avoid injury of the tumour surface

**Indications for endoscopic approach:** JAs limited to the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, sphenoid sinus and pterygopalatine fossa. With increasing experience, can extend to infratemporal fossa and space posterior to the pterygoid plate.

**Contraindications to endoscopic approach:** Broad skull base infiltration, extensive blood supply from the internal carotid artery, encasement of the ICA, and brain infiltration.

## Part C: Cerebellopontine Angle (CPA)

*Q159: CPA surgical anatomy. CPA lesions. Surgical approaches (2011 Dec)*

### CPA Surgical Anatomy

The cerebellopontine angle (CPA) is a triangular space in the posterior cranial fossa located at the junction of the cerebellum, pons and medulla oblongata. The CPA cistern (pontocerebellar cistern) is bounded by the pons and medulla anteromedially, the cerebellum posteriorly, and the petrous surface of the temporal bone anterolaterally.

**Key structures traversing the CPA:**

| Structure | Course and Relations |
|---|---|
| CN V (Trigeminal) | Exits pons laterally, enters Meckel's cave |
| CN VI (Abducens) | Exits pontomedullary junction, ascends to cavernous sinus |
| CN VII (Facial) | Exits pontomedullary junction anterolaterally through CPA to IAM porus; lies anterosuperior in IAM |
| CN VIII (Vestibulocochlear) | Exits pons, traverses CPA with VII to enter IAM; lies posteroinferior to VII |
| AICA | Branch of basilar artery; courses through CPA, often looping into IAM |
| Flocculus | Cerebellar structure at lateral recess of fourth ventricle |
| Choroid plexus | Protrudes from foramen of Luschka |
| CN IX, X, XI | Lie inferiorly at pontomedullary junction |

**The internal auditory meatus (IAM):**
- Transmits CN VII and VIII, superior and inferior vestibular nerves, and the labyrinthine artery (branch of basilar artery)
- The facial nerve lies in the anterosuperior quadrant of the meatus
- Bill's bar (vertical crest) separates the superior vestibular nerve from the facial nerve at the lateral end of the IAM
- The transverse crest separates the superior and inferior compartments

**Cisternal portion of the facial nerve** is approximately 24 mm long. The motor root of the facial nerve and the nervus intermedius course anterolaterally through the CPA cistern to the porus of the IAM, accompanied by and usually anterior to the cisternal segment of the vestibulocochlear nerve.

### CPA Lesions

**Benign lesions (in order of frequency):**

| Lesion | Frequency | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Vestibular schwannoma | 75-80% of CPA tumours | Arises from Schwann cells of CN VIII; most common CPA tumour |
| Meningioma | 10-20% of CPA tumours | Durally based; WHO grades 1-3; psammoma bodies; 60% have dural tail on MRI |
| Epidermoid cyst | 5-9% | Keratinous cyst; pearly white; 'cauliflower' shape; high signal on DWI/FLAIR |
| Arachnoid cyst | ~1% | CSF-filled; same signal as CSF; 85% asymptomatic |
| Lipoma | 0.05-0.1% | High signal T1, low signal on fat suppression; rarely grows |
| Facial nerve schwannoma | Rare | Involves any segment of CN VII; 22-46% involve CPA |
| Trigeminal schwannoma | 2-3% of CPA tumours | Enlargement of Meckel's cave |
| Lower CN schwannoma | <1% | Develops within jugular foramen |

**Additional CPA lesions:**
- Cholesterol granuloma (from petrous apex)
- Glomus tumours/paragangliomas (Fisch Type D extending from jugular foramen)
- Chordomas (midline clival tumours)
- Endolymphatic sac tumours (20% associated with Von Hippel-Lindau)
- Choroid plexus papilloma (fourth ventricle origin)

**Malignant CPA lesions:**
- Metastases (~1% of CPA tumours) — from breast, prostate, lung, melanoma, thyroid
- CPA lymphoma
- Squamous cell carcinoma of the temporal bone
- Chondrosarcoma

### Surgical Approaches to the CPA

Three main surgical approaches are used for CPA lesions. All skull base pathology is best managed in a multidisciplinary setting with a core team including otolaryngology, neurosurgery and neuroradiology.

**1. Translabyrinthine Approach:**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | Through the mastoid; bony labyrinthectomy performed |
| Hearing | Sacrificed (total hearing loss) |
| Advantages | Direct access to CPA; good facial nerve visualization; lower mortality |
| Best for | Any size tumour when hearing not serviceable; most commonly used otological approach |
| Key steps | Cortical [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]] → bony labyrinthectomy → skeletonize jugular bulb and vertical facial nerve → skeletonize IAM (270°) → open posterior fossa dura |
| Facial nerve | Lies between surgeon and tumour (posterior to tumour) |
| Closure | Middle ear obliteration, Eustachian tube packing, abdominal fat graft |

- Can be extended (extended translabyrinthine) with 360° bone removal around IAM for petrous apex lesions
- Combined translabyrinthine-retrosigmoid approach can be used for tumours >3-4 cm
- Transcochlear approach offers anterior exposure for clival and petrous ICA region tumours
- Cochlear nerve preservation during translabyrinthine approach allows subsequent [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implantation]]

**2. Middle Fossa Approach:**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | Extradural, from above through temporal bone craniectomy |
| Hearing | Preservation possible |
| Best for | Small tumours (<1.5 cm intracranial diameter) confined to IAC |
| Advantages | Good visualization of lateral IAM; hearing preservation |
| Disadvantages | Limited posterior fossa access; cramped; risk of epilepsy from temporal lobe retraction |
| Key steps | Vertical incision anterior to pinna → 5x5 cm craniectomy → elevate dura off petrous surface → identify arcuate eminence and GSPN → skeletonize IAM → identify facial nerve in anterosuperior quadrant |
| Facial nerve | Lies between surgeon and tumour (more vulnerable) |

- Extended middle fossa approach (EMFA) — more bone removed anterior and posterior to IAM for larger tumours up to 3 cm
- Two methods to locate IAM: (a) House method — follow facial nerve from geniculate ganglion medially; (b) Fisch method — bisect angle between GSPN line and superior SCC plane
- The maximum tumour size for comfortable hearing preservation is about 1-1.5 cm intracranial diameter

**3. Retrosigmoid (Suboccipital) Approach:**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Access | Through posterior fossa craniotomy behind sigmoid sinus |
| Hearing | Preservation possible |
| Best for | Large tumours; preferred neurosurgical approach |
| Advantages | Wide posterior fossa access; hearing preservation |
| Disadvantages | Cerebellar retraction required; post-operative headache more common; limited lateral IAM visualization |
| Key steps | Incision behind postauricular sulcus → 5x5 cm retrosigmoid craniectomy → open dura → release CSF → tumour removal |

- Post-operative headache is more common than with translabyrinthine approach — mechanisms include dural irritation around IAM, scalp flap adherence, neck muscle dissection, occipital nerve injury
- The significantly greater cerebellar retraction required does not result in greater long-term balance disability compared to translabyrinthine approach

**Choice of approach depends on:**
- Hearing status (serviceable hearing → retrosigmoid or middle fossa)
- Tumour size (small → middle fossa; medium → retrosigmoid; any size → translabyrinthine)
- Tumour location and extension
- Involvement of neurovascular structures
- Surgeon preference and experience

## Part D: Endoscopic Trans-sphenoidal Hypophysectomy

*Q166: Endoscopic trans-sphenoidal hypophysectomy — technique, post-op care, complications (2011 Dec)*

### Introduction and History

The surgical approach to the central skull base has evolved considerably. Sir Victor Horsley performed the first pituitary tumour removal in 1889 using a craniotomy with transfrontal route. Schloffer (1907) utilized a transfacial-transsphenoidal approach. Oskar Hirsch (1909) used a transnasal transseptal route. James Hardy in the 1960s performed the first microsurgical pituitary surgery via a transsphenoidal route using the operative microscope. In 1992, Jankowski published the first description of a fully endoscopic transnasal technique for the pituitary gland.

### Transition to Endoscopic Approach

**Advantages over microscopic surgery:**
- Decreased pain and damage to nasal mucosa
- Improved panoramic view with angled views (30-degree endoscope)
- Good mobility with dynamic visualization
- No external incision or associated numbness
- Wide angle allows critical landmarks and tumour to be seen in the same field of view
- Improved ability to remove extrasellar tumour extensions

**Requirement:** Endoscopic surgery of the sellar region requires two surgeons — one holds the endoscope while the other performs bimanual surgical dissection.

### Pre-operative Assessment

**Clinical assessment:**
- Comprehensive history and full head and neck examination
- Cranial nerve examination (particularly nerves passing through cavernous sinus)
- Visual status: acuity, visual fields, gaze restrictions (ophthalmological assessment)
- Rigid nasal endoscopy — for sinonasal disease or anatomic obstructions
- Endocrinological assessment — hormonal status and pituitary function

**Imaging:**
- **CT sinuses** — bone windows for tumour invasion/bony remodelling; sinonasal disease, septal deviation, sinus aeration, sphenoid access, Onodi cells, intersinus septa, course of carotid artery and optic nerve, areas of dehiscence
- **CT angiogram** — if concern about carotid artery
- **MRI with IGN protocol** — differentiates tumour from obstructed mucus/fluid; tumour morphology; dural enhancement/invasion
- **Image guidance navigation (IGN)** — routinely used for all skull base cases; confirms anatomical knowledge; especially helpful for revision cases, challenging anatomy, expanded approaches

**Pathology addressed:**
- Pituitary adenomas (functioning and non-functioning) — vast majority
- Rathke's cleft cysts, craniopharyngiomas, fibrous dysplasia, chordomas, chondrosarcomas, epidermoid cysts, meningiomas

### Surgical Technique

**Patient positioning:**
- Two-surgeon approach — table turned so anaesthetist at foot of patient
- Patient catheterized for fluid balance management (risk of diabetes insipidus)
- Lumbar drains not routinely required
- Broad spectrum prophylactic antibiotics
- Nasal decongestion with topical and local vasoconstrictors
- Abdominal/thigh prepared as graft donor site (fat and fascia)
- Head in reverse Trendelenburg position

**Step-by-step procedure:**

**Step 1 — Optimize the nasal cavity:**
- Address anatomical obstructions (septoplasty, removal of concha bullosa)
- Raise nasoseptal (Hadad) flap if extended approach anticipated — preserve posterior nasal artery vascular pedicle on one side
- If unsure about need for flap, at minimum preserve the vascular pedicle by incising from lower edge of sphenoid ostium extending anteriorly

**Step 2 — Access to sphenoid sinus:**
- Bilateral access created to increase working space for binasal two-surgeon approach
- Lateralize middle turbinates to expose sphenoethmoidal recess and superior turbinate
- Remove inferior half of superior turbinate flush with sphenoid face
- Identify sphenoid ostium (medial to superior turbinate at junction of lower third and upper two-thirds) — if not visible, measure 12 mm from posterior choanae
- Widen sphenoidotomy — from septum to lamina papyracea laterally, roof to floor vertically
- Elevate sphenoid mucosa from medial to lateral, leaving lateral mucosa attached (creates mucosal flaps for later reconstruction)

**Step 3 — Posterior septectomy:**
- Remove approximately 1-1.5 cm of posterior septum using microdebrider, scalpel, or backbiting forceps
- Protect pedicle of septal flap

**Step 4 — Sphenoid sinus preparation:**
- Carefully remove intersinus septations using rongeur or diamond drill — septations frequently veer towards carotid artery and/or optic nerve
- Identify key landmarks: medial and lateral opticocarotid recesses, anterior genu of carotid arteries, both optic nerves
- Confirm landmarks with image guidance

**Step 5 — Sellar bone removal:**
- For macroadenomas — sellar face is soft due to tumour expansion; Freer elevator fractures bone → 2 mm Kerrison punch removes bone
- For microadenomas — bone may be hard; diamond burr used to 'eggshell' the bone
- Expose underlying dura from one cavernous sinus to the other, from pituitary fossa floor to just below tuberculum sellae

**Step 6 — Dural opening and tumour removal:**
- **U-shaped dural incision** placed a few millimetres medial to each cavernous sinus, joined at floor of sella — produces superiorly based flap (cruciate incisions no longer used)
- Macroadenomas — tumour immediately apparent; tissue sent for histology
- Microadenomas — MRI and image guidance locate tumour
- Attempt extracapsular resection to improve chances of complete removal
- 30-degree angled endoscope useful for parasellar extension — provides visualization impossible with microscopic approaches
- Extrasellar extension is a risk factor for persistent disease — aggressively pursue extensions in secreting tumours

**Step 7 — Closure and reconstruction:**
- **Without CSF leak:** Gelfoam paste and pressure for haemostasis → dura placed over paste → sphenoid mucosal flaps over dura → Surgicel and fibrin glue → no packing in sphenoid sinus
- **With CSF leak:** Identify diaphragm sellae defect → small triangle of fat into defect → additional fat in sellar fossa → layer of fascia (rectus abdominis or fascia lata) → dura and sphenoid mucosa → Surgicel and fibrin glue → Gelfoam → BIPP-soaked ribbon gauze pack (removed day 3)
- **Large CSF leaks/extended approaches:** Pedicled nasoseptal (Hadad) flap raised → fascia or dural substitute as underlay intracranial graft → pedicled septal flap placed over graft onto bone of defect → edges secured with Surgicel and fibrin glue → Gelfoam → BIPP ribbon gauze pack (3-7 days)

### Post-operative Care

- Patient catheterized — monitor for diabetes insipidus (accurate fluid balance)
- Nasal pack (if placed) — BIPP-soaked ribbon gauze removed at day 3 post-surgery (up to day 7 for large defects)
- If post-operative CSF leak occurs — bedrest, lumbar drain placed; if leak does not cease within 24 hours, return to theatre for formal closure
- Endocrinological monitoring — hormonal status assessment
- Visual assessment — check for any deterioration in acuity/fields
- Nasal care — saline irrigations after pack removal; regular endoscopic debridement

### Complications

**Delayed complications:**
- **CSF leak** — most common complication; accepted rate should be <5% with multilayer closure; managed with bedrest, lumbar drain, or revision closure
- Mucosal scarring
- Hyposmia or anosmia
- Nasal crusting
- Sinusitis

**Immediate complications:**
- **Vision damage** — from optic nerve injury; pre-operative identification of Onodi cells critical
- **Venous bleeding** — from cavernous sinus
- **Carotid artery injury** — devastating complication with up to 30% neurological sequelae and approximately 20% mortality rate; requires calmness and skill from both surgeons; crushed muscle as a patch on the bleeding vessel is the quickest, safest, and most effective method

**Prevention of complications:**
- Identify Onodi cells pre-operatively (sphenoethmoidal cell lateral and superior to sphenoid sinus with optic nerve running within it)
- Identify intersinus septa attaching to the carotid artery on pre-operative imaging
- Identify medial location of the carotid artery
- Use image guidance navigation routinely

## Part E: Infratemporal Fossa

*2022-80: Infratemporal fossa anatomy. Surgical approaches. Compartment resection concept (2022 Dec)*

### Anatomy of the Infratemporal Fossa

The infratemporal fossa is a space beneath the skull between the pharynx and the ramus of the mandible.

**Boundaries:**

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| Roof (medial) | Infratemporal surface of greater wing of sphenoid |
| Roof (lateral) | Space deep to zygomatic arch |
| Anterior | Inferior orbital fissure behind maxilla |
| Lateral | Ramus of mandible |
| Medial | Medial pterygoid muscle + interpterygoid fascia |
| Posterior | Styloid process with carotid sheath behind |
| Inferior | No anatomical floor; continuous with the neck |

**Contents:**

1. **Muscles:**
   - Lateral pterygoid (two heads: upper from infratemporal surface of greater wing; lower from lateral pterygoid plate) — opens the mouth
   - Medial pterygoid (two heads: larger from deep surface of lateral pterygoid plate; smaller from maxillary tuberosity) — closes the mouth
   - Insertion of temporalis muscle into the coronoid process

2. **Arteries — Maxillary artery** (terminal branch of ECA):
   - Three parts described in relation to the lateral pterygoid muscle
   - First part (proximal): inferior alveolar, middle meningeal, accessory meningeal, deep auricular, anterior tympanic arteries
   - Second part (on muscle): branches to pterygoid muscles, temporalis, lingual and long buccal nerves
   - Third part (distal, in pterygopalatine fossa): five branches including sphenopalatine artery (principal nasal arterial supply)

3. **Veins — Pterygoid venous plexus:**
   - Lies within and on the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid muscle
   - Drains into two short maxillary veins → retromandibular vein
   - Three important communicating veins: (a) inferior ophthalmic vein through inferior orbital fissure; (b) connecting vein from cavernous sinus via foramen ovale; (c) deep facial vein beneath zygoma to anterior facial vein
   - These connections can allow infection to spread from face to cavernous sinus (cavernous sinus thrombosis)

4. **Nerves — Mandibular nerve (V3):**
   - Passes through foramen ovale → short trunk just deep to upper head of lateral pterygoid → divides into anterior and posterior divisions
   - Before division gives off: nervus spinosus (re-enters foramen spinosum) and nerve to medial pterygoid (also supplies tensor palati and tensor tympani)
   - Anterior division (motor except long buccal nerve): nerves to temporalis, masseter, lateral pterygoid
   - Posterior division (sensory except mylohyoid nerve): auriculotemporal nerve, inferior alveolar nerve (gives off mylohyoid nerve before entering mandibular foramen), lingual nerve (joined by chorda tympani 2 cm below skull base)

5. **Otic ganglion** — lies close to mandibular nerve just below foramen ovale, between the nerve and tensor palati; relays secretomotor fibres for parotid gland via lesser superficial petrosal nerve to auriculotemporal nerve

6. **Other contents:** Deep lobe of parotid gland, chorda tympani

### Surgical Approaches to the Infratemporal Fossa

**1. Fisch Infratemporal Fossa Approaches:**

The infratemporal fossa approaches developed by Fisch are classified by the pathology and region they address:

| Type | Access | Indications |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | Transmastoid, anterior rerouting of facial nerve, removal of EAC | Glomus jugulare tumours, lateral skull base lesions |
| Type B | As Type A + removal of cochlea and petrous apex | Petrous apex lesions, extensive skull base tumours |
| Type C | Pre-auricular + infratemporal approach | Nasopharyngeal angiofibroma, tumours of pterygopalatine fossa |
| Type D | Combined approaches for extensive disease | Extensive skull base tumours with intracranial extension |

**2. Transcervical Approach:**
- Most commonly used (46% of cases in parapharyngeal space surgery)
- Transverse skin crease incision approximately 5 cm below the mandible (protects marginal mandibular branch)
- Platysma divided; submandibular gland fascia raised superiorly to protect facial nerve
- Stylomandibular ligament divided; mandible distracted anteriorly
- Access to parapharyngeal space between digastric muscle and periosteum of mandible and medial pterygoid insertion
- Dissection predominantly by blunt finger dissection close to tumour capsule

**3. Transcervical-Transparotid Approach:**
- Used in 27% of cases; suited for lesions arising from deep lobe of parotid, vascular tumours, malignant lesions
- Doubly modified Blair's incision with anterior extension
- Standard [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]] with facial nerve identification and protection

**4. Mandibulotomy:**
- Used in 9% of cases
- Allows access for infiltrating malignancy, multifocal recurrent tumours
- Better visual access to superior parapharyngeal space and skull base
- Can be paramedian or lateral ('inverted L' osteotomy to protect inferior alveolar nerve)
- May require [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] and overnight intubation

**5. Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS):**
- Paradigm shift for suitable parapharyngeal space tumours
- Three-dimensional high-resolution image with magnification, tremor filtration and motion scaling
- Ideal for: benign salivary gland tumours in the pre-styloid compartment displacing carotid posteriorly
- Contraindicated for: tumours adherent to or involving the carotid artery, dumbbell tumours with significant parotid involvement, paragangliomas, tumours with bony skull base involvement, malignant tumours

**6. Endoscopic Endonasal Approaches (EEA):**
- Transnasal transmaxillary route with removal of posterior maxillary wall — most lateral trajectory for anterolateral infratemporal fossa
- Transpterygoid access opens anterior infratemporal and peritubal compartment
- Can be extended posteriorly to access foramen lacerum and petrous apex
- Advantages: direct access and visualization, two-surgeon four-hands technique
- Limitations: limited access to lateral infratemporal and parapharyngeal space; dural reconstruction challenging

### Compartment Resection Concept

The concept of compartment resection in the infratemporal fossa involves understanding the anatomical compartments of the skull base and performing en bloc resection of the involved compartment to achieve oncological clearance. The classification of endonasal approaches to the skull base is organized based on orientation in sagittal and coronal radiological planes relative to the sphenoid sinus:

**Sagittal plane modules:**
- Transfrontal, transcribriform, transplanum, transsellar, transclival (superior/middle/inferior), transodontoid

**Coronal plane modules:**
- Anterior cranial fossa (supraorbital, transorbital)
- Middle cranial fossa (medial cavernous sinus, medial petrous apex, suprapetrous, infratemporal skull base)
- Posterior cranial fossa (transcondylar, infrapetrous, parapharyngeal space)

The sphenoid sinus is the epicentre of endonasal skull base approaches. These modular approaches can be combined to provide complete access for large tumours or excision of sinonasal malignancy involving the skull base. For sinonasal malignancy, oncological principles can be preserved with endoscopic endonasal surgery — final resection margins must be negative (en bloc resection is often not achievable even with open approaches due to proximity to critical neurovascular structures).

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Paragangliomas arise from neural crest-derived paraganglia; 30% are related to SDH gene mutations; 10% are multicentric; 3% are secretory. All patients require catecholamine screening before surgery. The Shamblin classification (I-III) grades carotid body tumours by relationship to carotid vessels, predicting surgical difficulty.
>
> 2. The 'Lyre' sign (splaying of carotid bifurcation on angiography) and the 'salt and pepper' appearance on MRI are characteristic imaging features of carotid body tumours. Histologically, cells form Zellballen clusters surrounded by vascular sinusoids.
>
> 3. Juvenile angiofibroma arises close to the sphenopalatine foramen in adolescent males (0.4/million/year). The first branchial arch artery theory explains its origin, location, blood supply and the necessity of drilling the pterygoid base to remove tumour remnants.
>
> 4. JAs are classified as vascular malformations rather than true neoplasms, based on their characteristic irregularly-shaped vessels with endothelial lining only. Biopsy must be avoided due to the risk of severe bleeding from these non-contractible pathological vessels.
>
> 5. The CPA houses CN V-XI, AICA, and the internal auditory meatus. Vestibular schwannomas account for 75-80% of CPA tumours, meningiomas 10-20%, and epidermoid cysts 5-9%. The facial nerve lies in the anterosuperior quadrant of the IAM (Bill's bar landmark).
>
> 6. Three surgical approaches to the CPA — translabyrinthine (sacrifices hearing, direct access, any tumour size), middle fossa (hearing preservation, small tumours <1.5 cm), and retrosigmoid (hearing preservation possible, wide access, cerebellar retraction needed, more post-operative headache).
>
> 7. Endoscopic trans-sphenoidal hypophysectomy requires a two-surgeon team, image guidance navigation, and a systematic approach: bilateral sphenoidotomy, posterior septectomy, intersinus septation removal, sellar bone removal, U-shaped dural incision, tumour removal, and multilayer closure.
>
> 8. CSF leak is the most common complication of trans-sphenoidal surgery (<5% rate with multilayer closure). Carotid artery injury is devastating (30% neurological sequelae, 20% mortality). The nasoseptal (Hadad) flap is critical for reconstruction of large skull base defects.
>
> 9. The infratemporal fossa is bounded by the greater wing of sphenoid (roof), maxilla (anterior), mandibular ramus (lateral), medial pterygoid/interpterygoid fascia (medial), and styloid process (posterior), with no floor. Contents include the maxillary artery (three parts), mandibular nerve (V3), pterygoid venous plexus, and pterygoid muscles.
>
> 10. The Fisch infratemporal fossa approaches (Types A-D) provide systematic access to lateral skull base lesions. Modern endoscopic endonasal approaches offer modular access classified in sagittal and coronal planes relative to the sphenoid sinus, with compartment resection preserving oncological principles.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Pre-operative embolization of paragangliomas should be performed 24-48 hours before surgery and significantly reduces intra-operative blood loss but carries a small stroke risk. Balloon occlusion testing is mandatory for Class III Shamblin tumours where ICA sacrifice may be necessary.
>
> - In JA surgery, always dissect along the pseudocapsule — the highest density of irregularly-shaped vessels is at the tumour surface, and breaching it causes profuse, uncontrollable bleeding. Drilling the pterygoid base and clivus at the end of surgery is essential to prevent recurrence at the site of origin.
>
> - When approaching a CPA tumour via the translabyrinthine route, the secret of the operation is the extent of bone removal — remove bone up to middle fossa dura, widely over sigmoid sinus, and down to jugular bulb to create maximum access through soft, compressible dural surfaces.
>
> - The cochlear aqueduct encountered during translabyrinthine surgery is a useful landmark — it is just superomedial to the jugular bulb, and the lower cranial nerves lie anteroinferiorly to it. A brisk flow of CSF through it is heartening as the intracranial contents will relax.
>
> - In endoscopic trans-sphenoidal surgery, always identify Onodi cells pre-operatively on CT — failure to identify them places the optic nerve at risk. Intersinus septa frequently veer towards the carotid artery and must be removed with extreme caution (rongeur or diamond drill preferred over powered instruments).
>
> - The pterygoid venous plexus communicates with the cavernous sinus via a connecting vein through the foramen ovale — this connection explains the dangerous potential for facial infections to cause cavernous sinus thrombosis, and is a source of significant venous bleeding during infratemporal fossa surgery.
