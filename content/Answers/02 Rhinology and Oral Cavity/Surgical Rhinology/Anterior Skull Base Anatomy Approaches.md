# Anterior Skull Base: Anatomy, Approaches, Ess Complications and Management

> [!question] Questions Covered

## INTRODUCTION

The anterior skull base forms the floor of the anterior cranial fossa and the roof over the orbits and ethmoidal air cell system. Its intricate three-dimensional anatomy and proximity to critical neurovascular structures make it a region where comprehensive anatomical knowledge is essential for safe endoscopic sinus surgery and skull base tumour resection. Over the last two decades, a paradigm shift has occurred with the introduction of endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES) techniques, allowing many anterior and central skull base lesions to be managed through the nose. The Cheesman craniofacial resection classification provides a systematic approach to open surgery, while endoscopic approaches (transcribriform, transplanum, transfrontal) offer comparable oncological outcomes with reduced morbidity for appropriately selected cases. This answer integrates anatomy, surgical approaches (open and endoscopic), complications management and skull base reconstruction principles.

## PART A: ANTERIOR SKULL BASE ANATOMY

### Bony Framework

The anterior skull base forms the floor of the anterior cranial fossa and is constituted by three bones:

- **Frontal bone (orbital part):** Forms the roof of the orbital cavity. Medially it articulates with the nasal bones and lacrimal bone. The orbital plate articulates posteriorly with the paper-thin lamina papyracea of the ethmoid, and further posteriorly with the lesser wing and body of the sphenoid. The sharp posterior edge of the lesser wing of the sphenoid marks the posterior boundary of the anterior cranial fossa.

- **Ethmoid bone:** Consists of (a) the central perpendicular plate forming the superior nasal septum, which extends superiorly as the crista galli; (b) the cribriform plates on either side, through which approximately 20 olfactory nerve filaments pass on each side; and (c) the paired labyrinths containing air cells, lying medial to the orbit and forming part of the lateral nasal wall.

- **Sphenoid bone (lesser wings and body):** The lesser wings form the posterior boundary of the anterior cranial fossa. The body contains the sphenoid sinus. Superolateral to the sphenoid sinus lie the optic nerves in their canals, and laterally lie the internal carotid arteries.

### Surgically Important Structures

The ethmoidal labyrinth is surgically important in endoscopic sinus surgery. The superior and middle conchae are part of the ethmoidal labyrinth, as is the bulla ethmoidalis anteriorly. The lamina papyracea (paper-thin orbital plate of the ethmoid) separates the ethmoid sinuses from the orbital cavity. Knowledge of this relationship is critical for endoscopic access to the medial orbit for periorbital abscess drainage, orbital decompression, or access to orbital tumours.

### The 24-12-6 Rule (Key Vascular Landmarks)

Two critical arterial structures lie along the frontoethmoidal suture on the medial orbital wall:

| Structure | Distance | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|----------|----------------------|
| Anterior ethmoidal artery (AEA) | 24 mm posterior to anterior lacrimal crest | Key landmark during ESS; at risk of injury |
| Posterior ethmoidal artery (PEA) | 12 mm behind AEA | Located near posterior ethmoid roof |
| Optic nerve (in canal) | 6 mm behind PEA | Danger zone for orbital apex surgery |

The AEA is located between the second and third ethmoid lamella, just posterior to the nasofrontal recess. The anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries are best transected on the orbital side of the skull base during endoscopic resection. It is important to leave a small stump for cauterization so the vessel does not retract into the orbit and cause a retro-orbital haematoma.

### Keros Classification of the Olfactory Fossa

The depth of the olfactory fossa (formed between the fovea ethmoidalis laterally and the cribriform plate medially) determines susceptibility to iatrogenic CSF leak during ESS:

| Keros Type | Depth of Olfactory Fossa | Risk Level |
|------------|--------------------------|------------|
| Type I | 1-3 mm (shallow) | Lowest risk |
| Type II | 4-7 mm (moderate) | Intermediate risk |
| Type III | 8-16 mm (deep) | Highest risk of CSF leak |

The fovea ethmoidalis (lateral lamella of the cribriform plate) is the thinnest part of the anterior skull base and is the commonest site of iatrogenic skull base injury during ESS. Asymmetric Keros types between the two sides are common, making pre-operative CT assessment essential. The middle turbinate attachment to the skull base at the lateral lamella is a critical landmark, and when the olfactory fossa depth is greater than 4 mm, the middle turbinate is more likely to lie in a mesentery, increasing the risk of inadvertent entry into the cranial cavity.

### Sphenopalatine Foramen and Posterior Nasal Artery

Found at the top of the perpendicular plate of the palatine bone, posterior to the maxillary hiatus, the sphenopalatine foramen transmits the corresponding neurovascular bundle. The sphenopalatine artery rarely presents as one trunk, dividing into two or more (up to 10) branches just after exiting the foramen. The posterior septal artery (a branch of the sphenopalatine artery) supplies the nasoseptal flap used in skull base reconstruction.

## PART B: APPROACHES TO THE ANTERIOR SKULL BASE

### Open (Craniofacial) Approaches -- Cheesman Classification

The involvement of the cribriform plate was recognized as a major reason for failure to control ethmoid neoplasms with conventional approaches. Ketcham, Clifford and Cheesman described combined intracranial and transfacial approaches for tumours involving the cribriform plates, dura and frontal lobes. Cheesman and Reddy sub-classified craniofacial procedures into three types:

| Type | Approach | Description |
|------|----------|-------------|
| Type 1 | Extended medial maxillectomy (lateral rhinotomy) | Wide exploration via operating microscope with frozen section; allows resection and repair of ethmoid roof and orbital periosteum |
| Type 2 | Window craniotomy | Lateral rhinotomy + bicoronal incision for pericranial flap; small midline window craniotomy via [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] allows access to anterior cranial fossa floor for en bloc resection |
| Type 3 | Full craniofacial resection | Performed with neurosurgeon; transfacial approach combined with frontolateral craniotomy for extensive tumours |

For Type 2 resection, the dura is elevated from the ethmoid roof and cribriform plate, and a cranial osteotomy combined with lateral rhinotomy osteotomies allows en bloc resection of both ethmoid complexes. Involved dura and olfactory bulbs can be excised and repaired with fascia lata and pericranium. The window bone flap is replaced and fixed with miniplates.

For Type 3 resection, skull base surgery demands an interdisciplinary approach. There is a trend for the otorhinolaryngologist to use endoscopic techniques to guide the neurosurgeon's osteotomies from below. Many tumours suitable for Type 1 craniofacial resections can now be approached endoscopically, and extensive tumours requiring a Type 3 approach might often be deemed incurable.

### Endoscopic Endonasal Approaches

EES is team surgery requiring coordination of rhinology and neurosurgery. The primary principle is utilization of the endonasal corridor to provide optimal access with least morbidity.

**Classification of Endonasal Approaches (Snyderman):**

Sagittal plane modules extend from the frontal sinus to the upper cervical spine; coronal plane modules extend laterally corresponding to each cranial fossa:

- **Transfrontal:** Access anterior to cribriform plates. Draf 3 frontal sinusotomy is the foundation. Indications: chronic sinusitis, mucocele, osteoma, nasal dermoid (tract through foramen cecum).

- **Transcribriform:** Access for tumours arising from the cribriform plate (olfactory neuroblastoma, olfactory groove meningioma, meningoencephalocele). Usually combined with transfrontal and transplanum approaches. Key landmarks include the crista galli, olfactory filia/bulbs, and the lateral lamella.

- **Transplanum (suprasellar):** Access from cribriform plates anteriorly to the tuberculum posteriorly. Indications: meningiomas, craniopharyngiomas, pituitary macroadenomas. The planum forms the roof of the sphenoid sinus bounded by the optic canals posterolaterally.

- **Supraorbital:** Coronal plane approach to orbital roof. Maximal limit is mid-sagittal plane of orbit. Used for osteomas, meningiomas, and dural margins for sinonasal malignancy.

- **Endoscopic Modified Lothrop Procedure (EMLP):** Required when the intracranial component lies anterior to the anterior ethmoidal arteries. Also improves lateral access within the frontal sinus. Through a superior septectomy, surgeons can use the contralateral nostril to reach lesions beyond the mid-pupillary line.

**Contraindications to Endoscopic Approach:**
- Nasal bone involvement
- Frontal sinus involvement (beyond Draf 3 access)
- Dura involvement lateral to the ethmoidal roof (beyond mid-orbit)
- Significant involvement of orbital contents requiring exenteration
- Tumour encasement of cerebral vasculature (relative)

## PART C: APPROACHES TO THE MIDDLE SKULL BASE

### Middle Cranial Fossa Approach

Provides access to the petrous apex, internal auditory canal, and superior semicircular canal from above. The approach involves a temporal craniotomy with extradural elevation of the temporal lobe, using the middle fossa floor as a corridor.

### Endoscopic Transpterygoid Approach

Provides access through the pterygopalatine fossa to the infratemporal fossa and middle cranial fossa. This is a coronal plane approach through the medial and lateral pterygoid plates.

Steps: wide maxillary antrostomy, identification and mobilization of sphenopalatine artery and branches, vidian nerve identification (leads to petrous ICA), removal of pterygoid base and plates to expose the infratemporal fossa and middle cranial fossa floor.

### Infratemporal Fossa Approaches (Fisch Classification)

The infratemporal fossa lies beneath the skull between the pharynx and ramus of the mandible. Its contents include the lateral and medial pterygoid muscles, maxillary artery and branches, mandibular nerve and otic ganglion, pterygoid venous plexus, and the deep lobe of the parotid.

Fisch described several approaches:
- **Type A:** For [[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]]s involving the temporal bone; involves anterior rerouting of facial nerve
- **Type B:** For tumours of the petrous apex and clivus
- **Type C:** For tumours of the pterygopalatine fossa, infratemporal fossa, and nasopharynx; involves removal of pterygoid plates and access through the maxillary sinus
- **Type D:** For lesions extending to the central skull base with intradural extension

### Transmandibulotomy Approach

Used for large tumours of the [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] and infratemporal fossa. Involves mandibular osteotomy (usually in the parasymphyseal region) to gain wide lateral access to the parapharyngeal and infratemporal regions.

## PART D: ESS COMPLICATIONS AND MANAGEMENT

### Overview of Complications

Endoscopic approaches to sinonasal tumours share the complications of endoscopic sinus surgery, with additional risks related to skull base work.

**Minor Complications:**
- Synechiae formation between middle turbinate and lateral wall
- Orbital emphysema (from lamina papyracea breach with nose blowing)
- Intra-operative bleeding requiring packing
- Hyposmia/anosmia, nasal crusting, sinusitis

**Major Complications:**
- CSF leak (most common major complication)
- Orbital hematoma / retrobulbar haemorrhage
- Blindness (from optic nerve injury or orbital hematoma)
- Internal carotid artery injury
- Intracranial haemorrhage / tension pneumocephalus

### CSF Leak During ESS: Recognition and Management

**Recognition:**
- Clear watery fluid from the operative field
- Confirmation: beta-2 transferrin (specific for CSF)
- Intra-operative: may see brain pulsation through defect

**Management -- Multilayer Closure:**

For **small defects (<1 cm):** "Bath Plug" repair as described by Wormald. A plug of fascia or collagen matrix is used as an inlay graft, with the wider portion intracranially and a tail brought through the defect to create a watertight seal.

For **larger defects (1-3 cm):** Multilayer closure using:
1. Underlay of autogenic material (fascia lata from the thigh, or dural substitutes such as Duragen)
2. Overlay with a vascularized flap (nasoseptal flap preferred, or inferior/middle turbinate flaps)

For **very large defects (>3 cm):** Addition of a rigid buttress (septal cartilage or titanium mesh) prior to flap coverage to minimize the risk of encephalocoele formation.

Post-operative CSF leak protocol: bed rest, lumbar drain placement. If leak does not cease within 24 hours, return to theatre for formal closure.

### Orbital Hematoma: Recognition and Emergency Management

**Recognition (all must be assessed urgently):**
- Progressive proptosis and tense globe
- Subconjunctival haemorrhage
- Fixed, dilated pupil
- Decreased visual acuity / loss of light perception
- Elevated intra-ocular pressure

**Emergency Management (must be performed within 90 minutes to prevent permanent visual loss):**

1. **Immediate:** Stop surgery, release any nasal packing, administer IV dexamethasone, IV mannitol and IV acetazolamide to lower intra-ocular pressure
2. **Lateral canthotomy:** Incise the lateral canthus with scissors from the commissure to the lateral orbital rim
3. **Inferior cantholysis:** Cut the inferior limb of the lateral canthal tendon to decompress the orbit
4. **If canthotomy/cantholysis fails:** Formal orbital decompression through lamina papyracea (removal of medial orbital wall) to allow haematoma drainage into the ethmoid cavity
5. **Ophthalmology consultation:** Urgent for IOP monitoring and ongoing visual assessment

### Internal Carotid Artery Injury

The most serious and anxiety-provoking major vascular complication. Valentine and Wormald developed the crushed muscle patch technique (Valentine-Wormald technique) through animal wet-lab models. Application of crushed muscle to the site of a major arterial bleed can primarily control the bleed while maintaining vascular patency, leading to reduced blood loss and improved survival. Surgeons should undergo training in this technique before undertaking complex skull base cases.

## PART E: SKULL BASE RECONSTRUCTION

### Principles

Following tumour resection, any breach of the dura should be repaired to avoid meningoencephalocoele formation and ascending meningitis. Even if the dura remains intact, large defects should be repaired. The reconstruction follows a multilayer concept.

### Multilayer Reconstruction Protocol

| Layer | Material / Technique |
|-------|---------------------|
| Inlay (underlay) | Fascia lata, tensor fascia lata, or dural substitutes (Duragen) |
| Rigid buttress (>3 cm defects) | Septal cartilage or titanium mesh |
| Overlay (vascularized flap) | Nasoseptal flap (primary), or middle/inferior turbinate flap |
| Support | Surgicel, tissue glue, Gelfoam; Foley catheter or Merocel tampons |

### Nasoseptal Flap (Hadad-Bassagasteguy Flap)

This is the workhorse for endoscopic skull base reconstruction, described by Hadad and Bassagasteguy. It is a vascularized mucoperiosteal flap pedicled on the posterior septal artery, a branch of the sphenopalatine artery.

**Critical points for harvesting:**
- Must be raised BEFORE septectomy to preserve the vascular pedicle
- If a CSF leak is anticipated, the flap is raised early and stored in the nasopharynx or maxillary sinus
- Even if a leak is not anticipated, the pedicle must be preserved in case a flap is required later
- Superior incision runs parallel to the skull base, approximately 1 cm below it
- Preserving at least 1.5 cm of posterosuperior septal mucosa in the olfactory cleft region preserves smell and taste
- The anterior incision should include the most anterior-superior septal mucosa (anterior to middle turbinate attachment) for maximal flap length

**Rescue flap technique:** When a prior nasoseptal flap has been used or the septum is involved by tumour, alternatives include the inferior or middle turbinate flaps (also based on branches of the sphenopalatine artery) or an extracranial pericranial flap harvested through a bicoronal scalp incision.

### Alternative Flaps

- **Pericranial flap:** Harvested via bicoronal incision, pedicled on supraorbital vessels, passed through a bone window at the nasion into the nasal cavity. Provides maximal vascularized tissue for large anterior cranial base defects.
- **Inferior turbinate flap:** Based on inferior turbinate artery (branch of sphenopalatine artery)
- **Middle turbinate flap:** Based on middle turbinate artery
- **Free tissue grafts:** Fascia lata, dural substitutes for non-vascularized inlay layer

## PART F: MRND TYPE III (BRIEF)

Modified radical [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] (MRND) involves removal of all lymph node levels (I-V) while preserving one or more of the three non-lymphatic structures normally sacrificed in a radical neck dissection:

- Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI)
- Internal jugular vein (IJV)
- Sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM)

**MRND Type III** preserves all three non-lymphatic structures (SCM, IJV, and CN XI). This is essentially equivalent to a selective neck dissection of levels I-V, also termed a comprehensive selective neck dissection. It provides comprehensive lymph node clearance while preserving shoulder function, venous drainage, and neck contour. This procedure is typically indicated in N0 or limited N1 neck disease associated with head and neck malignancies where elective neck treatment is warranted.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The anterior skull base (floor of anterior cranial fossa) is formed by the orbital part of the frontal bone, the cribriform plate of the ethmoid, and the lesser wings/body of the sphenoid.
>
> 2. The 24-12-6 rule: the AEA lies 24 mm posterior to the anterior lacrimal crest, the PEA is 12 mm behind the AEA, and the optic nerve is 6 mm behind the PEA along the frontoethmoidal suture.
>
> 3. The Keros classification grades olfactory fossa depth into three types (I: 1-3 mm, II: 4-7 mm, III: 8-16 mm); Type III carries the highest risk of CSF leak during ESS due to the long, thin lateral lamella.
>
> 4. Cheesman craniofacial resection classification: Type 1 (extended medial maxillectomy via lateral rhinotomy), Type 2 (window craniotomy via bicoronal + lateral rhinotomy), Type 3 (full craniofacial resection with neurosurgeon and frontolateral craniotomy).
>
> 5. Endoscopic endonasal approaches include transcribriform, transplanum, transfrontal, and modified Lothrop. Contraindications include nasal bone involvement, frontal sinus involvement, and dura lateral to the ethmoid roof.
>
> 6. The nasoseptal flap (Hadad-Bassagasteguy) is the workhorse for endoscopic skull base reconstruction, pedicled on the posterior septal artery. It must be raised before septectomy to preserve the vascular pedicle.
>
> 7. The Bath Plug repair (Wormald) is used for small dural defects less than 1 cm. Multilayer closure with a rigid buttress of septal cartilage or titanium mesh is required for defects greater than 3 cm.
>
> 8. Orbital hematoma after ESS requires urgent lateral canthotomy and inferior cantholysis within 90 minutes to prevent permanent visual loss. IV dexamethasone, mannitol, and acetazolamide are adjunctive measures.
>
> 9. ICA injury during ESS: the Valentine-Wormald technique uses a crushed muscle patch applied to the bleeding vessel to control haemorrhage while maintaining vascular patency.
>
> 10. Endoscopic approaches are increasingly replacing open craniofacial resection for selected anterior skull base malignancies. EES is team surgery requiring coordination between rhinology and neurosurgery, with adherence to oncological principles.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The fovea ethmoidalis (lateral lamella of the cribriform plate) is the commonest site of iatrogenic skull base injury during ESS. Asymmetric Keros types between the two sides are common, making bilateral CT assessment mandatory before surgery.
>
> - Preservation of at least 1.5 cm of posterosuperior septal mucosa in the olfactory cleft region preserves smell and taste when raising the nasoseptal flap.
>
> - Frozen sections are reliable for confirming complete resection margins for most sinonasal tumours, with the exception of mucosal melanoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma, where they are less accurate.
>
> - Post-operative CT or MRI within 3-6 months provides a surveillance baseline that can be referenced should the patient develop features suggestive of recurrence.
>
> - The endoscopic approach can be complementary to open approaches. In combined approaches, the endoscope guides the neurosurgeon's osteotomies from below, while the craniotomy provides intracranial access from above.
>
> - When transecting the anterior ethmoidal artery endoscopically, always leave a small stump for bipolar cauterization to prevent vessel retraction into the orbit and consequent retro-orbital haematoma.
