# Frontal Recess and Surgical Approaches To the Frontal Sinus

**Question:** *A) Describe the anatomy of the frontal recess. B) Discuss the different surgical approaches to the frontal sinus.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 99 (The Frontal Sinus) & Chapter 98 (Surgical Management of Rhinosinusitis)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The frontal sinus is arguably the most complex of the paranasal sinuses. It demonstrates great variability in size and septation and is rarely symmetrical. It is absent or rudimentary in up to 5% of the population. Only in the frontal sinus is mucus actively transported inwardly along the intersinus septum, laterally along the roof and back medially along the floor, with less than 60% exiting the sinus in any given circuit (significant mucus recirculation). A normal healthy frontal sinus is dependent on the health of the anterior ethmoids and the frontal recess.

---

## PART A: ANATOMY OF THE FRONTAL RECESS

### Definition

The frontal sinus ostium drains into an hourglass-shaped space termed the frontal recess. Previously incorrectly termed the "frontonasal duct," it is not a tubular structure but a three-dimensional space which communicates with the ethmoidal infundibulum within the middle meatus.

### Boundaries

| Boundary | Structure |
|----------|-----------|
| Anterior | Agger nasi cell, frontal process of maxilla (frontal beak) |
| Medial | Superior attachment of middle turbinate, lateral lamella of cribriform plate |
| Lateral | Lamina papyracea |
| Posterior | Upward continuation of anterior face of bulla (bulla lamella) |
| Superior | Frontal sinus ostium |

- The thickness of the frontal beak depends on agger nasi pneumatization and frontoethmoidal cells
- Greater number of cells and pneumatization generally produces a smaller beak and wider frontal ostium
- If the bulla lamella is absent superiorly, a suprabulla space communicates directly with the frontal recess and the anterior ethmoidal artery is at risk during dissection

### Agger Nasi Cell

- Present in over **90%** of patients
- The most important landmark for approaching the frontal recess
- Crescent-shaped uncinate process attaches inferiorly to the inferior turbinate; superiorly it can attach to:
  - Lamina papyracea (33%)
  - Skull base (10%)
  - Middle turbinate and combinations (57%)
- The medial, posterior and superior walls of the agger nasi cell are often attached to the superior aspect of the uncinate process

### Accessory Cells in the Frontal Recess (IFAC Classification)

The International Frontal Sinus Anatomy Classification (IFAC) describes accessory cells based on their location relative to the agger nasi and ethmoid bulla:

**Anterior cells:**

| Cell Type | Description | Significance |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Agger nasi | Cell anterior to/above origin of MT | Pushes drainage medially/posteriorly |
| SAC (Type 1/2) | Supra agger cell above agger nasi | Pushes drainage posteriorly |
| SAFC (Type 3) | Extends into frontal sinus | May require extended procedure |
| Large SAFC (Type 4) | Extends >50% height of frontal sinus | Requires MEL/Draf III approach |

**Posterior cells:**

| Cell Type | Description | Significance |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Supra bulla cell (SBC) | Above bulla, not into frontal sinus | May contain anterior ethmoid artery |
| Supra bulla frontal cell (SBFC) | Crawls along skull base into sinus | Pushes drainage anteriorly |
| Supraorbital ethmoid cell (SOEC) | Pneumatizes orbital plate of frontal bone | AEA likely in mesentery; ~65% Caucasians |

**Medial cells:**
- **Intersinus septal cell:** Pneumatizes the interfrontal sinus septum; pushes drainage laterally

### Key Surgical Principles of Frontal Recess Anatomy

- Building a three-dimensional image from coronal, axial and **parasagittal** CT planes is crucial
- Frontal ethmoidal cells push the drainage pathway posteriorly and/or medially
- Frontal bulla cells push the drainage pathway anteriorly
- SOEC opens more posteriorly and laterally and can be mistaken for the frontal sinus
- Following uncinectomy viewed from below, the superior cut end of uncinate resembles "a vertical bar" — access to the frontal sinus is medial to this bar

## PART B: SURGICAL APPROACHES TO THE FRONTAL SINUS

### General Principles

- Prior to considering surgery, review clinical history, imaging and indications
- Underlying conditions (diabetes, immune disorders, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease) must be identified
- Contemporary management targets the drainage pathway (frontal recess) rather than the sinus itself
- **Mucosal preservation** is critical as mucosa regenerating over bare bone lacks effective cilia
- The surgical approach should be graduated — least invasive procedure to achieve the desired outcome

### Draf Classification (Graduated Approach)

| Approach | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| No exploration | No frontal recess disease; do not instrument a healthy recess |
| Balloon sinuplasty | Minimally invasive; balloon dilates ostium; limited evidence vs standard ESS |
| **Draf type I** | Complete ethmoidectomy + removal of cells below the frontal ostium (frontal sinusotomy) |
| **Draf type IIa** | Enlarge frontal ostium from lamina papyracea (LP) to middle turbinate (MT) |
| **Draf type IIb** | Enlarge frontal ostium from LP to nasal septum; remove anterior MT, drill floor to midline |
| **Draf type III (MEL)** | Modified endoscopic Lothrop; resect upper nasal septum, frontal intersinus septum and floor of both frontal sinuses to create a single common drainage pathway |
| Osteoplastic flap (OPF) | External approach; anterior table elevated as bone flap; with or without obliteration |
| Above and below | Combined OPF + MELP endoscopic approach |
| Riedel's procedure | Removal of anterior table of frontal sinus |
| Cranialization | Removal of sinus mucosa and posterior table |

### Draf Type I (Ethmoidectomy and Frontal Sinusotomy)

- **Commonest primary procedure** for chronic frontal sinusitis
- Aim: clear obstructing disease inferior to the level of the frontal ostium
- Key step: complete ethmoidectomy including removal of anterosuperior ethmoid cells, agger nasi and uncinate process
- Best limited to cases with only minor frontal sinus pathology
- May not be sufficient in significant [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|nasal polyposis]] with aspirin intolerance and asthma

### Draf Type II (Extended Frontal Sinusotomy)

- **Type IIa:** Widening of frontal ostium from LP to MT using Kerrison or Hajek-Kofler punch
- **Type IIb:** Extension beyond MT to nasal septum; involves removing the anterior end of the MT and drilling the floor of the sinus to the midline
- Indications for IIb: unilateral frontal ostium osteoneogenesis, [[Schneiderian Papilloma|inverted papilloma]], osteomas, medially placed frontal mucoceles

### Draf Type III (Modified Endoscopic Lothrop Procedure - MELP)

Originally described by Lothrop (1914) as an external procedure; Draf described an intranasal microscopic approach. Creates a large **common drainage pathway** for both frontal sinuses.

**Essential steps:**
1. Complete sphenoethmoidectomy
2. Clear frontal recess and identify frontal ostium (frontal mini trephines may be required)
3. Create septal window (≥2 × 2 cm); posterior limit at anterior end of middle turbinate
4. Trim anterior end of middle turbinate to skull base
5. Dissect mucosa posteriorly to identify first olfactory neuron
6. Drilling commences anterior, superior and lateral from the frontal ostium
7. Extend drilling contralaterally via septal window
8. Reduce frontal intersinus septum and frontal beak
9. Preserve mucosa over posterior frontal ostium
10. Lower bone over anterior skull base projection to the primary olfactory neuron

**The "Frontal T":** Key landmark. The vertical limb corresponds to the posterior margin of the septectomy (perpendicular plate of ethmoid); the horizontal limbs to the posterior margin of the frontal sinus floor resection, joining the two amputated ends of the middle turbinates.

**Indications for MELP:**
- Refractory chronic frontal sinusitis after previous ESS
- CRSwNP with asthma and aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease
- Large frontal mucoceles (even beyond mid-pupillary line)
- Salvage after failed osteoplastic flap ("unobliteration")
- Frontal osteoma (Grade I and II)
- Inverted papilloma extending into frontal sinus
- [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]s from frontal sinus trauma
- Cranionasal resection for anterior cranial fossa tumours
- Alternative to OPF with obliteration (able to survey for recurrent disease)

**Outcomes:**
- Overall patency rate: **95.9%**
- Overall failure rate: 13.9% (80% of failures undergo revision MELP, remainder OPF obliteration)
- Early patency 90% at 12 months, falling to 68% after 72 months

### Osteoplastic Flap Procedure (OPF)

Offers unparalleled access to the frontal sinus. Developed to manage chronic frontal sinus disease refractory to external frontoethmoidectomy.

**Technique:**
1. Coronal, brow or mid-forehead incision to expose anterior table
2. Template from Caldwell X-ray, CT or navigation to mark frontal sinus margins
3. Periosteal incision 1.5 cm outside the sinus margin
4. Oscillating saw angled at 30° towards sinus; osteotomy carried to supraorbital ridge bilaterally
5. Bone lid elevated (ideally hinges on periosteal flap)
6. **Without obliteration:** pathology addressed; bone lid replaced and periosteum sutured
7. **With obliteration:** all mucous membrane completely removed; diamond burr on inner cortex; frontal ostium plugged with fascia; sinus obliterated with abdominal fat

**OPF success rates:** Up to **93% at 8 years**, but long-term failure rate up to 25%

**Complications:** CSF leaks, persistent forehead numbness, severe headaches, frontal bossing, supraorbital neuralgia, mucocele formation, donor site complications, difficulty with post-operative surveillance

### Other Approaches

**Frontal sinus trephination:**
- Mini trephine through small infrabrow incision
- Useful as adjunct to other procedures for identification of frontal ostium
- Fluorescein-stained saline irrigated through trephine aids dissection

**External frontoethmoidectomy (Lynch-Howarth):**
- Historical procedure with high failure rate; now replaced by endoscopic approaches
- Conceptually flawed as it destroyed bony support of frontal recess causing fibrosis
- Lynch incision still used to access anterior ethmoidal artery and drain periorbital abscesses

**Riedel's procedure:**
- Removes floor, anterior wall and entire mucosal lining of frontal sinus
- Rarely performed; reserved for failed obliteration, osteomyelitis or tumours of anterior wall
- Main criticism: cosmetic defect (reduced by chamfering supraorbital ridge)

**Cranialization:**
- Combined with neurosurgeons via coronal incision and midfrontobasal craniotomy
- Posterior wall and floor removed; mucosa inverted into nasal cavity
- Relies on complete mucosa removal and diamond burr drilling
- Indications: extensive posterior table fractures, tumours, osteomyelitis

### Factors Influencing Surgical Approach

| Factor | Simple Approach | Extended Approach |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|
| Patient factors | Compliance issues, comorbidities | Asthma, aspirin sensitivity |
| Anatomy | Preserved landmarks, no complex cells | Osteoneogenesis, thick beak, complex cells |
| Pathology | CRS ± polyps, no prior surgery | Recurrent disease, AERD, tumours |

### Reasons for Failure of Frontal Sinus Surgery

1. **Ongoing mucosal disease:** Eosinophilic CRS with polyps fares less well
2. **Incomplete dissection:** Retained uncinate process and residual cells in the frontal recess
3. **Middle turbinate lateralization:** Incidence 36–78% in revision surgery; frontal sinus rescue procedure described
4. **Scarring and synechia:** Meticulous attention to mucosal preservation critical
5. **Osteoneogenesis:** Higher in operated sinuses; persistent osteitis promotes mucosal inflammation

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Frontal recess is an hourglass-shaped 3D space (not a duct); bounded by agger nasi anteriorly, bulla posteriorly, lamina papyracea laterally, middle turbinate medially
> 2. Agger nasi cell present in >90% of patients; uncinate attaches to LP (33%), skull base (10%), or MT/combinations (57%)
> 3. IFAC classification: anterior cells (SAC, SAFC) push drainage posteriorly; posterior cells (SBC, SBFC) push drainage anteriorly; medial cells push laterally
> 4. Type 4 cells (large SAFC) extend >50% of frontal sinus height and usually require a Draf III/MELP approach
> 5. Draf classification: graduated approach from type I (ethmoidectomy + sinusotomy) through type III (MEL/drill-out)
> 6. MELP: essential to identify the "frontal T" (first olfactory neuron landmark); overall patency rate 95.9%
> 7. Osteoplastic flap: success rate up to 93% at 8 years; long-term failure rate up to 25%; dwindling indications in endoscopic era
> 8. Mucosal preservation is critical — mucosa regenerating over bare bone lacks effective cilia
> 9. Frontal recess should NOT be explored in the absence of positive CT findings and clinical symptoms
> 10. Parasagittal CT views are essential for identifying frontal recess cells and drainage pathways


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Unnecessary instrumentation in a healthy frontal recess causes mucosal injury and adhesion formation — leave it alone if there is no disease
> - Supraorbital ethmoid cells open more posteriorly and laterally than the frontal sinus — easily mistaken for the true sinus
> - In Draf III, if access is blocked by osteoma/osteoneogenesis, a midline transeptal "inside to outside" approach can be used
> - In the current endoscopic era, less than 2% of frontal sinus cases require OPF with obliteration in tertiary centres
> - Circumferential mucosal injury in the frontal recess will undoubtedly result in stenosis and recurrent sinusitis
> - CT images should always be available in coronal, axial AND parasagittal planes for frontal recess surgery
