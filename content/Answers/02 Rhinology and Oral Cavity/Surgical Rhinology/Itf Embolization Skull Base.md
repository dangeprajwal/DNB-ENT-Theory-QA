# Infratemporal Fossa Anatomy, Surgical Approaches & Endovascular Embolization in Skull Base Tumours

**Question:** *Q50: Describe the anatomy of the infratemporal fossa. What are the surgical approaches and their limitations? Q60: Discuss the role of embolization in skull base tumours. Q75: Enumerate the indications of endovascular embolization in head and neck surgery. Describe the embolic materials used and their advantages and disadvantages.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 98 (Anatomy of the Skull Base and Infratemporal Fossa) and Chapter 100 (Vascular Assessment and Management)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The infratemporal fossa (ITF) is a deep, irregularly shaped space beneath the skull base that lies between the pharynx medially and the mandibular ramus laterally. It houses critical neurovascular structures including the maxillary artery, mandibular nerve (V3), and the pterygoid venous plexus. Many skull base tumours -- paragangliomas, [[Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma|juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma]]s (JNA), meningiomas, chordomas and sarcomas -- arise within or extend into this region. The intimate relationship of these tumours with the internal carotid artery (ICA), cranial nerves, and the intracranial circulation mandates thorough pre-operative vascular assessment. Pre-operative embolization has become an indispensable adjunct to skull base surgery, aiming to devascularize hypervascular tumours selectively to reduce intra-operative blood loss, improve operative field visualization, shorten operative times, and improve rates of radical tumour removal. Understanding ITF anatomy, the available surgical corridors, and the principles and materials of endovascular embolization is essential for safe and effective management of these complex lesions.

## PART A: INFRATEMPORAL FOSSA -- BOUNDARIES AND CONTENTS

### Boundaries

The infratemporal fossa is a space beneath the skull between the pharynx and the ramus of the mandible. Its boundaries as described by Huins are as follows.

The **roof** is formed by the infratemporal surface of the greater wing of the sphenoid bone as it articulates with the squamous temporal bone, just in front of the articular eminence. Anteriorly, this infratemporal surface ends at the **inferior orbital fissure** behind the posterior surface of the maxilla, transmitting the maxillary nerve. The **lateral** extent is limited superiorly by the space deep to the zygomatic arch, with the ramus of the mandible forming the lateral boundary. **Medially**, the medial pterygoid muscle, together with the interpterygoid fascia, marks the medial border. The **posterior** boundary is formed by the styloid process of the temporal bone, with the carotid sheath lying behind it. Critically, the ITF has **no anatomical floor** -- it is continuous inferiorly down into the neck.

| Boundary | Structures |
|----------|-----------|
| Roof | Greater wing sphenoid + squamous temporal |
| Anterior | Posterior surface maxilla + inferior orbital fissure |
| Lateral | Zygomatic arch + mandibular ramus |
| Medial | Medial pterygoid muscle + interpterygoid fascia |
| Posterior | Styloid process + carotid sheath |
| Floor | NONE -- continuous with neck |

### Contents of the Infratemporal Fossa

The ITF contains the deep lobe of the parotid gland, the lateral and medial pterygoid muscles, the insertion of the temporalis muscle into the coronoid process, the maxillary artery and its branches, the mandibular nerve (V3) and otic ganglion, chorda tympani, the pterygoid venous plexus, and the maxillary veins.

### Muscles of the Infratemporal Fossa

The **lateral pterygoid muscle** arises from two heads: the upper head from the infratemporal surface of the greater wing of the sphenoid, and the lower head from the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid plate. Passing backwards, these two heads converge to form a tendon that inserts into the front of the neck of the mandible. Supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, it acts to open the mouth by pulling the condyle forwards and down the articular eminence.

The **medial pterygoid muscle** also arises from two heads. The larger deep head arises from the medial (deep) surface of the lateral pterygoid plate, while a small superficial slip joins from the tuberosity and pyramidal process of the maxilla. Angled laterally, posteriorly, and inferiorly, it inserts into the angle of the mandible, acting to close the mouth and move the mandible towards the opposite side in chewing. It is innervated by the mandibular nerve.

### Maxillary Artery -- Three Parts

The maxillary artery is a terminal branch of the external carotid artery (ECA) that enters the ITF as the other terminal branch, the superficial temporal artery, passes laterally. It runs a variable course around the lateral pterygoid muscle medially towards the pterygomaxillary fissure and into the pterygopalatine fossa (PPF). The artery is described in three parts relative to the lateral pterygoid muscle: proximal, on, and distal.

**First part** (proximal to lateral pterygoid) -- gives off five branches:
- Inferior alveolar artery
- Middle meningeal artery (MMA) -- passes up through foramen spinosum
- Accessory meningeal artery -- passes through foramen ovale
- Deep auricular artery -- supplies external auditory canal
- Anterior tympanic artery -- enters petrotympanic fissure to the middle ear

**Second part** (on lateral pterygoid) -- gives off muscular branches to:
- Lateral and medial pterygoid muscles
- Temporalis muscle
- Lingual and long buccal nerves

**Third part** (distal, in pterygopalatine fossa) -- divides into five branches accompanying nerves including branches of the pterygopalatine ganglion, supplying the orbit, nose, and palate. The sphenopalatine artery enters the nasal cavity through the sphenopalatine foramen to form the principal arterial supply.

### Pterygoid Venous Plexus

The pterygoid plexus of veins lies within and on the lateral surface of the lateral pterygoid muscle, receiving tributaries corresponding to the branches of the maxillary artery. It drains into two short, large maxillary veins that pass horizontally backwards deep to the neck of the mandible to join the superficial temporal vein and form the retromandibular vein. Three important communicating veins exist:

1. Inferior ophthalmic vein passes through the inferior orbital fissure to join the facial vein
2. A connecting vein passes vertically from the cavernous sinus via foramen ovale (or foramen of Vesalius)
3. Deep facial vein runs forward beneath the zygoma to join the anterior facial vein

These connections can allow infection to spread from the face via the pterygoid plexus to produce a cavernous sinus thrombosis.

### Mandibular Nerve (V3) and Otic Ganglion

The mandibular nerve passes through the foramen ovale and, after a short course just deep to the upper head of the lateral pterygoid, divides into anterior (motor) and posterior (sensory) divisions. The **anterior division** is motor except for the long buccal nerve, and supplies the temporalis, masseter, and lateral pterygoid. The **posterior division** is sensory except for the mylohyoid nerve, and includes the auriculotemporal, inferior alveolar, and lingual nerves.

The **otic ganglion** lies close to the mandibular nerve just below the foramen ovale, between the nerve and the tensor palati muscle. It relays secretomotor fibres for the parotid gland via the lesser superficial petrosal nerve, transmitting them to the auriculotemporal nerve.

The **chorda tympani** joins the lingual nerve 2 cm below the base of the skull.

## PART B: SURGICAL APPROACHES TO THE INFRATEMPORAL FOSSA

### Overview of Approaches

The ITF is one of the most surgically challenging regions due to its deep location, dense neurovascular contents, proximity to the ICA, and the concentration of cranial nerves. Several approaches have been developed, each offering different advantages depending on the pathology and its extent.

### Fisch Infratemporal Fossa Classification (Types A-D)

The Fisch classification was originally developed for paraganglioma surgery but has become the standard classification for lateral skull base approaches.

| Fisch Type | Access Region |
|------------|---------------|
| Type A | Middle ear, mastoid, jugular bulb, jugular foramen |
| Type B | Petrous apex, clivus, ICA in petrous bone |
| Type C | Anterior ITF, parasellar region, nasopharynx |
| Type D | Lateral ITF with intracranial extension |

The Fisch Type B approach affords the best exposure to the petrous apex. The facial nerve is skeletonized in the mastoid segment and traced into the parotid gland; the cochlea is removed and the ICA exposed, with the petrous apex lying medial to the ICA.

### Transmandibulotomy-Transcervical Approach

This approach is indicated for nasopharyngeal tumours extending into the anterior ITF. An extended submandibular incision is made from the mastoid tip to the lip. After dividing platysma and reflecting the submandibular gland superiorly, the great vessels and cranial nerves are identified. A median mandibular osteotomy with pre-fitted mini-plates is performed. The mandible is retracted laterally, and dissection proceeds across the floor of the mouth into the [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]]. Ligation of the ECA distal to the lingual artery provides access to the anterolateral ITF. Additional division of styloid musculature opens the retropharyngeal space and provides access to the Eustachian tube and central skull base.

### Maxillary Swing (Anterolateral Approach)

A Weber-Ferguson-Longmire incision is made, extending laterally onto the zygoma. The osteotomy separates the zygoma from the maxilla, divides along the infraorbital rim, releases the medial maxillary wall, and sections the pterygoid plates. The maxilla is swung laterally on its periosteal pedicle, providing wide exposure to the nasopharynx and anterior ITF. Contraindicated for lesions involving the petrous ICA or extending into the posterior ITF.

### Extended Endoscopic Endonasal Approaches

The extended transnasal endoscopic approaches (EEAs) have largely replaced open procedures for midline and paramedian skull base pathology. Two surgeons use the four-hands technique with the patient supine. The transnasal, transmaxillary route with removal of the posterior maxillary wall gives the most lateral trajectory to the anterolateral ITF. The addition of the transpterygoid approach through the PPF opens the anterior ITF and peritubal compartment, which can be extended posteriorly to the foramen lacerum and petrous apex. The classic transnasal pathway after reduction of the vomer and posterior septum provides workspace for nasopharyngeal and clival lesions.

Advantages of EEAs include unparalleled visualization with HD optics, direct access to pathology, and reduced morbidity compared to open approaches. Limitations include restricted access to the lateral ITF and parapharyngeal space, challenging dural reconstruction, and limited data on margin assessment in malignant disease.

### Lateral Approaches (Preauricular-Cervical)

Lateral skull base approaches via a preauricular incision combined with cervical extension provide access to the middle ear, mastoid, sigmoid sinus, jugular bulb, parotid gland, facial nerve, and ITF. These can be extended to reach the clivus, basiocciput, basisphenoid, and nasopharynx. Four muscles lateral to the skull base are important in achieving surgical exposure: the masseter, temporalis, sternomastoid, and digastric.

### Limitations Common to All Approaches

- **ICA proximity**: The intimate relationship of the ICA to the ITF and skull base tumours mandates thorough pre-operative vascular assessment and balloon test occlusion
- **Cranial nerve density**: The mandibular nerve (V3), facial nerve, and lower cranial nerves (IX-XII) are all at risk
- **Deep location**: Long working distances and restricted operative corridors limit instrumentation
- **Combination approaches**: Many extensive tumours require combined anterior-lateral or open-endoscopic approaches for complete resection

## PART C: EMBOLIZATION -- PRINCIPLES AND INDICATIONS

### Indications for Pre-operative Embolization

The main indication for pre-operative embolization of skull base tumours is to reduce intra-operative blood loss and improve visualization of the operative field during surgery. This may translate into shorter operative times and lead to a safer, easier, and more successful surgical procedure. Embolization is rarely curative but improves the rate of radical tumour removal and reduces the surgical complication rate and the incidence of recurrence.

Other indications include:
- **Palliation** of symptoms by inducing a reduction in tumour size
- **Control of haemorrhage** caused by tumour erosion of a vessel wall

### Fundamental Principle

The aim of tumour embolization is **complete obliteration of the vascular bed within the tumour**. Simply occluding the feeding arteries with emboli may result in the recruitment of a new collateral blood supply and make removal of the tumour even more difficult. Tumours with a rich vascular bed are most suitable for embolization, a feature usually indicated by the degree of contrast enhancement on CT or MRI, although increased enhancement can also be produced by accumulation in extravascular intercellular spaces.

### Skull Base Tumours Benefiting from Embolization

- Paragangliomas ([[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]]s)
- Juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibromas (JNA)
- Meningiomas
- Chordomas
- Sarcomas
- Oestrogenic tumours
- Metastases
- Olfactory neuroblastomas

### Diagnostic Angiography

Diagnostic angiography is an essential part of the pre-operative evaluation of skull base tumours, particularly if neurointerventional endovascular treatment is being considered. At the very least, both ICAs and both ECAs are examined. Vertebral artery injection and selective ascending pharyngeal artery injection may be considered given its extensive supply to the skull base, temporal bone, and dura. For any skull base tumour related topographically to a segment of the ICA, **determination of the intracranial collateral circulation at the level of the Circle of Willis is absolutely mandatory**.

A more detailed angiographic assessment is required for tumours in which embolization is being considered. This includes identification of all feeding pedicles, a search for collateral supply, dangerous arterioarterial anastomoses, and any arterial supply to cranial nerves, skin, or pinna. In addition, the vascular composition, angioarchitecture, and venous drainage must be assessed.

### Balloon Test Occlusion (BTO)

BTO is performed under **local anaesthetic** to enable assessment of motor and sensory function, speech, and visual fields. Following femoral arterial puncture, a guiding catheter is placed in the ICA. The patient is fully heparinized. A soft micro-balloon is inflated at the point of anticipated permanent occlusion for approximately **30 minutes**, during which both clinical and angiographic evaluation is performed.

Clinical tolerance of BTO implies a reduced risk with parent vessel sacrifice but does not eliminate the risk of stroke. A study of 150 subjects reported a **stroke risk of 16%** with parent vessel sacrifice even after passing BTO; however, another smaller study in 31 patients utilizing BTO and SPECT reported no neurological sequelae with occlusion in patients who passed both tests.

An **external-internal carotid bypass** should be considered for patients who fail the test procedure yet require carotid occlusion.

## PART D: EMBOLIC MATERIALS

### Classification and Characteristics

Microcatheters enable superselective catheterization and embolization of individual feeding arteries, generally less than 0.02 inches in diameter. Two types of microcatheter are used: flow-directed catheters (suitable for tumours with high-flow arteriovenous shunts) and over-the-wire catheters (for tumours with slower blood flows).

| Category | Agent | Key Features |
|----------|-------|-------------|
| Mechanical | Coils, Gelfoam | Proximal vessel occlusion |
| Particulate (non-spherical) | PVA particles | Less predictable penetration |
| Particulate (spherical) | Embosphere, Embozene (40-1300 um) | Predictable penetration, longer occlusion |
| Liquid (glue) | NBCA (N-butyl-cyanoacrylate) | For AV shunting tumours |
| Liquid (polymer) | Onyx, PHIL | Direct puncture or transarterial |
| Liquid (sclerosant) | Dehydrated alcohol | Haemangiomatous malformations |

### Mechanical Agents

**Coils and Gelfoam** are used for vessel occlusion proximal to the tumour. Gelfoam powder is derived from pork-skin gelatin and can be cut to appropriate sizes for more proximal vessel occlusion, often following embolization of distal vessels with particulate material.

### Particulate Agents

Spherical particles (Embosphere, Embozene) are the most widely used particulate embolic agents for skull base tumour embolization and are available in sizes from 40 to 1300 micrometres. They offer more predictable embolic penetration compared to PVA and have a longer occlusive effect. Smaller particles (40-140 um range) are more effective in inducing tumour necrosis but are also more likely to cause ischaemia of normal tissue.

**Critical size considerations:**
- Particles **less than 80 um** may risk cranial nerve palsy, likely due to occlusion of the **vasa nervorum**
- Angiographically **non-visualized arterial anastomoses** exist in the **50-80 um** size range
- It is therefore recommended to use particles **greater than 150 um**

### Liquid Embolic Agents

**NBCA** is a polymerizing glue useful for embolization of tumours exhibiting rapid arteriovenous shunting, where particles may pass directly through the tumour to the lungs. **Onyx** (ev3 Neurovascular) and **PHIL** (Precipitating Hydrophobic Injectable Liquid) can be used for both transarterial embolization and direct tumoural puncture. **Dehydrated alcohol** is used mainly for percutaneous treatment of haemangiomatous malformations of the head and neck. Liquid embolic agents can also be injected into the tumour upon direct puncture and have been shown to decrease intra-operative blood loss.

## PART E: TUMOUR-SPECIFIC EMBOLIZATION

### Paragangliomas

Angiography has several roles in paraganglioma management: identification of blood supply and flow dynamics, delineation of venous drainage, demonstration of intraluminal tumour in the internal jugular vein, assessment of Circle of Willis for possible carotid occlusion, and detection of synchronous tumours.

**All paragangliomas are supplied by branches of the ascending pharyngeal artery:**
- **Inferior tympanic branch** supplies glomus tympanicum tumours
- **Neuromeningeal branch** supplies glomus jugulare tumours
- **Musculospinal branch** supplies glomus vagale and carotid body tumours

Carotid body tumours acquire additional supply from the carotid body artery arising directly from the carotid bifurcation and splay the ICA and ECA. Glomus vagale tumours displace both ICA and ECA anteriorly and receive additional blood supply from muscular branches of the occipital artery.

Histological studies reveal a centripetally orientated system of intratumoural arterioles (approximately 90 um at the periphery, 300-600 um centrally) with arteriolo-arteriolar anastomoses and arteriolar-venous shunts. The multi-compartmental blood supply requires separate injections of embolic agent into each feeding artery.

Pre-operative embolization of paragangliomas of **3 cm or more** with PVA is safe, facilitates surgery, and results in significantly reduced operative bleeding, shorter operative times, and decreased surgical risk. Liquid embolic agents (Onyx, PHIL) can also be used for transarterial embolization or direct tumoural puncture. A study comparing transarterial particulate embolization with direct puncture and percutaneous embolization of carotid body tumours with Onyx demonstrated **significantly lower intra-operative blood loss** with the direct puncture technique.

Supply from AICA or PICA indicates **intradural spread**, and in these cases the risk of posterior fossa stroke **precludes embolization**.

### Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma (JNA)

JNAs are non-encapsulated fibrovascular tumours arising in the region of the sphenopalatine foramen. Their vascular components consist of endothelial-lined spaces without muscular layers, and they bleed profusely. The **distal internal maxillary artery** together with its sphenopalatine and pterygovaginal branches supplies the tumour at its site of origin. Larger tumours extending into the sphenoid sinus, ITF, or parapharyngeal space receive additional supply from accessory meningeal, ascending pharyngeal, and ascending palatine arteries. Advanced tumours may have supply from ICA and vertebral artery branches, with bilateral blood supply in some cases.

Prior to endovascular techniques, average intra-operative blood loss was approximately **2000 mL**; this has been reduced to **less than 1000 mL** by the routine use of embolization. Direct percutaneous puncture with Onyx embolization prior to surgery demonstrates significantly lower intra-operative blood loss and reduced red blood cell transfusion requirements compared to transarterial particulate embolization alone.

### Meningiomas

Meningiomas are often hypervascular, with the angioblastic and transitional types more vascular than fibroblastic or syncytial types. Psammomatous subtypes are sometimes almost avascular. There is usually a **dual blood supply**: a radial pattern of meningeal artery vessels penetrating from the base, and an additional pial supply from cortical arteries arising from the ICA. Embolization is **most effective when an exclusive meningeal supply** is present.

Orbital roof meningiomas are supplied by the anterior frontal meningeal artery and anterior branches of the MMA. Olfactory groove and planum sphenoidale meningiomas receive supply from the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arterial branches of the ophthalmic artery. The **small size** of these dural branches, **unfavourable vessel geometry**, and the dangers associated with the ophthalmic artery territory all **limit the role of embolization** for frontobasal meningiomas.

The efficacy of embolization can be assessed with gadolinium-enhanced MRI, showing **loss of flow signal void**, decreased contrast enhancement, and new areas of non-enhancement indicating tumour necrosis. The optimum timing of surgery post-embolization is debated: some surgeons operate within hours to avoid acute peritumoural oedema, while others wait approximately **four days** to allow tumoural necrosis. Corticosteroids given immediately before embolization are thought to limit tumour swelling.

Reported complication rates in meningioma embolization have fallen from as high as **21% to 6%** in more recent literature. A review of 36 studies involving 459 patients reported **two mortalities**.

### Complications of Embolization

Embolization is preferably performed under **general anaesthesia** to guarantee optimal working conditions and enable haemodynamic monitoring.

**Carotid angiography complications:**
- Permanent neurological deficit in **less than 1%**
- Arterial dissection
- Groin haematoma
- Contrast media reactions (itch to anaphylactic shock)
- Contrast-induced nephropathy (low risk even in emergency settings)

**Embolization-specific complications:**
- **Blindness** -- due to inadvertent injection of embolic material into the ophthalmic artery through anastomoses with the MMA, other arterioarterial anastomoses, or unrecognized variant origins
- **Skin necrosis** -- caused by embolic occlusion of cutaneous ECA branches; the **pinna is particularly susceptible**, and embolization in the anterior and posterior auricular arteries should be conducted with extreme care
- **Cranial nerve palsy** -- from small volumes of embolic material in vessels traversing cranial nerve foramina
- **Stroke** -- from flow reversal through extra-to-intracranial anastomoses during embolization
- **Meningioma-specific**: tumoural haemorrhage, hemiparesis secondary to tumour oedema; risk factors include large tumour size and presence of cysts

**Critical safety principle:** The vascular anatomy of the skull base is complex. Any foramen, especially those carrying a cranial nerve, can be expected to contain a potential site of communication between intra- and extracranial circulations. During embolization, as blood flow towards the target decreases, the direction of flow in collateral pathways may reverse and result in flow through extra-to-intracranial anastomoses not evident on the initial angiogram. Repeated angiograms may have to be performed during the procedure to exclude this possibility. These techniques are certainly **not for the occasional operator**.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The ITF has no anatomical floor and is continuous with the neck; its roof is formed by the greater wing of sphenoid, lateral limit by the zygomatic arch and mandibular ramus, and medial limit by the medial pterygoid and interpterygoid fascia.
>
> 2. The ITF contains the deep parotid lobe, pterygoid muscles, maxillary artery (three parts with five branches from each), mandibular nerve (V3), otic ganglion, pterygoid venous plexus, and chorda tympani -- the pterygoid plexus communicates with the cavernous sinus.
>
> 3. Surgical approaches to the ITF include the Fisch classification (Types A-D), transmandibulotomy-transcervical, maxillary swing, extended endoscopic endonasal, and lateral preauricular-cervical approaches -- each with specific indications and limitations.
>
> 4. The main indication for skull base embolization is reduction of intra-operative blood loss and improved operative visualization -- embolization is rarely curative but improves radical removal rates and reduces complication rates and recurrence.
>
> 5. The aim is complete obliteration of the tumour vascular bed; proximal feeding artery occlusion alone recruits collateral supply and makes surgery more difficult.
>
> 6. Embolic agents are classified as mechanical (coils/Gelfoam), particulate (PVA/Embosphere), and liquid (NBCA/Onyx/PHIL); particles greater than 150 um are recommended as particles less than 80 um risk cranial nerve palsy from vasa nervorum occlusion.
>
> 7. Pre-operative embolization of JNA reduces blood loss from approximately 2000 mL to less than 1000 mL; direct percutaneous Onyx embolization demonstrates lower blood loss than transarterial particulate embolization alone.
>
> 8. All paragangliomas are supplied by ascending pharyngeal artery branches; PVA embolization of tumours 3 cm or more significantly reduces operative bleeding, operative time, and surgical risk.
>
> 9. BTO is performed under local anaesthesia for 30 minutes to assess tolerance to potential carotid sacrifice; a stroke risk of 16% exists even after passing BTO -- an EC-IC bypass should be considered if BTO fails and carotid occlusion is needed.
>
> 10. Carotid angiography carries less than 1% risk of permanent neurological deficit; embolization complications include blindness, stroke, skin necrosis, and cranial nerve palsy -- these procedures are not for occasional operators.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - During embolization, flow reversal through previously non-visualized extra-to-intracranial anastomoses can occur as blood flow to the target decreases -- repeated angiograms during the procedure are mandatory to exclude dangerous reflux into the intracranial circulation.
>
> - The pinna is particularly susceptible to skin necrosis after embolization; the anterior and posterior auricular arteries require extreme care, and the operator must actively search for dangerous anastomoses before injecting embolic material.
>
> - The efficacy of meningioma embolization is assessed on gadolinium-enhanced MRI by loss of flow void and new non-enhancing areas indicating necrosis; corticosteroids given before embolization help limit peritumoural swelling.
>
> - The timing of surgery post-embolization remains debated: operating within hours avoids peritumoural oedema, while waiting approximately four days allows tumoural necrosis to develop -- each approach has proponents.
>
> - Supply from AICA or PICA to a paraganglioma indicates intradural spread, and embolization in these cases risks posterior fossa stroke -- this is a contraindication to embolization.
>
> - Non-visualized arterioarterial anastomoses exist in the 50-80 um range; this is the rationale for using particulate agents greater than 150 um and explains why smaller particles, although more effective at inducing necrosis, carry unacceptable risks of cranial nerve injury and stroke.
