# Paranasal Sinus Mucocoeles & Nasal Drug Delivery Methods

**Question 1:** *How to classify and treat frontoethmoidal mucocoeles? How to prevent recurrence?*

**Question 2:** *Methods of instillation of nasal drops and spray. Exhalation delivery system and uses.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1: Chapter 100 (Mucoceles of the Paranasal Sinuses), Chapter 28 (Drug Therapy in Rhinology)

---

## Introduction

Paranasal sinus mucocoeles are epithelium-lined, mucus-filled sacs that form within a paranasal sinus secondary to outflow obstruction and inflammatory processes. They represent an important cause of orbital and intracranial complications in rhinological practice. The frontal and ethmoidal sinuses are most commonly affected due to their complex and narrow drainage pathways. Management centres on wide endoscopic marsupialization with long-term follow-up to prevent recurrence. Separately, effective topical nasal drug delivery is fundamental to rhinological therapeutics. The method of instillation significantly influences drug distribution within the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. Understanding the principles of nasal drops, sprays and emerging technologies such as exhalation delivery systems is essential for optimizing treatment outcomes.

---

## PART A: Definition, Pathogenesis and Classification of Mucocoeles

### Definition

A mucocoele is an epithelium-lined mucus-filled sac within a paranasal sinus with expansion of the sinus cavity and remodelling of the sinus walls. It forms secondary to obstruction of the sinus outflow tract together with an inflammatory process within the sinus. It is lined by pseudostratified or low-columnar epithelium — in contradistinction to a cyst that has a distinct separate membrane. Where bony erosion has occurred, the mucocoele's epithelium is often fused with dura or orbital periosteum. When the mucocoele becomes secondarily infected, it is termed a **mucopyocele** (pyocele).

### Pathogenesis

Two factors are essential for mucocoele formation:

1. **Obstructed sinus outflow tract** — preventing mucus drainage
2. **Inflammatory process within the sinus** — driving continued mucus secretion

Osteolytic cytokines (IL-1, TNF) are present within the epithelial lining of mucocoeles and are responsible for the bony erosion and expansion that characterize these lesions.

### Classification by Site

The frontal, ethmoid, maxillary and sphenoid sinuses are involved in descending order of frequency. [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|Frontal sinus]] mucocoeles are probably more common because of the complex and narrow drainage pathway that is easily obstructed.

| Site | Frequency (Bockmühl) | Frequency (Lee) |
|------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Frontal | 43% | 45% |
| Frontoethmoidal | 8% | 28% |
| Ethmoid | 14% | 18% |
| Sphenoethmoidal | 10% | — |
| Maxillary | 25% | 9% |

Mucocoeles can also form in any other aerated structure, including a concha bullosa of the middle or superior turbinate. Obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct causes a lacrimal sac mucocoele (dacryocele).

### Classification by Aetiology

| Cause | Frequency | Details |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| Prior sinus surgery | 66-86% | Commonest cause; esp. external fronto-ethmoidectomy |
| Trauma | 10-14% | Mean interval: 9 years |
| Spontaneous | 15-17% | Idiopathic; no prior surgery/trauma |
| CRS without surgery | 2% | Chronic inflammatory obstruction |
| Tumours | 1-5% | Benign or malignant causing obstruction |

**Prior sinus surgery** is now the leading cause of mucocoele formation. The interval between surgery and mucocoele development ranges from 1 to 19 years, with frontal and sphenoid mucocoeles developing earlier than maxillary ones. The Lynch-Howarth operation and Caldwell-Luc procedures are particularly associated with mucocoele formation — in one large series, 78% of post-surgical mucocoeles were associated with Lynch-Howarth and/or Caldwell-Luc operations, versus only 1.5% after endonasal surgery.

External fronto-ethmoidectomy causes mucocoeles through loss of lateral bony support of the frontal recess, leading to herniation of periorbital tissue into the frontal sinus outflow tract. Endoscopic surgery in the frontal recess can cause circumferential injury to the frontal sinus outflow tract with subsequent stenosis.

A significant incidence (50% in one study) of mucocoeles has been reported following endoscopic repair of skull-base fractures in children using middle turbinate overlay patches.

## PART B: Clinical Features and Diagnosis

### Symptoms

Bony erosion leads to expansion of the sac beyond the sinus cavity into the cranial cavity or orbit. Symptoms depend on the sinus involved.

**Ophthalmological symptoms** (more common than rhinological):

- Periorbital swelling (most common presentation)
- Pain
- Exophthalmos / proptosis
- Limited ocular mobility and diplopia
- Visual disturbance
- Optic neuropathy (up to 18% of patients presenting with ophthalmological symptoms — due to direct compression in posterior ethmoid/sphenoid)

**Rhinological symptoms:**

- Nasal obstruction (especially concha bullosa mucocoele)
- Nasal discharge
- Anosmia

**Other presentations:**

- Visible forehead mass (frontal mucocoele)
- Medial canthal swelling
- Gingivobuccal sulcus mass (maxillary mucocoele)
- Epiphora with cystic medial canthal swelling (dacryocele)

### Imaging

**CT scan** is the imaging modality of choice:

- Homogeneous, isodense lesion within an expanded sinus
- Bony remodelling of sinus walls
- Bony defect of lamina papyracea and/or superomedial orbital rim (frontoethmoidal)
- Globe displacement (laterally and/or inferiorly)
- Posterior table erosion of frontal sinus with intracranial extension
- Contrast enhancement only with pyocele (infected mucocoele)

**MRI** should be considered when significant bony erosion of the posterior table or lamina papyracea exists, to delineate mucocoele from adjacent soft tissue (brain, orbit). MRI can be misleading due to variable mucocoele content — mucocoeles with high water content are hyperintense on T1; pyoceles have higher protein content and variable signal intensity on both T1 and T2 sequences.

### Differential Diagnosis

1. Benign or malignant tumours
2. Allergic fungal sinusitis / other fungal disease
3. Cholesterol granuloma (rare)

## PART C: Surgical Management and Prevention of Recurrence

### Principle of Treatment

The goal is to widely marsupialize the sac to provide permanent ventilation and sinus drainage and to relieve pressure on vital structures. Cosmetic deformities often settle with time as bony remodelling takes place.

### Frontoethmoidal Mucocoeles

Wide marsupialization is essential whether by endoscopic and/or open technique.

**Endoscopic approach (first line):**

- Wide marsupialization/nasalization is the first-line treatment for simple mucocoeles
- Recurrence rates with endoscopic techniques: 0-25%
- The **Modified Endoscopic Lothrop Procedure (MELP / Draf III)** is often required for more complex frontal sinus mucocoeles
- MELP is especially useful where there is loss of lateral support in the frontal recess due to bony erosion or previous removal of the superior lamina papyracea

**Combined approaches:**

- Required for laterally located mucocoeles where thick bony septations prevent endoscopic access alone
- Lynch-Howarth approach can access laterally located frontal sinus mucocoeles but carries risk of long-term frontal outflow obstruction
- Osteoplastic frontal flap in combination with MELP may be required
- **Transorbital Neuro-Endoscopic Surgery (TONES)** — specifically the superior eyelid approach — provides an alternative, less invasive approach for lateral frontal sinus pathology

**Stenting:**

- Remains controversial
- Most authors do not advocate stent use except in smaller unilateral marsupializations
- If placed, the stent must be loose-fitting to prevent circumferential pressure necrosis leading to stenosis

**Obliteration:**

- Has a high success rate (93%) but major complication rate > 20%
- Contraindicated when there is extensive posterior table erosion (inability to remove epithelium on dura)
- Has largely fallen out of favour

### Maxillary Sinus Mucocoeles

- Wide middle meatal antrostomy usually suffices
- Partial medial maxillectomy with lacrimal system preservation may be needed for laterally located mucocoeles
- Caldwell-Luc approach only where endoscopic surgery is unavailable

### Sphenoid Sinus Mucocoeles

- Wide sphenoidotomy and intranasal marsupialization are all that is required
- No attempt to remove lateral sphenoid mucosa — bony erosion places the internal carotid artery and optic nerve at risk

### Other Mucocoeles

- Concha bullosa mucocoeles: resection of lateral aspect of concha bullosa
- Dacryoceles: endoscopic [[Lacrimal System DCR|dacryocystorhinostomy]]

### Prevention of Recurrence

- **Wide marsupialization** — the single most important factor; narrow marsupialization leads to restenosis
- **MELP/Draf III** for complex frontal mucocoeles — provides the widest possible frontal drainage pathway
- **Avoid circumferential injury** to the frontal recess during primary FESS
- **Preserve mucosa** where possible during initial sinus surgery
- **Meticulous post-operative care** — regular endoscopic debridement and surveillance
- **Long-term follow-up** — essential as recurrence can occur decades after initial treatment
- **Avoid stenting unless necessary** — and ensure loose fit if used
- **Prefer endoscopic over external approaches** for primary surgery — external approaches (Lynch-Howarth, Caldwell-Luc) carry higher mucocoele formation rates

### Complications of Mucocoele Surgery

- Sudden expansion of cranial contents during decompression of a large frontal mucocoele can disrupt dural vessels → subdural haematoma
- Dural disruption → [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]
- Close post-operative monitoring is essential in cases with significant intracranial compression

## PART D: Methods of Nasal Drop and Spray Instillation

### Nasal Sprays

Nasal sprays are the most widely used delivery method for topical nasal medications, particularly intranasal corticosteroids. The key types include:

**Metered-dose nasal sprays (MDNS):**

- Deliver a consistent, pre-measured dose per actuation
- Available corticosteroids: mometasone furoate, fluticasone propionate, fluticasone furoate, budesonide, beclomethasone dipropionate, ciclesonide, triamcinolone acetonide
- Also available for antihistamines (azelastine), anticholinergics (ipratropium bromide 0.03%), and cromones (sodium cromoglicate 4%)

**Correct technique for nasal spray administration:**

- If two nasal sprays are to be administered to each nostril, one spray should be directed **upwards** and the other **backwards**
- The patient should **not breathe or breathe in gently** during administration
- Avoid sniffing vigorously — this directs the drug to the nasopharynx rather than the target areas
- Use the opposite hand to the nostril being sprayed (right hand for left nostril) to angle the spray laterally

**Limitations of nasal sprays:**

- Limited penetration beyond the nasal valve region
- Poor distribution to the olfactory cleft, sinuses and posterior nasal cavity
- Particle size affects deposition — larger particles deposit anteriorly
- Compliance issues with long-term use

### Nasal Drops

Nasal drops allow higher doses of topical medication and improved distribution compared to sprays, particularly for reaching the olfactory cleft.

**Correct position for nasal drop instillation — Head-down (Moffett's position):**

The best position for administering nasal drops is **head-down** (also known as the Kaiteki or Mygind position). The patient kneels and places the head on the floor with the vertex pointing downwards, or hangs the head off the edge of a bed. This position maximizes distribution of drops to:

- Olfactory cleft (important for anosmia treatment)
- Middle meatus and ethmoid region
- Frontal recess

**Fluticasone propionate nasules:**

- 400 μg per nasule — one nasule shared between each nostril once daily
- Provides a precisely known dose
- Higher topical steroid dose than spray formulations
- Particularly useful in nasal polyposis and post-operative treatment

**Other drop preparations:**

- Ephedrine nasal drops (0.5% and 1%) — weak sympathomimetic decongestant
- Oxymetazoline/xylometazoline drops — more potent vasoconstrictors (limit to 10 days to avoid rhinitis medicamentosa)
- Sodium cromoglicate 4% (Rynacrom) — prophylactic use in [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|allergic rhinitis]] (requires 4-6 times daily dosing, limiting compliance)
- 25% glucose in glycerine drops — for atrophic rhinitis; restores moisture to nasal mucosa (3-month shelf life)

### Saline Irrigation

Saline nasal irrigation (douching) is an effective adjunct treatment for rhinosinusitis. Methods include:

- Large-volume low-pressure irrigation (squeeze bottle, neti pot)
- Nebulized saline
- Saline sprays (lower volume)

When combined with a wide post-surgical cavity, the ability to control sinus mucosa with irrigations and topical therapies is vastly improved compared to simple nasal sprays.

### Other Delivery Methods

**Cocaine preparation for surgery:**

- Cocaine solution (5% or 10%) or cocaine paste (25%) used to prepare the nose prior to surgery
- Moffat's solution application by spray immediately after induction of anaesthesia provides vasoconstriction and local anaesthesia for a bloodless surgical field

**Nebulized delivery:**

- Provides finer particle sizes (1-5 μm) for deeper deposition
- Useful for post-operative sinus medication delivery through opened sinus cavities
- Can be combined with mucolytic agents

## PART E: Exhalation Delivery System (EDS) and Uses

### Concept and Rationale

The Exhalation Delivery System (EDS) is an innovative nasal drug delivery technology based on the principle of **bidirectional nasal airflow**. During exhalation against resistance, the soft palate closes automatically, sealing the nasal cavity from the oral cavity. This creates a closed nasal circuit where air entering one nostril exits through the other.

### Mechanism of Action

The EDS device has a nosepiece inserted into one nostril and a mouthpiece through which the patient exhales. The sequence is:

1. Patient places nosepiece in one nostril and mouthpiece in the mouth
2. Patient exhales through the mouthpiece
3. Exhalation pressure closes the soft palate (velopharyngeal closure)
4. Air carrying the drug is directed into the nose through the nosepiece
5. Air flows through the nasal cavity in a bidirectional pattern — entering one nostril and exiting the other
6. The positive pressure and bidirectional flow deposits drug in areas poorly reached by conventional sprays

### Advantages Over Conventional Nasal Sprays

| Feature | Conventional Spray | Exhalation Delivery |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Soft palate position | Open | Closed (seals nasopharynx) |
| Drug loss to pharynx | Significant | Minimal |
| Olfactory cleft delivery | Poor | Improved |
| Sinus penetration | Minimal | Enhanced |
| Post-nasal drip sensation | Common | Reduced |
| Posterior nasal cavity | Poor coverage | Good coverage |

### Available EDS Formulations

**EDS-Fluticasone (XHANCE — fluticasone propionate):**

- FDA-approved for nasal polyps treatment
- 93 μg per spray, twice daily (2 sprays per nostril)
- Demonstrated superiority over conventional fluticasone spray in clinical trials for nasal polyp reduction and symptom improvement
- Better drug deposition in the middle meatus, olfactory cleft and sinus ostia

### Clinical Applications

- **Nasal polyposis:** Primary approved indication; improved polyp grade reduction compared to conventional sprays
- **[[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|Chronic rhinosinusitis]]:** Enhanced delivery of corticosteroids to sinus mucosa
- **Post-operative sinus care:** Improved medication delivery to opened sinuses
- **Olfactory dysfunction:** Better olfactory cleft targeting for steroid treatment
- **Allergic rhinitis:** Potential for improved symptom control through better mucosal coverage

### Limitations

- Requires patient cooperation and correct technique
- More expensive than conventional sprays
- Contraindicated in patients unable to exhale (young children, severe respiratory compromise)
- Limited availability in some markets

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Recurrence can occur decades after mucocoele treatment** — long-term endoscopic follow-up is mandatory; one follow-up visit is never sufficient.
> - **Sudden decompression of a large frontal mucocoele** displacing the frontal lobe can cause subdural haematoma from dural vessel disruption — decompress slowly and monitor closely.
> - **Optic neuropathy occurs in up to 18%** of patients presenting with ophthalmological symptoms — this is a surgical emergency requiring urgent decompression.
> - **Rhinitis medicamentosa** results from overuse of sympathomimetic decongestant nasal sprays — limit topical decongestant use to 10 days maximum.
> - **TONES (Transorbital Neuro-Endoscopic Surgery)** via the superior eyelid approach is an emerging less invasive alternative for laterally located frontal sinus mucocoeles.
> - **Sodium cromoglicate is safe in young children** but requires 4-6 times daily dosing — poor compliance is the main limitation; no significant side effects reported.
