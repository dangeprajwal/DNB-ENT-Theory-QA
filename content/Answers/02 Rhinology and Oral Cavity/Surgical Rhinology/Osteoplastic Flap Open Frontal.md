# Osteoplastic Flap and Open [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|Frontal Sinus]] Approaches

**Question:** *Q120 (Oct 2024): Describe the osteoplastic approach to the frontal sinus including indications, surgical steps and complications. Q123 (Jun 2025): Discuss the open surgical approaches to the frontal sinus. Write short notes on hybrid FESS and frontal balloon sinuplasty.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 99 (The Frontal Sinus)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The frontal sinus is arguably the most complex of the paranasal sinuses. Over the years, the surgical management of frontal sinus disease has evolved from open obliterative procedures to endoscopic mucosal-sparing techniques. Although extensive and complex frontal sinus pathology can be managed endoscopically, there remains a definitive role for open procedures such as the osteoplastic flap (OPF). The best surgical approach to the frontal sinus is a graduated one, selecting the least invasive procedure to achieve the desired outcome. In a large tertiary series performing over 700 frontal sinus procedures in a 3-year period, less than 2% of cases were managed by OPF with obliteration, reflecting the dwindling indications for open surgery in the current endoscopic era.

---

## PART A: OVERVIEW OF FRONTAL SINUS SURGICAL APPROACHES

The most widely accepted classification of frontal sinus surgical approaches is that described by Draf. The classification describes increasingly aggressive approaches to the frontal sinus. The general rule is to choose the least invasive approach to achieve the desired outcome. The surgery must be tailored to each patient and depends on factors such as previous surgery, extent of disease and anatomy.

| Procedure | Description | Key Feature |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Draf I | Ethmoidectomy + frontal sinusotomy | Clears cells below ostium |
| Draf IIa | Enlarge ostium LP to MT | Unilateral widening |
| Draf IIb | Enlarge ostium LP to septum | Wider; includes MT resection |
| Draf III (MELP) | Bilateral ostia + septectomy + ISS + floor | Maximum endoscopic drainage |
| OPF | Anterior table removal, access from above | Unparalleled sinus access |
| OPF + obliteration | Remove all mucosa + obliterate with fat | Renders sinus non-functional |
| Riedel's procedure | Remove anterior table entirely | Cosmetic deformity |
| Cranialization | Remove mucosa + posterior table | For posterior table fractures |

### Factors Influencing the Surgical Approach

The choice of surgical procedure depends on multiple patient, anatomical and pathological factors:

- **Patient factors:** Compliance issues and significant comorbidities favour simple FESS with minimal manipulation. Asthma and aspirin sensitivity require comprehensive frontal recess clearance.
- **Anatomical factors:** Preserved landmarks with minimal previous surgery and no complex cells favour a Draf type I procedure. Poor landmarks, osteoneogenesis, multiple revisions, thick frontal beak and complex frontal cells require a Draf III (MELP) approach.
- **Pathological factors:** Uncomplicated CRS with or without polyps and no previous surgery responds to Draf I. Recurrent disease, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease with polyps, eosinophilic mucin CRS and tumours extending laterally require a Draf III or osteoplastic flap approach. Complicated frontal sinusitis with posterior table erosion, anterior table osteitis or tumours warrant a Draf III or OPF, potentially as a combined approach.

## PART B: THE OSTEOPLASTIC FLAP PROCEDURE -- INDICATIONS

The OPF procedure was developed to manage chronic frontal sinus disease refractory to procedures such as the external frontoethmoidectomy, which were associated with high failure rates. Obliteration with fat was introduced later and popularized by Goodale and Montgomery. Although the OPF with fat obliteration is reported to be the gold standard treatment with success rates over 90%, it does have a long-term failure rate of up to 25%. Without question, the OPF approach offers unparalleled access to the frontal sinus and is indicated when wide exposure is necessary.

### Indications for OPF

| Scenario | Pathology |
|----------|-----------|
| Wide exposure needed, endoscopically inaccessible | Large osteomas, [[Schneiderian Papilloma|inverted papilloma]] with lateral extension, malignant tumours, far lateral mucoceles |
| Failed endoscopic procedures | Maximal medical therapy and MELPs have failed |
| Extensive frontal bone osteomyelitis | Unresponsive to medical therapy and ESS |
| Extensive neo-osteogenesis | Secondary to failed ESS, facial fractures, osteomyelitis |
| Diseased small underdeveloped sinuses | AP distance <8 mm making MELP difficult |
| Frontal sinus fractures | Anterior and posterior table fractures, [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]s, outflow tract involvement |

### OPF Without Obliteration

An OPF can be performed without obliteration to access the sinus for tumour removal or mucocele drainage while preserving sinus function. The ability to tackle pathology from above via an OPF approach and simultaneously endoscopically restore ventilation from below (the "above and below" approach) has offered an alternative to obliteration. This retains a functioning sinus that is endoscopically accessible and can be monitored by imaging.

### OPF With Obliteration

In a graduated approach to frontal sinus surgery, obliteration is considered the final step, as the frontal sinus is rendered non-functional. Its success depends on the complete removal of all mucous membrane elements. In the current endoscopic era, the indications for obliteration are dwindling. Traditionally, obliteration was suggested for fractures of the anterior table involving the outflow tract; currently, one might consider repairing the fracture, monitoring with imaging and endoscopically intervening at a later stage if necessary.

## PART C: OPF -- SURGICAL STEPS

### Step 1: Incision

The anterior table of the frontal sinus may be exposed through several incision types:

- **Coronal (bicoronal) incision** -- most commonly used; best cosmesis
- **Brow incision** -- useful for limited unilateral exposure
- **Mid-forehead incision** -- placed in a forehead crease
- **Zigzag coronal incisions** -- better camouflaged in male pattern baldness

### Step 2: Injection and Preparation

A combination of local anaesthesia (lignocaine 1%), vasoconstrictors (adrenaline 1:1000), steroid (Adcortyl 5 mg) and hyaluronidase (Hylase 1500 IU) is injected to aid dissection and minimize bleeding.

### Step 3: Scalp Flap Elevation

An incision is made down to the periosteum. Using a combination of blunt and sharp dissection, the scalp flap is elevated to the supraorbital ridge and over the root of the nose. The flap is pulled caudally on both sides, leaving behind the periosteum and the bone, thus preserving the supraorbital and supratrochlear neurovascular bundles.

### Step 4: Defining Frontal Sinus Extent

Precisely defining the extent of frontal sinus pneumatization is essential to avoid inadvertent intracranial entry. Methods include:

- **6-foot Caldwell view (occipitofrontal X-ray):** Used as a template to mark out the frontal sinus margin; this is the conventional method
- **CT-generated templates:** Virtually identical to Caldwell radiograph-derived templates and may obviate the need for additional imaging
- **Transillumination:** An alternative but less reliable method
- **Surgical navigation:** Shown to be more accurate and may reduce the risk of inadvertent intracranial entry
- **3D models:** Created from pre-operative CT data to produce an accurate template

### Step 5: Periosteal Incision and Osteotomy

- The periosteum is incised **1.5 cm outside** the frontal sinus margin (or template) and elevated to the sinus margin
- An alternative is to entirely elevate the pericranium with the skin flap
- An **oscillating saw angled at 30 degrees** towards the frontal sinus provides an opening with a large surface area (beveled edge) for easier fixation of the bone lid
- The bony incision is carried to the supraorbital ridge on both sides
- For bilateral exposure, the intersinus septum must be separated from the anterior frontal sinus wall with an angled chisel
- The bony lid is fractured and elevated inferiorly (ideally hinges on the periosteal flap, though in practice this is rarely achievable)

### Step 6: Procedure Within the Sinus

**Without obliteration:**
- Address the pathology (tumour removal, mucocele drainage)
- Restore ventilation endoscopically from below if needed ("above and below" approach)

**With obliteration:**
- Meticulous removal of ALL mucous membrane elements using the operating microscope or loupe magnification
- A diamond burr is used on the inner cortex of the sinus to exenterate mucosal invaginations and promote neovascularization
- The frontal ostium is plugged with fascia or oxidized cellulose (Surgicel)
- The sinus is obliterated with abdominal free fat (most common); alternatives include vascularized pericranial flap, cancellous bone, muscle or alloplastic material (hydroxyapatite)

### Step 7: Closure

- The bone lid is replaced and secured with titanium microplates or sutures through drill holes
- Often the periosteum may not be intact and the bone lid requires securing with mini plates
- The scalp wound is closed over a closed-suction drain

## PART D: COMPLICATIONS AND OUTCOMES OF OPF

### Complications

The OPF with obliteration is associated with significant morbidity:

- **CSF leak** -- from inadvertent posterior table or skull base breach
- **Persistent forehead numbness** -- supraorbital and supratrochlear nerve injury during flap elevation
- **Severe headaches** -- without evidence of recurrent disease
- **Frontal bossing** -- contour irregularity from bone flap malposition
- **Supraorbital neuralgia** -- chronic neuropathic pain
- **Mucocele formation** -- the most serious long-term concern; fat obliteration may not seal completely and residual mucosa regenerates; may present decades later
- **Donor site complications** -- from abdominal fat harvesting (seroma, infection, hernia)
- **Difficulties with post-operative surveillance** -- no endoscopic access; requires MRI monitoring; MRI can be difficult to distinguish fat necrosis from mucocele formation
- **Cosmetic concerns** -- forehead scar, contour deformity

### Outcomes

- **OPF with obliteration:** Success rate up to **93% at 8 years** but a long-term failure rate of up to **25%** (mainly mucocele formation)
- **MELP comparison:** Overall patency rate **95.9%**; overall failure rate 13.9% (80% of failures undergo revision MELP, remainder OPF with obliteration)
- Early patency rates of 90% at 12 months, falling to 68% after 72 months have been reported for endoscopic frontal sinus surgery

### Other Open Approaches

**Riedel's Procedure:**

Riedel's procedure involves removing the floor, anterior wall of the frontal sinus and its entire mucosal lining. Although rarely performed, it has a role in a small proportion of patients where frontal sinus drainage cannot be established and frontal sinus obliteration has failed. These include patients with osteomyelitis or tumours involving the anterior wall. The main criticism is the cosmetic defect, which can be reduced by chamfering the supraorbital ridge and smoothing the margin of the sinus to a gentle curve. As the mucosa within the sinus is removed, recurrent complications are uncommon.

**Cranialization of the Frontal Sinus:**

Cranialization for refractory chronic frontal sinusitis is rarely performed. It has a role in procedures requiring a craniotomy, performed in conjunction with neurosurgeons. The commonest indication is extensive fractures of the frontal sinus, particularly the posterior table. Other indications include tumours and osteomyelitis. Using a coronal incision, a midfrontobasal craniotomy is performed. The posterior wall and floor of the sinus are removed. Mucosa of the frontal sinus outflow tract is inverted into the nasal cavity and sealed with fascia or a strip of pericranium and fibrin glue. Success relies on meticulous removal of the entire frontal sinus mucosa and drilling the sinus with a diamond burr. Dead space between the anterior wall and dura is filled with fat or a vascularized pericranial flap. Complications include dural injury with CSF leak, bone flap necrosis, frontal lobe trauma and intracranial mucocele formation.

**External Frontoethmoidectomy (Lynch-Howarth):**

This historical procedure has been replaced by endoscopic approaches. It was conceptually flawed as it destroyed the bony support of the frontal recess, leading to scarring and fibrosis obstructing the outflow tract. The Lynch incision is still used today to externally access the anterior ethmoidal artery, drain periorbital abscesses and if extended superolaterally, to trephine the floor of the frontal sinus.

## PART E: BALLOON SINUPLASTY AND HYBRID FESS

### Balloon Sinuplasty

Introduced in 2006, balloon sinuplasty is proposed as a minimally invasive approach to the management of CRS involving the frontal sinus. In the Extent of Frontal Sinus Surgery (EFSS) classification, it corresponds to Grade 0 -- no tissue removal.

**Technique:**
- A balloon is introduced over a guide wire, which may be illuminated, to confirm its position in the frontal sinus
- Once in place spanning the ostium, the balloon is dilated with a controlled inflation device
- This gently enlarges the ostium by effecting microfractures of the surrounding bone and compressing the soft tissue
- Soft tissue injury is suggested to be minimal

**Evidence and Limitations:**
- It is a safe and well-tolerated procedure
- However, evidence in support of its use as an alternative to standard ESS techniques is **limited**
- The rate of synechia formation is reportedly at least equal to that seen with conventional ESS
- Complications include inability to cannulate the sinus and CSF leak
- **Best role:** Isolated frontal sinus disease with limited pathology and minimal polyposis; not suitable for extensive disease requiring tissue removal

### Hybrid FESS

In many instances, balloon dilation is combined with conventional ESS and is termed a **hybrid procedure**. The rationale is to use the balloon for frontal sinus ostium dilation, preserving the frontal mucosa, while conventional instruments address the ethmoid and other sinuses. This is an emerging technique with increasing but still limited high-level data.

### Above and Below Approaches

Endonasal procedures may be combined with external procedures such as an osteoplastic flap or trephine. This combined approach has meant that where some sinuses would have been obliterated in the past, it may not be necessary to do so now. It may be used when pathology in the frontal sinus outflow tract cannot adequately or safely be addressed by an endonasal approach alone. A MELP can be combined with a frontal trephine or OPF to remove tumours such as inverted papilloma with lateral attachments. The obvious advantage is to retain a functioning sinus that is endoscopically accessible and can be monitored by imaging. Applications include medially based osteomas, neo-osteogenesis of the frontal recess, posterior table CSF leaks and encephaloceles.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The osteoplastic flap offers unparalleled access to the frontal sinus but its indications are dwindling in the current endoscopic era, with less than 2% of cases requiring OPF with obliteration in major tertiary centres
> 2. Indications for OPF include large osteomas, inverted papilloma with significant lateral extension, malignant tumours, far lateral mucoceles and failed endoscopic procedures including failed MELP
> 3. The 6-foot Caldwell view X-ray or CT-generated template is essential to map frontal sinus margins during OPF; surgical navigation has been shown to be more accurate than either
> 4. The oscillating saw is angled at 30 degrees towards the sinus during osteotomy to create a beveled edge with a large surface area for easier bone flap refixation
> 5. The supraorbital and supratrochlear neurovascular bundles must be preserved during scalp flap elevation by leaving the periosteum attached to bone
> 6. Meticulous removal of ALL sinus mucosa (using diamond burr on inner cortex) is the single most critical step in frontal sinus obliteration; incomplete removal leads to mucocele formation
> 7. OPF with fat obliteration has up to 93% success at 8 years but a long-term failure rate of up to 25% due to mucocele formation from residual mucosa
> 8. Riedel's procedure (anterior table removal) is reserved for failed obliteration or osteomyelitis and is associated with significant cosmetic deformity
> 9. Balloon sinuplasty (introduced 2006) causes microfractures to enlarge the frontal ostium without tissue removal (EFSS Grade 0) but evidence for its use as an ESS alternative is limited
> 10. The MELP (Draf III) has become the preferred alternative to OPF with an overall patency rate of 95.9% and the ability to survey for recurrent disease endoscopically


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The "above and below" approach (OPF + endoscopic ventilation restoration from below) preserves sinus function while providing maximum access, and has reduced the need for frontal sinus obliteration
> - Post-OPF obliteration surveillance requires MRI -- CT cannot reliably distinguish fat from mucocele; MRI is particularly useful in assessing patients with ongoing symptoms who have previously undergone OPF with obliteration
> - Mucocele formation after obliteration may present decades later due to residual mucosa regenerating within the obliterated cavity -- lifelong follow-up is recommended
> - Zigzag coronal incisions provide better scar camouflage in patients with male pattern baldness than standard coronal incisions
> - Balloon sinuplasty is best reserved for isolated frontal sinus disease in patients without extensive polyposis; in many cases it is combined with conventional ESS as a hybrid procedure
> - When considering an OPF for tumour removal, frontal sinus obliteration should be considered a contraindication given the inability to detect subsequent tumour recurrence
