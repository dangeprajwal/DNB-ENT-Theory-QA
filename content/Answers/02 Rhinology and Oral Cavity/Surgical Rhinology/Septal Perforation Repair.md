# Nasal Septal Perforations — Aetiology, Assessment & Surgical Repair

**Question 1:** *Describe the surgical repair of septal perforations. Discuss factors influencing [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] surgical approaches.*

**Question 2:** *Describe the etiology, clinical features, investigations and management of septal perforation.*

**Question 3:** *Describe the pathogenesis of septal perforation. What are the treatment options for a large perforation?*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1: Chapter 104 (Nasal Septal Perforations — Charles East and Kevin Kulendra)

---

## Introduction

A nasal septal perforation is defined as a through-and-through defect of the nasal septum. The exact prevalence is unknown as many perforations are asymptomatic, but the incidence is estimated at just over 1%, making the condition relatively common. The majority of perforations involve the anterior quadrilateral cartilage. The most common cause in the UK is local trauma, frequently self-induced or iatrogenic following septal surgery. In a series of 69 septal perforations, 62.4% were completely free from any symptoms. The condition remains a challenging technical problem for nasal surgeons, although reliable techniques with 80–90% closure rates have been published.

## PART A: Aetiology

### Comprehensive Classification of Causes

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| **Traumatic** | Nasal surgery (SMR/septoplasty), nose picking, septal cauterization (bilateral), nasal packing for [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]], septal haematoma/abscess, cryosurgery, nasogastric/tracheal intubation, desiccation (ozena), radiation, stab/gunshot wounds, foreign bodies (button batteries) |
| **Surface irritants** | Cocaine insufflation and adulterants, heroin inhalation, decongestant nasal sprays, intranasal corticosteroids, lime/cement/glass/salt dust, tar and pitch, chromic/sulfuric acid fumes, arsenicals, mercurials, phosphorus, sodium carbonate, copper-smelting fumes |
| **Infections** | Syphilis, typhoid, diphtheria, tuberculosis, rhinoscleroma, lepromatous leprosy, leishmaniasis, mucor, rhinosporidiosis, Alternaria, actinomycosis, aspergillosis |
| **Neoplastic** | Melanoma, adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, metastatic carcinoma, lymphoma |
| **Inflammatory** | Sarcoidosis, Crohn's disease, dermatomyositis, rheumatoid arthritis, relapsing polychondritis, GPA (Wegener's), SLE |

### Pathogenesis

The sequence leading to perforation follows a consistent pattern:
1. **Mucosal inflammation** → dryness, crusting
2. **Continued irritation** (digital trauma, cocaine, surgery) → ischaemic crusty area → ulceration ("pick ulcer")
3. **Cartilage exposure** → cartilaginous necrosis
4. **Perforation** → if inflammation persists, the hole enlarges ("active perforation")
5. Large perforations may eventually make surgical closure impossible

## PART B: Symptomatology and Clinical Assessment

### Symptoms

The principal symptoms associated with perforations are:
- **Crusting** — most common; due to drying and impaired mucociliary clearance
- **Epistaxis** — recurrent, triggered when crust separates or is removed; can be significant from sphenopalatine vessels
- **Whistling** — especially with small anterior perforations
- **Dryness/discomfort** — feeling of emptiness or general nasal discomfort
- **Nasal blockage** — paradoxical; large stable perforations may cause perceived blockage despite increased airflow
- **Rhinolalia** — huge perforations creating a common nasal cavity
- **Inability to clear secretions** — with very large defects

Size and position directly influence symptoms. **Anterior perforations** and **large perforations** where the anterior margin is in front of the nasal valve are the most troublesome — due to slow mucociliary clearance, low humidity and loss of mucosa.

### Clinical Assessment

1. **Detailed history:** Nasal trauma, cautery, surgery, substance abuse (cocaine), medications, systemic disease
2. **External nasal examination:** Saddle deformity, columella retraction, deviation
3. **Nasal endoscopy:** Best method to assess margins, state of residual septum, inflammation
4. **Measurement:** Paper ruler introduced into contralateral airway, scale read through the hole
5. **Symptom scoring:** Visual analogue scale questionnaire — crusting, nosebleeds, whistling, nasal discomfort, emptiness/blockage, secretion clearance

### Special Investigations

**Baseline tests** (when aetiology unclear or inflammation present):
- Full blood count, ESR
- Urea and electrolytes, urine analysis
- C-ANCA (GPA/Wegener's)
- Treponemal investigations (syphilis)
- ACE titres (sarcoidosis)
- Chest X-ray
- Nasal swab

**Biopsy:** Not routine — rarely reveals anything other than chronic inflammation; may convert an inactive perforation to an "active state" causing enlargement. Indicated only if:
- Unexplained aetiology with persistent inflammation
- Irregular margins
- Suspicion of malignancy

## PART C: Management

### Non-Surgical Management

**1. Conservative/Medical:**
- Alkaline nasal douches
- Saline sprays
- Petroleum-based ointments (twice daily)
- Sesame seed oil (longer duration of effect than saline)
- Antiseptic silicone barrier creams over 6–8 weeks
- Effectiveness usually apparent within 6–8 weeks

**2. Obturation (Septal Buttons):**

| Advantage | Limitation |
|-----------|-----------|
| Controls whistling and epistaxis | Does not resolve crusting/discomfort |
| Non-surgical option | 27.4% extrude or require removal |
| Good results: 72.8% remain in place | Bulk effect may increase blockage at nasal valve |
| | Granuloma formation from mucosal irritation |

Silastic obturators cover the inflamed mucosal margin, preventing drying and encouraging epithelialization. Brain reported good results in 44.4% of medium holes and 32.4% of large holes (≥1 cm).

### Surgical Repair

**Indications:** Symptomatic perforations (crusting, epistaxis, whistling) not controlled by conservative measures; patient willing to accept surgical risks.

### Classification of Repair Techniques

| Type | Techniques |
|------|-----------|
| Free grafts | Simple/composite autografts, allografts (acellular dermal allografts) |
| [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|Pedicled flap]]s | Local nasal mucosal, buccal mucosal, composite septal cartilage + mucosa, composite skin/cartilage |
| Rotation/advancement | Mucoperichondrial or mucoperiosteal flap mobilization |

### Two Key Factors for Successful Closure (80–90% rates)

1. **Sufficient residual mucosa** that can be mobilized and transposed allowing direct suturing of the mucosal defect
2. **Connective tissue interposition graft** (temporalis fascia, mastoid periosteum, cranial periosteum, cartilage, perichondrium) to support the repair

### Surgical Approaches

**1. Endonasal approach:**
- Limited by nostril size and hole position
- Good for small holes (up to 0.5 cm)
- Hemitransfixion incision with bipedicled flaps preserving anterior and posterior blood supply
- Horizontal relieving incisions + interposition graft

**2. Endoscopic approach:**
- Anterior pedicled turbinate flap reflected forward and sutured to perforation margins
- Unilateral septal flap based on anterior ethmoid artery vessels including mucoperiosteum from nasal floor
- Opposite side epithelializes over weeks with minimal valve disturbance

**3. External rhinoplasty approach:**
- Trans-columella approach or columella-philtrum incision
- Excellent exposure of septum and lower dorsum
- Useful when concomitant rhinoplasty or additional grafting is needed
- Allows larger flap design and easier suturing
- May require second minor procedure to divide pedicle at ~3 months

**4. Mid-face degloving approach:**
- Reserved for very large holes (>2 cm)
- Extensive dissection; offers little benefit over external approach for closure
- Higher complication rate — vestibular stenosis in 20%
- Tissue expansion (1 × 3 cm expander over 6–8 weeks) can generate more lining
- Reported success: 18/22 patients with holes 2–4 cm diameter

### Perforation Size and Approach

| Size | Recommended Approach |
|------|---------------------|
| Small (<0.5 cm) | Endonasal with bipedicled flaps + graft |
| Small-medium (0.5–2 cm) | Endoscopic or external rhinoplasty; posteriorly pedicled rotation/transposition flaps |
| Large (>2 cm) | External rhinoplasty with extensive undermining; consider tissue expansion or mid-face degloving |
| Very large (>2.5 cm) | Technical challenge; extensive flaps along nasal floor and under upper lateral cartilage; success rates lower |

### Graft Materials

- **Temporalis/temporoparietal fascia** — at least two layers; preferred by authors; current success rate 87%
- **Auricular cartilage** — useful but curved; risk of valve obstruction anterosuperiorly
- **Acellular dermal allografts** — similar success to fascia; expensive; avoids donor site morbidity
- **Composite grafts** — fascia + cartilage sandwich when little supporting quadrilateral cartilage remains

### Post-operative Care

- Antibiotic prophylaxis (10-day course + topical antibacterial ointment)
- Crucial to prevent sepsis — infection can cause saddle deformity from cartilage destruction
- Splinting unnecessary in majority of cases (increased perforation incidence with splints)

## PART D: Prevention of Septal Perforations

1. **During septal surgery:** Meticulous mucoperichondrial flap elevation in correct plane; start on easier (concave) side; avoid overlapping bilateral tears; use autograft cartilage/ethmoid plate to support tears
2. **Repair mucosal tears:** Excess mucosa from spur removal can be repaired with absorbable sutures
3. **Quilting sutures:** Tie loosely to allow for post-operative oedema — tight sutures cause ischaemic necrosis
4. **Splints:** Unnecessary in routine septal surgery — associated with higher perforation incidence
5. **Steroid spray technique:** Use opposite hand to nostril being treated to minimize septal impact; withdraw medication if ulceration develops
6. **Septal ulceration:** Treat by removing source of irritation + promoting healing (eradicate pathogens, avoid aggressive cleaning, use mucosal protectants)

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Septal perforation is a through-and-through defect, most commonly involving the anterior quadrilateral cartilage; >62% are asymptomatic.
> 2. Trauma (including prior surgery) is the most common cause in the UK; the "pick ulcer" from digital trauma is a frequent mechanism.
> 3. Principal symptoms are crusting, epistaxis and whistling — anterior perforations are more symptomatic than posterior ones.
> 4. Investigation should include C-ANCA, treponemal tests, ACE, FBC/ESR; biopsy only if unexplained aetiology with active inflammation or irregular margins.
> 5. Non-surgical management (saline douches, petroleum jelly, sesame seed oil) should be tried first for symptomatic perforations.
> 6. Septal obturators control whistling and epistaxis in ~73% but crusting and discomfort often persist.
> 7. Successful surgical repair (80–90%) depends on: (a) sufficient mobilizable residual mucosa and (b) connective tissue interposition graft.
> 8. Temporalis fascia (≥2 layers) as interposition graft achieves 87% success rate in experienced hands.
> 9. Small to medium holes can be closed reliably by surgery; very large perforations (>2.5 cm) remain a technical challenge.
> 10. Prevention during septal surgery is key — meticulous flap elevation, avoid bilateral mucosal tears, loose quilting sutures, avoid splints.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **60% of perforation repairs at the Royal National Throat, Nose and Ear Hospital had a previous history of septal surgery** — emphasizing the importance of prevention.
> - **Prevalence after SMR (17–25%) is higher than after septoplasty (1.4–5%)** — the more conservative septoplasty is the safer approach.
> - **Biopsy may convert an inactive perforation to an active state** causing enlargement — avoid routine biopsy of healed, stable, asymptomatic perforations.
> - **All steroid sprays can cause caudal septal inflammation** — patients should use the spray with the opposite hand to the nostril being treated.
> - **Acellular dermal allografts give similar success to fascia** but are expensive; the repair with fascia is entirely the patient's own tissue.
> - **Cocaine is an increasingly common cause** of septal necrosis — aggressive nasal self-toilet converts inflamed mucosa to ischaemic crusting, ulceration, cartilage exposure and perforation.
