# Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma (JNA)

> [!question] Questions
> Discuss the etiopathogenesis, theories of origin, investigations and management of juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 113 (Juvenile Angiofibroma) & Volume 2, Chapter 22 ([[Epistaxis Management|Epistaxis]])

---

## INTRODUCTION

Juvenile angiofibroma (JA) is a unique tumour presenting almost exclusively in adolescent males. The tumour is rare, with an incidence of 0.4 cases per 1 million inhabitants per year in Denmark, with higher incidence reported in Southern Europe and Asia. Although histopathologically classified as benign, bony erosions of the pterygoid, clivus and sphenoid sinus floor are common hallmarks. The tumour arises from the lateral wall of the posterior nasal cavity close to the sphenopalatine foramen and the pterygoid base, and its early submucosal expansion toward the nasopharynx earned the term "nasopharyngeal angiofibroma."

---

## PART A: ETIOPATHOGENESIS

### Tissue Architecture

JAs are benign fibrovascular tumours with characteristic irregularly-shaped vessels:

- Vessels may be lined by endothelial cells only, or show an incomplete vascular wall architecture
- These non-contractile pathological vessels cause **profuse bleeding** when disrupted during dissection
- Extreme variability exists in fibrous tissue and vessel density within JAs
- **Highest vessel density** (particularly irregularly-shaped vessels) is found below a pseudocapsule at the tumour surface
- Toward the tumour centre: increased fibrous tissue, decreased vessel density
- Based on clinical findings and specific vascular architecture, JAs have been defined as **vascular malformations** rather than true neoplasms
- Recent genetic and molecular findings (genome-wide copy number profiling) support this classification

### First Branchial Arch Artery Theory

The most widely accepted theory proposes that the tumour originates from an **incomplete regression of the first branchial arch artery** during embryological development:

| Observation | Explanation by Theory |
|------------|----------------------|
| Tumour origin at sphenopalatine foramen/pterygoid base | First branchial arch artery recedes close to these regions during development |
| Blood supply from sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries | Vascular remnants are incorporated into these arteries |
| ICA blood supply despite anatomical distance | First branchial arch artery connects to C4-segment of ICA |
| Residual tumour at pterygoid base and clivus | Site of origin of vascular remnants; necessitates bone drilling |

### Hormonal Factors

- Tumour occurs almost exclusively in **adolescent males** — hormonal sensitivity is strongly implicated
- Higher incidence during puberty supports role of sex hormones in tumour growth
- Androgen and oestrogen receptors have been identified in tumour tissue

## PART B: CLINICAL FINDINGS

### Presenting Features

- **Nasal obstruction** (usually unilateral, progressive)
- **Epistaxis** (unilateral, recurrent — a red flag in adolescent males)
- Mucopurulent or bloody nasal discharge
- Eustachian tube dysfunction (unilateral middle ear effusion)
- Facial swelling (lateral extension beyond maxillary sinus)
- **Proptosis and visual disturbance** (orbital extension via inferior orbital fissure)
- Pain and cranial neuropathies (advanced disease)

### Patterns of Spread

JAs spread along natural foramina and fissures:

| Direction | Pathway | Structures Involved |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|
| Lateral | Sphenopalatine foramen | Pterygopalatine fossa → Infratemporal fossa |
| Posterior | Erosion of clivus/pterygoid base | Sphenoid sinus, skull base |
| Superior | Inferior orbital fissure | Orbit (proptosis, optic nerve compression) |
| Intracranial | Roof of infratemporal fossa/superior orbital fissure | Mainly extradural; dural/brain infiltration is rare |

**Characteristic radiological findings:**
- **Widened sphenopalatine foramen**
- **Anterior displacement of posterior maxillary sinus wall** (Holman-Miller sign)
- Bony erosion of pterygoid plates, clivus, sphenoid floor

### Vascular Supply

| Tumour Stage | Arterial Supply |
|-------------|----------------|
| Early stages | Sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries (external carotid branches) |
| Advanced stages | Additional ECA branches (ascending pharyngeal artery) |
| Large tumours | Internal carotid arteries and vertebral arteries may contribute |

---

## PART C: INVESTIGATIONS

### Imaging

- **MRI with contrast (gadolinium):** Gold standard for tumour mapping; demonstrates tumour extent, relationship to neurovascular structures, flow voids within the vascular tumour
- **CT scanning:** Bony detail; demonstrates erosion of pterygoid plates, clivus and sinus walls; widened sphenopalatine foramen
- **Digital subtraction angiography (DSA):** Demonstrates vascular supply; allows identification of all feeding vessels from ipsilateral and contralateral ECA, ICA and vertebral arteries; essential for pre-operative planning

### Key Principles

- **Tumour biopsy should be AVOIDED** due to risk of severe, life-threatening bleeding
- Diagnosis is based entirely on **typical clinical and radiological findings**
- Angiographic evaluation should include: ipsilateral and contralateral ECAs and ICAs; both vertebral arteries should be examined in advanced tumour stages
- Pre-operative superselective embolization recommended by most authors

### Pre-operative Embolization

- Non-encapsulated fibrovascular tumours with vascular spaces lacking muscular layers — result in severe bleeding without muscular compression
- Average intra-operative blood loss historically ~2000 mL; reduced to **<1000 mL** with routine embolization
- Angiographic assessment defines blood supply, vascular composition, venous drainage
- Distal internal maxillary artery (sphenopalatine and pterygovaginal branches) supplies the tumour
- Both transarterial embolization and direct percutaneous embolization (with Onyx) have been studied
- Direct percutaneous Onyx embolization showed significantly lower intra-operative blood loss
- Some surgeons prefer not to embolize, instead closing feeding vessels intra-operatively and using tumour bleeding to detect remnants

## PART D: MANAGEMENT

### Surgical Treatment

Tumour resection is the therapy of choice. Various surgical approaches have been described.

### Surgical Approaches

| Approach | Access | Indication |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| Endoscopic endonasal | Via nostrils as natural openings | Majority of JAs (nasal, nasopharyngeal, sphenoid, PPF) |
| Midfacial degloving | Wide anterior access to maxilla and nose | Moderate-sized tumours; no external incision |
| Lateral rhinotomy | External skin incision with wide access | Larger tumours with infratemporal extension |
| Infratemporal approach | Lateral skull base access | Advanced tumours with significant lateral/intracranial extension |

### Endoscopic Surgical Technique

**Endoscopic resection has become the preferred approach** in the hands of experienced sinonasal surgeons for most JAs. The strategy follows these steps:

1. **Opening the maxillary sinus** and exposure of its posterior wall performed early during surgery; size of tumour dictates size of medial maxillectomy
2. **Posterior maxillary wall removed** with through-cutting punches or drills, avoiding trauma to the periosteum in the pterygopalatine fossa
3. **Main feeding vessel control:** Following periosteum incision, sphenopalatine and maxillary arteries are identified and clipped or coagulated
4. **Posterior nasal septum resection** to widen access and define tumour margins
5. **Anterior sphenoid sinus wall removed** to expose tumour within sphenoid sinus (bilateral opening usually necessary)
6. **Tumour dissected off pterygoid base**, pterygoid canal regions and clivus; venous bleeding from bone managed with drilling, chemical haemostatic agents or fine endosurgical diathermy
7. **Drilling of pterygoid base and clivus** at end of surgery — of utmost importance to avoid residual tumour at the site of origin
8. **Endoscopic dissection follows pseudocapsule** — avoid injury to tumour surface as this causes severe bleeding due to high-density irregularly-shaped vessels at the surface
9. **Finger-like tumour extensions** can be gently pulled out along the pseudocapsule

### Indications and Limitations of Endoscopic Surgery

**Suitable for endoscopic resection:**
- JAs limited to nasal cavity, nasopharynx, sphenoid sinus, pterygopalatine fossa
- With increasing experience: JAs extending into infratemporal fossa and space posterior to pterygoid plate
- Tumour tissue in orbit and parasellar region (primary surgery, experienced surgeons)

**NOT recommended endoscopically:**
- Broad skull base infiltration
- Extensive blood supply from internal carotid artery
- Encasement of the internal carotid artery
- Brain infiltration

### Residual Tumour

- Surgeon may be forced to leave residual tumour around optic nerve, cavernous sinus, ICA and dura — **regardless of surgical approach selected**
- Residual tumour does not necessarily expand on serial MR imaging
- Revision surgery for residual tumour requires careful consideration — residual tumour may be firmly adherent by fibrosis to neurovascular structures
- Careful radiological follow-up of residual tumour is essential

### Adjuvant Treatment

- **[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]]:** Reserved for cases with intracranial extension, recurrent disease, or residual tumour in surgically inaccessible areas; significant long-term morbidity (growth disturbance, secondary malignancy)
- **Chemotherapy:** Rarely used; reserved for advanced, unresectable tumours

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. JA presents almost exclusively in adolescent males; incidence 0.4 per million per year; origin close to sphenopalatine foramen and pterygoid base
> 2. First branchial arch artery theory: incomplete regression of embryological vessel explains tumour origin, blood supply pattern and residual tumour at pterygoid base
> 3. JAs are vascular malformations rather than true neoplasms; irregularly-shaped non-contractile vessels cause profuse bleeding
> 4. Tumour biopsy must be AVOIDED — diagnosis is entirely clinical and radiological
> 5. Widened sphenopalatine foramen and anterior displacement of posterior maxillary wall (Holman-Miller sign) are pathognomonic radiological findings
> 6. Pre-operative embolization reduces blood loss from ~2000 mL to <1000 mL; DSA of both ECAs and ICAs mandatory
> 7. Endoscopic resection is preferred approach for most JAs in experienced hands; dissection along pseudocapsule is critical
> 8. Drilling of pterygoid base and clivus at end of surgery is of utmost importance to avoid residual tumour at the site of origin
> 9. Highest vessel density is at the tumour surface — avoid entering the tumour surface during dissection
> 10. Endoscopic approach NOT recommended for broad skull base infiltration, ICA encasement, or brain infiltration


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A cell saver can be used during surgery to avoid allogenic blood donation — some surgeons prefer this over embolization to detect remnants by bleeding
> - Residual tumour does not necessarily expand on serial MRI — not all residual disease requires re-operation
> - Finger-like tumour extensions in the infratemporal fossa can be gently pushed medially with a finger placed externally at the lateral border of the maxillary sinus below the jaw
> - In revision surgery, residual tumour may be firmly adherent to neurovascular structures by fibrosis — limitations are different from primary surgery
> - Unilateral recurrent epistaxis in an adolescent male is a red flag — always consider JNA before any intranasal biopsy
