# Le Fort Fractures of the Maxilla

**Question:** *Describe the Le Fort classification of midfacial fractures, their clinical features, and management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 107 (Nasal and Facial Fractures)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Fractures of the midfacial skeleton can be subdivided into lateral (zygomatic) or central (maxillary, nasal, naso-orbito-ethmoid) fractures. The bone of the midfacial region is generally very thin and offers little resistance to anterior and lateral forces. Le Fort described three levels of midfacial fracture based on experimental studies. While the Le Fort classification is attractive and accurate for low-energy injuries, in high-energy injuries there are very few instances of pure Le Fort fractures and most configurations involve multiple Le Fort levels, together with zygomatic and nasal/naso-orbito-ethmoid components.

---

## PART A: LE FORT CLASSIFICATION

| Feature | Le Fort 1 | Le Fort 2 | Le Fort 3 |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Also known as | Guerin's fracture | Pyramidal fracture | Craniofacial disjunction |
| Level | Low (dentoalveolar) | Middle (subzygomatic) | High (suprazygomatic) |
| Orbit involvement | No | Yes (floor and medial wall) | Yes (medial and lateral walls) |
| Mobile segment | Maxillary alveolus | Central midface pyramid | Entire facial skeleton |

### Le Fort 1

The fracture runs above the floor of the nasal cavity, through the nasal septum, maxillary sinuses and inferior parts of the medial and lateral pterygoid plates. This is a horizontal fracture separating the dentoalveolar portion of the maxilla from the upper midfacial skeleton. The palate and teeth are mobile as a single unit.

### Le Fort 2

This is a pyramidal fracture which runs from the floor of the maxillary sinuses superiorly to the infraorbital margin and through the zygomaticomaxillary suture. Within the orbit, it passes across the lacrimal bone to the nasion. The infraorbital nerve is often damaged by involvement in this fracture. The central midface is mobile while the zygomatic complexes remain attached to the cranium.

### Le Fort 3

This represents a complete disconnection of the facial skeleton from the cranial base. The fracture traverses the medial wall of the orbit to the superior orbital fissure and exits across the greater wing of the sphenoid and zygomatic bone to the zygomaticofrontal suture. Posteriorly, the fracture line runs inferior to the optic foramen, across the lesser wing of the sphenoid to the pterygomaxillary fissure and sphenopalatine foramen. The arch of the zygoma is also broken. The entire face is mobile relative to the cranium.

---

## PART B: CLINICAL FEATURES

### General Signs and Symptoms

The classical features of a midfacial fracture are:

- Circum-orbital ecchymosis (panda facies)
- Facial oedema
- Surgical emphysema
- Lengthening of the face
- Anterior open bite
- Infraorbital anaesthesia (more common in higher fractures)
- Bruising at the junction of the hard and soft palate
- [[Epistaxis Management|Epistaxis]]

Not all signs need be present. The symptoms and signs depend on the level of the fracture.

### Signs by Le Fort Level

**Le Fort 1:**
- Mobile dentoalveolar segment
- Deranged dental occlusion
- Bilateral epistaxis
- Bruising of the upper buccal sulcus

**Le Fort 2:**
- Pyramidal midface mobility
- Infraorbital nerve anaesthesia (numbness of cheek, upper lip, teeth)
- Panda facies (bilateral periorbital ecchymosis)
- Subconjunctival haemorrhage
- Step deformity at infraorbital margin
- Dish-face deformity
- [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]] (if cribriform plate involved)

**Le Fort 3:**
- Complete craniofacial separation with gross facial mobility
- Bilateral panda facies
- Significant facial elongation
- Massive epistaxis
- CSF rhinorrhoea (high risk)
- Diplopia
- Enophthalmos
- Telecanthus (if NOE component present)

## PART C: ASSOCIATED INJURIES

### Naso-Orbito-Ethmoid (NOE) Fractures

NOE fractures are the most severe nasal injuries encountered and usually result from high velocity trauma. They often have associated maxillary fractures. The external buttresses of the nose give way and the ethmoid labyrinth collapses on itself. This causes the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid to rotate and the quadrilateral cartilage to fall backwards, resulting in a classic foreshortened saddled nose with telecanthus. Disruption of the medial canthal ligament from the crest of the lacrimal bone exaggerates the telecanthus.

Two categories recognized by Raveh:

- **Type I:** Anterior skull base, posterior wall of [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] and optic canal remain intact
- **Type II:** Disruption of the posterior wall of the frontal sinus with potential intracranial extension

### Facial Buttresses

Thickened areas of bone between the maxilla and skull base act as buttresses and provide a strategy for reconstruction. These are key areas for osteosynthesis. The main buttresses include:

- Nasomaxillary (medial vertical) buttress
- Zygomaticomaxillary (lateral vertical) buttress
- Frontal bar (superior horizontal) buttress
- Maxillary alveolus (inferior horizontal) buttress

---

## PART D: INVESTIGATIONS

- **CT scanning:** Standard of care; 3D CT reconstruction demonstrates vertical, horizontal and transverse disruption to the craniofacial skeleton; essential for surgical planning
- **Occipitomental radiographs:** 15° and 30° views (largely superseded by CT)
- **Dental assessment:** Occlusion, loose teeth, arch integrity
- **Ophthalmological assessment:** Visual acuity, eye movements, pupil reactions
- **CSF leak assessment:** If high-level fractures (Le Fort 2/3) suspected

---

## PART E: MANAGEMENT

### Emergency Treatment

Midfacial fractures can easily compromise the airway with torrential epistaxis and posterior impaction of the maxilla. Emergency management:

- **Airway:** Secure the airway; may require intubation or emergency [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] if severe posterior displacement
- **Bleeding control:** Epistaxis arrested using epistats or anterior and posterior nasal packs
- **Maxillary disimpaction:** If retroposition of the maxilla is a problem, it can be pulled forwards using the index and middle finger placed behind the patient's soft palate

### Reduction

The maxilla is mobilized by a combination of digital pressure and traction on arch bars or interdental wires. The position of the maxilla is assessed with reference to the bony buttresses and dental occlusion. This can be difficult when the bone fragments are comminuted or the dentition is mutilated. Rowe maxillary disimpaction forceps can prove invaluable if the maxilla is impacted.

### Fixation

Modern management of the fractured maxilla parallels that of the mandible. Internal fixation with 1.3 or 1.5 mm low-profile miniplates placed along the buttresses provides very satisfactory stabilization of the fractured segments. Access to fractures of the anterior and lateral maxilla is achieved through a gingivobuccal incision ensuring an adequate cuff of unattached mucosa to isolate the plates from the mouth at closure. The posterior limit of the incision should be no further than the first permanent molar to preserve good blood supply. Subperiosteal elevation with preservation of the infraorbital nerve allows reconstruction of the paranasal and zygomatic buttresses.

**Specific fixation considerations:**

- **Le Fort 1:** Fixation at the pyriform aperture and zygomaticomaxillary buttress bilaterally
- **Le Fort 2:** Infraorbital rim needs to be reduced and fixed in addition to the buttresses; orbital floor may require reconstruction
- **Le Fort 3:** Requires fixation at the zygomaticofrontal suture, infraorbital rim, and zygomaticomaxillary buttress; often requires coronal flap approach

**Orbital access incisions:**

| Incision | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|----------|-----------|--------------|
| Transconjunctival | Good exposure, aesthetic | Slight risk of entropion |
| Transconjunctival with cantholysis | Excellent exposure | Risk of lid malposition |
| Lower eyelid | Straightforward | Risk of ectropion, visible scar |
| Infraorbital | Rapid, minimal scleral show | Poor cosmesis |

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Le Fort 1: horizontal fracture above nasal floor through nasal septum, maxillary sinuses and pterygoid plates
> 2. Le Fort 2: pyramidal fracture from maxillary sinus floor to infraorbital margin through orbit to nasion; infraorbital nerve commonly damaged
> 3. Le Fort 3: craniofacial disjunction through medial orbit, superior orbital fissure, greater wing of sphenoid, zygomaticofrontal suture and zygomatic arch
> 4. Classical features: panda facies, facial oedema, emphysema, facial lengthening, anterior open bite
> 5. Pure Le Fort fractures are rare in high-energy injuries; most involve multiple Le Fort levels with zygomatic and NOE components
> 6. Facial buttresses are key areas for osteosynthesis - thickened areas of bone providing a strategy for reconstruction
> 7. Emergency management: secure airway, control epistaxis, disimpact maxilla manually if causing airway obstruction
> 8. Internal fixation with 1.3-1.5 mm low-profile miniplates along buttresses has replaced external fixation for most maxillary fractures
> 9. Gingivobuccal incision posterior limit should not extend beyond the first permanent molar to preserve blood supply
> 10. NOE fractures cause telecanthus from medial canthal ligament disruption and collapse of the ethmoid labyrinth


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - In high-energy injuries, always suspect multiple Le Fort levels and associated zygomatic/NOE fractures
> - Bruising at the hard-soft palate junction is a reliable clinical indicator of maxillary fracture
> - Rowe maxillary disimpaction forceps are invaluable for reducing impacted maxillary fractures
> - The buccal fat pad can be avoided during gingivobuccal incision by angling the blade at 45 degrees to the gingival cuff
> - CSF rhinorrhoea must be excluded in Le Fort 2 and 3 fractures before nasal packing
> - 3D CT reconstruction is essential for surgical planning of complex multilevel fractures
