# Maxillofacial Trauma — Fractures, TON, and Osteotomies in ENT

> [!question] Questions
> "Classify [[Le Fort Fractures|Le Fort]] fractures and discuss traumatic optic neuropathy. Describe zygomatic fracture features and management. Discuss mandibular condyle fracture mechanisms and management. Classify nasal fractures and management. Enumerate various osteotomies done in ENT practice."

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapters 7 (Maxillary Malignancy — osteotomies), 57 (TMJ Disorders), 80 (Rhinoplasty Following Nasal Trauma), 84 (Osteotomies); Volume 1, Chapter 98 ([[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]] Complications), 107 (Nasal and Facial Fractures)

## Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma encompasses a wide spectrum of injuries from simple nasal fractures to complex panfacial fractures. Nasal fractures are the most common facial fractures, accounting for 40% of all facial fractures. The intimate relationship between the facial skeleton and critical structures — the orbit, cranial nerves, and brain — means that maxillofacial trauma can have serious ophthalmic and neurological consequences. The otorhinolaryngologist must be familiar with fracture classification, management principles, and the various osteotomies employed across ENT surgical practice.

---

## Part A — Le Fort Fracture Classification

René Le Fort described three patterns of midfacial fracture based on cadaveric experiments:

| Type | Fracture Line | Key Features |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| Le Fort I | Horizontal above alveolus | Palatal mobility, floating palate |
| Le Fort II | Pyramidal, through infraorbital rim | Dish-face, [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]] |
| Le Fort III | Craniofacial disjunction | Complete separation midface from skull base |

- **Le Fort I (Guerin):** Horizontal fracture through maxilla above teeth apices, crossing the piriform aperture, lateral maxillary wall, and pterygoid plates. Results in a mobile palate ("floating palate")
- **Le Fort II (Pyramidal):** Fracture through nasion, medial orbital wall, orbital floor, infraorbital rim, and pterygoid plates. Produces the classic "dish-face" deformity with infraorbital nerve anaesthesia
- **Le Fort III (Craniofacial disjunction):** Through nasofrontal suture, medial and lateral orbital walls, zygomatic arch, and pterygoid plates. Complete separation of the midface from the skull base

**Note:** In clinical practice, fractures are often asymmetric and combinations of Le Fort patterns are common. Management involves intermaxillary fixation (IMF) and open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) with miniplates.

## Part B — Traumatic Optic Neuropathy (TON)

### Definition and Mechanism

Traumatic optic neuropathy is an indirect injury to the optic nerve resulting from transmitted force during facial or cranial trauma. The optic nerve is most vulnerable within the **optic canal** where it is tethered to the periosteum of the bony canal. Mechanisms include:

- **Shearing forces** transmitted through the skull base to the optic canal
- **Oedema** within the confined bony canal causing compression
- **Haemorrhage** within the nerve sheath or surrounding canal
- **Bone fragments** from fractures of the optic canal (less common)

### Clinical Evaluation

- **Visual acuity:** Ranges from decreased VA to no light perception
- **RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil):** Most important clinical sign — indicates unilateral optic nerve dysfunction
- **Colour vision:** Impaired (especially red desaturation)
- **Visual field defects:** Variable patterns
- **CT scan (thin cuts):** Optic canal fractures, retrobulbar haemorrhage, bone fragment impingement
- **MRI:** Optic nerve signal changes, haemorrhage

### Management

Management remains controversial with no universally accepted protocol:

- **Observation:** Some cases recover spontaneously, especially partial injuries
- **High-dose IV methylprednisolone:** 1 g/day for 3 days (based on CRASH trial, use is declining; the IONTS showed no benefit over observation)
- **Optic nerve decompression:** Endoscopic endonasal approach through the sphenoid sinus to decompress the optic canal — reserved for cases with no improvement on steroids, progressive visual loss, or identifiable compression
- **External decompression:** Transcranial approach — now largely replaced by endoscopic technique
- **Timing:** Best results if decompression performed within 7 days of injury

## Part C — Zygomatic Complex (ZMC) Fractures

### Anatomy and Classification

The zygoma articulates at four buttresses — frontozygomatic suture, infraorbital rim (zygomaticomaxillary), zygomatic arch (zygomaticotemporal), and zygomaticomaxillary buttress. Fracture of all four creates the classic "tripod" or "tetrapod" fracture (ZMC fracture).

**Knight and North Classification:**
- Group I: No significant displacement (stable)
- Group II: Zygomatic arch fracture (isolated)
- Group III: Unrotated body fracture (downward/inward displacement)
- Group IV: Medially rotated body fracture
- Group V: Laterally rotated body fracture
- Group VI: Complex/comminuted

### Clinical Features

- **Malar flattening** — loss of cheek prominence (best seen from above/behind patient)
- **Periorbital ecchymosis** — raccoon eyes
- **Infraorbital nerve anaesthesia** — numbness of cheek, upper lip, teeth
- **Trismus** — impingement of depressed zygomatic arch on coronoid process of mandible
- **Diplopia** — from orbital floor involvement or extraocular muscle entrapment
- **Subconjunctival haemorrhage** — without posterior limit
- **Step deformity** — palpable at infraorbital rim or frontozygomatic suture
- **Enophthalmos** — late finding from increased orbital volume

### Management

- **Non-displaced (Knight I):** Conservative — observation, soft diet, avoid nose blowing
- **Gillies temporal approach:** Temporal incision, elevator placed beneath the temporalis fascia deep to the zygomatic arch to lift the depressed fragment
- **Keen's intraoral approach:** Sublabial incision for direct access to anterior maxillary wall
- **Direct ORIF with miniplates:** At frontozygomatic suture and infraorbital rim — gold standard for displaced fractures, provides stable three-point fixation
- **Orbital floor reconstruction:** Titanium mesh or resorbable plates if significant floor defect with entrapment or enophthalmos

## Part D — Mandibular Condylar Fractures

### Classification

- **Intracapsular:** Within the joint capsule (more common in children)
- **Extracapsular (condylar neck):** Below the capsule but above the sigmoid notch
- **Subcondylar:** Below the sigmoid notch (most common in adults)

### Mechanism

Condylar fractures occur in approximately **30% of mandibular fractures**. The mechanism is typically a blow to the chin with force transmitted along the mandible to the condyle (indirect fracture). They may be unilateral or bilateral and can be associated with fractures elsewhere on the mandible.

### Clinical Features

- **Pre-auricular pain and swelling** over the TMJ
- **Deviation of the mandible to the affected side** on opening
- **Malocclusion:** Contralateral open bite (displaced condyle); ipsilateral open bite (haemarthrosis)
- **Limited mouth opening** (trismus)
- **Haemotympanum** — if fracture involves temporal bone
- **Bleeding from the external auditory canal** — if EAC perforated

### Management

**Children:**
- **Conservative** treatment preferred — mobilization and analgesia
- IMF with elastics for 2–4 weeks if occlusion deranged
- Avoid prolonged IMF due to risk of ankylosis
- Long-term monitoring for growth abnormalities (undergrowth, overgrowth, or ankylosis)

**Adults:**
- **Conservative (closed reduction):** IMF for non-displaced or minimally displaced fractures with normal occlusion; soft diet and physiotherapy
- **Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF):** Indicated for dislocation into the middle cranial fossa, lateral extracapsular displacement, bilateral fractures with anterior open bite, foreign body in joint
- **Arthrocentesis** for effusion or haemarthrosis

## Part E — Nasal Fracture Classification and Management

### Classification (Stranc and Robertson)

Based on the direction of the caustic force:

**Lateral force injuries:**
- Type 1: Ipsilateral nasal bone depression without septal injury
- Type 2: Contralateral nasal bone out-fracture with C-shaped septal deviation

**Frontal force injuries:**
- Type 1: Splaying of nasal bones with septal buckling
- Type 2: Comminution with saddle deformity (severe)

**Murray and Maran Modification:** Adds assessment of septal injury severity and soft-tissue involvement.

### Management by Type

| Severity | Management |
|----------|-----------|
| Undisplaced | Observation, analgesia, review |
| Simple displaced | Closed reduction (MUA) within 2–3 weeks |
| Complex with septal injury | Open reduction, septoplasty |
| Comminuted with saddle | Formal rhinoplasty (delayed 6 months) |
| Nasoethmoidal (NOE) | ORIF, medial canthal reattachment |

- **Closed reduction (MUA):** Window of 2–3 weeks (possibly earlier in children); Walsham forceps for septum, Ash forceps for bony pyramid
- **Four-step manoeuvre** for impacted deviated pyramid: exaggerate deformity → lift depressed bone → elevate central vault → push sidewalls inward
- **External splint** for 1 week; internal splint if septal disimpaction performed

## Part F — Osteotomies in ENT Practice

### Rhinoplasty Osteotomies

- **Lateral osteotomy:** Low-to-low (piriform to nasion) or low-to-high; narrows the bony vault; closes the open roof after hump reduction
- **Medial oblique osteotomy:** From the lateral edge of the nasal bone toward the midline; allows infracture
- **Intermediate osteotomy:** Between medial and lateral; for asymmetric bones or broad dorsum

### Maxillectomy Osteotomies

- **Medial maxillectomy:** Through ethmoid cells, frontal process of maxilla, piriform aperture; used for benign/selected malignant tumours
- **Total maxillectomy (classic):** Osteotomies through zygoma body, midline palate, pterygoid plates, frontal process of maxilla, and orbital floor
- **Le Fort I osteotomy:** For access to the clivus and central skull base (combined with midface degloving)

### Mandibular Osteotomies

- **Median/paramedian mandibulotomy:** Splits the mandible for access to oropharyngeal and parapharyngeal tumours; lip split required
- **Marginal mandibulectomy:** Rim resection preserving continuity; for tumours abutting mandible without cortical invasion
- **Segmental mandibulectomy:** For mandibular invasion; requires reconstruction (fibula [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]])

### Other ENT Osteotomies

- **Cortical [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]]:** Removal of cortical bone over the mastoid antrum
- **Frontal craniotomy:** For craniofacial resection of anterior skull base tumours
- **Laryngofissure/thyrotomy:** Midline split of thyroid cartilage; for cordectomy or access to endolarynx
- **Cricotracheal resection:** For [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]]

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Le Fort I = floating palate (horizontal fracture above alveolus); Le Fort II = pyramidal (dish-face); Le Fort III = craniofacial disjunction
> 2. Nasal fractures account for 40% of all facial fractures and are the most common facial fracture
> 3. Traumatic optic neuropathy is an indirect injury — RAPD is the most important clinical sign
> 4. ZMC fractures involve four buttresses — malar flattening and infraorbital nerve anaesthesia are characteristic
> 5. Condylar fractures occur in 30% of mandibular fractures — chin impact is the most common mechanism
> 6. In children, condylar fractures are intracapsular and conservative management is preferred to avoid growth disturbance
> 7. Closed reduction of nasal fractures should be performed within 2–3 weeks of injury
> 8. The four-step manoeuvre for impacted deviated pyramid: disimpact → lift opposite → elevate central vault → close sidewalls
> 9. Lateral osteotomy in rhinoplasty closes the open roof created by dorsal hump reduction
> 10. Total maxillectomy osteotomies involve the zygoma, palate, pterygoid plates, and frontal process of maxilla
> 11. Mandibulotomy (median/paramedian) provides access to oropharyngeal and parapharyngeal tumours
> 12. Optic nerve decompression for TON is best performed within 7 days via endoscopic endonasal approach


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Le Fort fractures are rarely symmetric** — combination patterns are the rule in clinical practice
> - **Always check infraorbital nerve sensation** in midface trauma — anaesthesia suggests Le Fort II or ZMC fracture
> - **Gillies temporal approach** is the simplest elevation technique for isolated zygomatic arch depression
> - **Bilateral condylar fractures** can present with anterior open bite and inability to close teeth — often need ORIF
> - **Never delay nasal fracture reduction** beyond 3 weeks — bony union makes closed reduction impossible
> - **Growth disturbance monitoring** is essential after condylar fractures in children — ankylosis, undergrowth, and overgrowth can all occur
