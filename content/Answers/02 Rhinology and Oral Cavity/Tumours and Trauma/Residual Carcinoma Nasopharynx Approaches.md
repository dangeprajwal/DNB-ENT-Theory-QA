# Approaches for Residual Carcinoma of Nasopharynx

**Question:** *Describe the approaches for residual/recurrent carcinoma of the nasopharynx.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 109 (Approaches to the Nasopharynx) & Volume 3, Chapter 8 ([[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma]])

---

## INTRODUCTION

Salvage treatment for nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is moderately successful but with significant morbidities. A large survey of 200 local failures treated with different modalities showed an overall 3-year survival of 74%. Since patients with recurrences have already been exposed to the toxicities of primary treatment (chemoradiation), the choice of salvage treatment must consider the tolerance of normal tissue. Disease should only be considered persistent if biopsy is positive at 12 weeks after [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]]. The nasopharynx is notoriously difficult to access surgically, and most routes require facial incisions, multiple osteotomies and transgress significant amounts of normal tissue. Nasopharyngectomy is therefore reserved for salvaging radiation failures.

---

## PART A: NON-SURGICAL SALVAGE OPTIONS

### Re-irradiation with External Beam

Traditional 2D re-irradiation for local failure had poor results with a 5-year survival of only 7.6%, limited by dosimetry constraints to avoid excessive radiation to vital organs (brainstem, optic chiasma, temporal lobe). With the development of 3D stereotactic radiotherapy and IMRT, dose to vital organs can be reduced. A series of 30 patients showed 5-year overall survival and local control using stereotactic radiotherapy of 40% and 57% respectively.

### Brachytherapy

Delivers high dose of radiation with limited penetration inside the nasopharynx for local failures without deep invasion. Two radioactive sources are used:

- **Iridium-192 (Ir-192):** Loaded into a tailor-made plastic mould fitted into the nasopharynx via the oral cavity under local anaesthesia
- **Gold-198 (Au-198):** Gold grains implanted to the nasopharynx after soft palate split under general anaesthesia

Both techniques are only suitable for tumours less than 2 cm in maximal dimension. 5-year survival rates in the range of 50-60%.

### Surgery vs Re-irradiation

There was no significant difference in overall survival between second course radiotherapy and surgery, but nasopharyngectomy was associated with lower morbidities. Only patients with rT1 and rT2 diseases had survival advantage; patients with rT3 and rT4 diseases showed no improvement with retreatment by either means.

## PART B: SURGICAL APPROACHES - CLASSIFICATION

The surgical approaches to the nasopharynx for residual/recurrent NPC can be classified by direction of access:

| Direction | Approach | Key Feature |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Inferior | Transpalatal | No facial incision |
| Inferolateral | Transcervico-mandibulo-palatal | Lip-split mandibulotomy |
| Anterior | Midfacial degloving | No facial scar |
| Anterolateral | Maxillary swing | Wide exposure, preferred |
| Anterolateral | Facial translocation | Maximum lateral exposure |
| Lateral | Infratemporal fossa Type C | Neurotological approach |
| Combined | Subtemporal-preauricular infratemporal | For T4 lesions |
| Endoscopic | Endoscopic endonasal approach (EEA) | Minimally invasive |
| Robotic | Transoral robotic surgery (TORS) | Evolving indications |

---

## PART C: DETAILED SURGICAL APPROACHES

### 1. Transpalatal Approach (Inferior)

**Indication:** Malignant tumours of the posterior nasopharyngeal wall that are no larger than 2 cm; access to tumour extension into the clivus and craniocervical junction.

**Technique:** Mucoperiosteal flap of the hard palate elevated anterior to posterior. Soft palate detached from the hard palate and retracted inferiorly to expose the nasopharynx. Posterior edge of the hard palate removed to increase exposure. Recurrent tumour excised under direct vision. Palatal flap re-sutured at conclusion. Can be extended through [[Le Fort Fractures|Le Fort]] I-palatal split (open door maxillotomy) for unrivalled exposure of the entire clivus and craniocervical junction.

**Outcomes:** Fee et al. reported 5-year overall survival of 52% and local control of 67% (37 patients).

**Advantages:** Minimal bone removal, no facial incision.

**Limitations:** Limited lateral access to [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]], risk of palatal fistula (30-40%), [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] requirement, narrow working field with long working distance.

### 2. Transcervico-Mandibulo-Palatal Approach (Inferolateral)

**Indication:** Nasopharyngeal tumours extending into the anterior infratemporal fossa and parapharyngeal space.

**Technique:** Extended submandibular incision from mastoid tip to lip split. Median mandibulotomy with pre-fitted mini-plates. Floor of mouth dissection towards parapharyngeal space. Mandible retracted laterally. External carotid artery ligated distal to lingual artery for additional retraction. Hemi-palatal flap created for oropharyngeal sleeve. Eustachian tube divided to release nasopharynx from skull base. Whole pharyngeal sleeve displaced to opposite side, exposing central skull base.

**Advantages:** Good access to epipharynx, parapharyngeal and anterior infratemporal space; direct approach; good vascular control.

**Limitations:** Potential infection through oral contamination; possible mandibular non-union; prolonged tracheostomy need; delayed swallowing recovery (mean 7 weeks); palatal defect (8/11 patients), trismus (6/11), dysphagia (6/11), mandibular malunion (3/11).

### 3. Midfacial Degloving Approach (Anterior)

**Indication:** Residual NPC accessible from the anterior direction.

**Technique:** Similar to midfacial degloving for nasal cavity cancers and [[Juvenile Nasopharyngeal Angiofibroma|juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma]]s. Bilateral medial maxillectomies and posterior nasal septectomy performed to increase exposure and room for instrument manipulation.

**Outcomes:** Clear margins achieved in 12/15 cases; long-term oncological outcome significantly worse than maxillary swing approach.

**Advantages:** No facial scar; avoids risk of palatal fistula.

**Limitations:** Exposure can be poor even with medial maxillary wall removal; limited lateral access; NPC frequently has lateral extension that cannot be addressed; inferior oncological outcomes compared to maxillary swing.

### 4. Maxillary Swing Approach (Anterolateral) - PREFERRED

**Indication:** Nasopharyngeal tumours with limited extension into the anterior infratemporal region.

**Contraindications:** Lesions involving petrous ICA; lesions extending to petrous apex, parasellar and/or posterior infratemporal region (combination with lateral approach required).

**Technique:** Weber-Ferguson-Longmire incision extending laterally onto the zygoma. Vertical incision through upper lip continued between central incisors onto hard palate. Osteotomies made at: anterior maxilla inferior to orbital rim; midline at hard palate lateral to nasal septum; anterior zygoma separating maxilla from zygoma; posterior maxillary wall from pterygoid plates at maxillary tuberosity. Entire maxilla swung laterally attached to cheek flap and masseter muscle, widely exposing the nasopharynx and parapharyngeal space. En bloc resection of confined pathologies is possible. Surgeon can localize parapharyngeal ICA by palpation during dissection. Maxilla replaced and fixed with titanium plates.

**Outcomes:** Wei reported 20-year experience in 246 patients: 5-year local control 74%, 5-year disease-free survival 56%.

**Advantages:** Simple technique; wide exposure of nasopharynx and parapharyngeal space; good cosmetic and functional results.

**Limitations:** Facial scar; risk of trismus; palatal fistula formation; limited parapharyngeal and infratemporal exposure compared to facial translocation; lack of vascular control; post-operative trismus; risk of necrosis of the maxilla.

### 5. Facial Translocation Approach (Anterolateral)

**Indication:** Tumours requiring wider lateral access than maxillary swing.

**Technique:** Entire maxilla including floor of orbit, lateral orbital rim, malar prominence and zygomatic arch is removed. Part of coronoid process also removed for infratemporal fossa and masticator space exposure. Maxilla detached from cheek flap and replaced as free bone graft, secured with titanium plates.

**Outcomes:** Hao et al. reported 5-year local control 53.6%, 5-year overall survival 48.7% (53 patients), comparable to maxillary swing.

**Advantages:** Better lateral exposure than maxillary swing.

**Limitations:** Jeopardizes blood supply to maxilla bone; lateral exposure usually not required for small recurrent NPC - maxillary swing should suffice for most cases.

### 6. Infratemporal Fossa Approach Type C (Lateral)

**Indication:** Infratemporal fossa tumours extending into the temporal bone with involvement of the epipharynx; nasopharyngeal carcinoma.

**Contraindications:** Advanced disease with extension into sella, contralateral middle fossa or anterior skull base.

**Technique:** Post-auricular C-shaped incision extending into temporal region and neck. External auditory canal closed as blind sac. Extended [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|mastoidectomy]] and total [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]] with facial nerve rerouting. Zygomatic arch removed; temporalis muscle divided. Subtotal petrosectomy skeletonizing sigmoid sinus, ICA and middle fossa dura. TMJ capsule exposed, articular disc excised, mandibular condyle displaced inferiorly. Pterygoid process removed with lateral and medial plates for access to nasopharynx.

**Advantages:** Wide access to infratemporal, parasellar and temporal region; direct approach; short working distance.

**Limitations:** Temporary post-operative trismus and malocclusion; V3 hypaesthesia; permanent conductive hearing loss; temporary frontal facial paresis in 30%.

### 7. Subtemporal-Preauricular Infratemporal Fossa Approach (Combined)

**Indication:** Large tumours (T4) of the infratemporal space extending into the nasopharynx, cavernous sinus and middle cranial fossa.

**Contraindications:** Bilateral ICA involvement; bilateral optic chiasm involvement; posterior cranial fossa extension.

**Technique:** Extended Blair incision with cervicofacial flap. Combines lateral skull base access with cervical approach.

### 8. Endoscopic Endonasal Approach (EEA)

The EEAs have revolutionized surgical treatment of midline lesions. Largely replacing open transoral and transpalatal procedures. Direct access and visualization with four-hands technique (two surgeons working simultaneously).

**Advantages:** Simple technique; direct approach; excellent visualization; no facial incision.

**Limitations:** Limited access to lateral infratemporal and parapharyngeal space; difficult margin assessment in malignant disease; dural reconstruction challenging; risk of meningitis from wound contamination.

### 9. Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS)

**Indications:** Benign tumours of the nasopharynx and parapharyngeal space; indications are evolving. Salvage surgery for small recurrences of NPC in the posterior nasopharyngeal wall with promising outcomes.

**Advantages:** 3D HD visualization; superior instrument dexterity and mobility in confined spaces; no facial incision.

**Limitations:** High set-up costs; utility and indications yet to be defined; current data limited to confined posterior wall lesions.

Tsang et al. reported 12 patients with robotic-assisted nasopharyngectomy: 2-year local control 86%, comparable to other endoscopic series.

## PART D: SELECTION CRITERIA FOR SALVAGE SURGERY

**Patient selection:**

- Patient must be fit for a 5-hour operation under general anaesthesia
- Tumours showing ICA encasement, extensive skull base infiltration or intracranial extension are NOT suitable for salvage surgery

**Approach selection based on tumour extent:**

| Tumour Location/Extent | Preferred Approach |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| Small posterior wall (less than 2 cm) | Brachytherapy or EEA or TORS |
| Central nasopharynx, limited lateral extension | Transpalatal or maxillary swing |
| Lateral extension to parapharyngeal space | Maxillary swing (preferred) |
| Extension to anterior infratemporal fossa | Transmandibular-transcervical or maxillary swing |
| Extension to infratemporal fossa and temporal bone | Infratemporal fossa Type C |
| T4 with cavernous sinus/middle cranial fossa | Subtemporal-preauricular combined |
| Clivus and craniocervical junction | Transpalatal with Le Fort I extension |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Salvage treatment for NPC: 3-year overall survival 74% (200 patients, all modalities)
> 2. Disease considered persistent only if biopsy positive at 12 weeks post-radiotherapy (not at 8 weeks)
> 3. Only rT1 and rT2 patients show survival advantage with retreatment; rT3/rT4 show no improvement
> 4. Nasopharyngectomy has comparable survival to re-irradiation but lower morbidities
> 5. Maxillary swing is the preferred approach: 5-year local control 74%, DFS 56% (246 patients, Wei series)
> 6. Brachytherapy (Ir-192, Au-198) suitable only for tumours less than 2 cm; 5-year survival 50-60%
> 7. Stereotactic re-irradiation: 5-year OS 40%, local control 57%
> 8. Midfacial degloving has significantly worse oncological outcomes than maxillary swing due to inability to address lateral disease
> 9. Transpalatal approach: 5-year OS 52%, local control 67% (Fee series); risk of palatal fistula 30-40%
> 10. EEA has largely replaced open transoral and transpalatal procedures for midline lesions
> 11. Robotic-assisted nasopharyngectomy: 2-year local control 86% (Tsang series, 12 patients)
> 12. ICA encasement, extensive skull base infiltration or intracranial extension are contraindications for salvage surgery


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always confirm disease persistence with biopsy at 12 weeks post-radiotherapy, not earlier (tumour cells at 8 weeks may still undergo complete regression)
> - The maxillary swing approach allows the surgeon to palpate the parapharyngeal ICA during dissection - a critical safety feature
> - NPC frequently has lateral extension - inability to address lateral disease with midfacial degloving contributes to its inferior outcomes
> - Facial translocation offers better lateral exposure than maxillary swing but at the cost of jeopardizing maxillary blood supply - usually unnecessary for most recurrent NPC
> - EEA limitations include difficulty with lateral parapharyngeal/infratemporal access and dural reconstruction challenges
> - Re-irradiation for isolated nodal failure is not recommended due to additional soft tissue damage; surgery is preferred
