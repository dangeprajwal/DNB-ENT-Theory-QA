## Part A: Biofilm — Definition, Stages and Role in ENT Infections

*Q23: Biofilm — definition, stages, role in ENT infections (2021 Jun)*

### Definition

A biofilm is an aggregate of microorganisms in which cells form a community within a slime-like extracellular matrix, adhering to a biological or non-biological surface. Biofilms are well known to form on non-biological surfaces immersed in liquid, such as intravascular devices immersed in the bloodstream, but may also exist on mucosal surfaces within the body, such as the tonsils, the mucosa of the sinuses and the middle ear. Like many other organisms, *Staphylococcus aureus* is a notable biofilm-former. Biofilms may involve more than one bacterial species.

### Stages of Biofilm Formation

The formation and maturation of a biofilm follows a well-defined sequence:

1. **Reversible attachment**: The planktonic (free-living) form of the organism initially attaches to a surface in a reversible manner. At this stage, the organisms can still be dislodged easily.

2. **Irreversible attachment**: As the biofilm develops, the attachment becomes irreversible. The bacterial cells begin to adhere more firmly to the surface and to each other.

3. **Extracellular matrix (slime) production**: The bacterial community becomes covered in an extracellular matrix slime. This slime acts as a protective barrier, shielding the organisms from the host immune response and from antimicrobial agents.

4. **Quorum sensing and maturation**: Within the matrix, the individual cells communicate with each other via a process called quorum sensing, which allows regulation of gene expression throughout the colony. This coordinated communication enables the biofilm to function as a community, regulating virulence factors, nutrient sharing and structural integrity.

5. **Dispersal**: A fully mature biofilm is able to release planktonic organisms back into the environment, which can then colonize new surfaces and restart the cycle. This dispersal phase is responsible for the recurrence and spread of biofilm-related infections.

### Treatment Challenges

Treatment of infections involving a biofilm is complicated by two major factors:

- **Extracellular slime barrier**: The slime matrix makes penetration of antimicrobials difficult. Antibiotics cannot easily reach the bacteria embedded within the biofilm.
- **Reduced metabolic activity**: The bacteria within the biofilm exhibit reduced metabolic activity and divide less frequently than their free-living planktonic forms. This renders many antimicrobials ineffective, particularly those that target actively dividing cells (e.g., beta-lactams).
- Agents that act on the ribosome (e.g., macrolides, aminoglycosides) may have some effectiveness against biofilm-associated bacteria, but in general, treatment requires physical disruption of the biofilm (surgery, debridement) combined with systemic and topical antimicrobials.

### Examples of Biofilms in Medicine (Table 19.5)

| Clinical Context | Details |
|---|---|
| Dental plaque | Classic biofilm example |
| Otitis media | Biofilm on middle ear mucosa |
| [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|Chronic rhinosinusitis]] | Biofilm in sinus mucosa |
| Chronic respiratory disease | Lung colonization (e.g., cystic fibrosis) |
| Chronic wounds | Colonization preventing healing |
| Osteomyelitis | Bone-associated biofilms |
| Intestinal microbiome | Normal and pathological |
| Endocarditis | Vegetation as biofilm |
| Device-related infections | Intravascular devices, implants |

### Role of Biofilms in ENT Infections

In ENT practice, biofilms are increasingly recognized as contributing to:

- **Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS)**: Biofilms on sinus mucosa contribute to the recalcitrant nature of CRS, particularly in post-surgical recurrences. The presence of biofilm correlates with poorer outcomes after [[FESS Steps Complications|functional endoscopic sinus surgery]].
- **[[OME Pathophysiology|Otitis media with effusion]] (OME)**: Biofilms form on the middle ear mucosa and on ventilation tubes, contributing to persistent effusion and chronic otorrhoea.
- **Chronic tonsillitis**: Tonsillar crypts harbour biofilm communities, which may explain recurrent and chronic tonsillitis.
- **Cystic fibrosis**: Colonization of the lower respiratory tract with *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* biofilms is a hallmark of pulmonary disease progression.
- **Device infections**: Tracheostomy tubes, [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]]s and other ENT implants may become colonized by biofilm, leading to persistent infection.
- **Cholesteatoma**: Biofilm formation within cholesteatoma contributes to its persistence and recurrence after surgery.

## Part B: Branchial Cleft Anomalies — Embryology and Second Branchial Fistula Management

*Q27: Branchial cleft anomalies embryology. 2nd branchial fistula management (2020 Dec)*

### Embryology of the Branchial Apparatus

The branchial apparatus is the embryological framework from which many head and neck structures develop. It consists of three types of structures:

1. **Branchial arches**: These are mesodermal structures that go on to form muscle, cartilage and bone. First and second branchial arch anomalies result in structural deformities of the face, as in Treacher Collins syndrome or hemifacial microsomia.

2. **Branchial clefts**: There are spaces between the arches on the outside of the embryo, lined with ectoderm. Branchial cleft anomalies result in a congenital sinus with an opening on a skin surface. This may be around the angle of the mandible or in the EAC (first branchial cleft sinus) or in the neck along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid (second branchial cleft sinus).

3. **Pharyngeal pouches**: There are also spaces between the arches on the inside of the embryo, as the arches bulge into the foregut. These are lined with endoderm. Pharyngeal pouch anomalies (third or fourth) result in a sinus with an opening on a mucosal surface, typically in the left piriform fossa.

### Branchial Arch Derivatives

| Arch | Nerve | Muscles | Skeletal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | CN V (trigeminal) | Muscles of mastication | Meckel's cartilage, malleus, incus |
| 2nd | CN VII (facial) | Muscles of facial expression | Reichert's cartilage, stapes, styloid |
| 3rd | CN IX (glossopharyngeal) | Stylopharyngeus | Greater horn of hyoid |
| 4th/6th | CN X (vagus, SLN/RLN) | Laryngeal muscles | Thyroid, cricoid, arytenoid cartilages |

### First Branchial Cleft Sinuses

First branchial cleft sinuses are rare and account for less than 10% of branchial cleft anomalies. The external opening in the neck varies in position but lies on a line between the tragus and the hyoid bone. The opening is often inconspicuous and appears as a skin-lined pit. The superior attachment of the sinus is variable: there may be an opening anterior to the tragus or the tract may form a true fistula running inferior to the floor of the EAC and opening at the osseocartilaginous junction in the external auditory meatus.

**Work and Work Classification:**

- **Type I lesions**: Of ectodermal origin, present medial to the concha, usually superficial and simple in nature.
- **Type II lesions**: Both ectodermal and mesodermal in origin, contain cartilage and hair follicles. The more inferior opening of the type II fistula is usually below the angle of the mandible and the tract is intimately related to the facial nerve trunk.

**Key surgical points for 1st branchial cleft sinuses:**
- Facial nerve exposure and protection is required in nearly every case
- The superficial position of the facial nerve in small children must be considered
- A parotidectomy-type incision is modified to include the lower opening
- Intraoperative facial nerve monitoring is essential
- The upper end must be followed and excised completely to ensure no squamous epithelium remains
- This may require opening of the EAC and use of the otological microscope

### Second Branchial Cleft Sinuses

Second branchial cleft sinuses are the commonest of the branchial anomalies (up to 90%). The second branchial arch forms the epidermis of the upper neck and dorsal pinna. The mesoderm forms the facial muscles and the body of the hyoid, and the endodermal elements form the root of the tongue, the foramen caecum, the thyroid stalk and the tonsil.

**Anatomy of the 2nd branchial cleft sinus tract:**
- Presents as a congenital opening on the lower neck, anterior to the sternocleidomastoid muscle
- The tract is directed proximally and medially to pass between the internal and external carotid arteries
- The proximal end may communicate with the pharynx through the palatine tonsil to form a true fistula, although most lesions are not patent through their whole length
- The sinus nearly always leaks clear or mucoid fluid from the distal external opening due to secretions from ectopic salivary tissue in the wall of the sinus

**Second branchial cleft sinuses may be associated with branchio-oto-renal syndrome, especially if bilateral.**

### Management of Second Branchial Fistula

**Investigation:**
- As a rule, the diagnosis is evident clinically and no further investigation is required
- Neck ultrasonography can characterize the size of tract or associated cyst, and renal tracts can be imaged to exclude horseshoe or duplex kidney
- Radiopaque sinogram is not routinely indicated
- MRI is preferable to CT in children to minimize exposure to ionizing radiation and provide excellent detail of soft-tissue planes

**Surgical technique:**
1. Surgery can be performed at any age and aims to excise the tract completely when no infection is present
2. Surgery is ill-advised during acute infection — a period of quiescence should be awaited
3. A fusiform skin incision is made around the external opening
4. The tract is followed upwards with microbipolar dissection and counter-traction with lacrimal retractors
5. If the distance between the external opening and pharynx is too great, a second stepladder incision is made superiorly in the neck to enable the cranial portion of the tract to be visualized and excised (rarely necessary in the young child)
6. The tract will be seen to enter the pharyngeal muscle at the level of the palatine tonsil and can be ligated and excised at this level
7. Ipsilateral tonsillectomy is unnecessary in the vast majority of cases, but should be considered in larger type IV second branchial cleft cysts that abut the pharynx
8. Care must be taken to avoid the hypoglossal nerve in the superior part of the dissection
9. Complete excision of the sinus should prevent recurrence

**For second branchial cleft cysts**: Surgery should be planned when infection has resolved, taking care to avoid injuring the internal jugular vein and hypoglossal nerve superiorly.

### Third and Fourth Pharyngeal Pouch Sinuses

These are rare abnormalities, accounting for 1-2% of branchial lesions. They can present as a sinus, cyst, thyroid gland abscess or even a fistula from pharynx to skin. They almost always occur on the left side and tend to present in early childhood with associated infections or in neonates with airway obstruction. Both originate at the pyriform sinus. The third arch is associated with the glossopharyngeal nerve whereas the fourth is related to the superior laryngeal branch of the vagus nerve.

**Management:**
- Modern management utilizes endoscopic cautery of the pyriform fossa sinus alone as first-line treatment
- Bailey described successful endoscopic diathermy of the pyriform fossa opening in 2004
- Garabedian recognized a higher recurrence rate in neonates and recommended resection via open approach rather than endoscopic approach in this younger age group

## Part C: Branchial Cyst — Etiopathogenesis and Management

*Q150: Branchial cyst — etiopathogenesis and management (2013 Jun)*

### Etiopathogenesis

Second branchial cleft cysts are thought to be congenital in origin even though they rarely present in childhood and are much more common in young adults. They typically present as a spherical swelling inferior to the angle of the mandible, anterior to the sternocleidomastoid, although other unusual locations occur.

**Embryological basis**: The second branchial cleft sits immediately caudal to the second branchial arch structures. It is persistence of this cleft that leads to the formation of the second branchial arch sinus. Cysts form when the cleft remnant does not communicate with the skin or pharynx but instead remains as a closed epithelial-lined cavity that gradually enlarges as fluid accumulates.

### Bailey's Classification of Second Branchial Cleft Cysts

This classification provides a framework for diagnosis and surgical planning of unusually placed cysts:

| Type | Location | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Type I | Deep to platysma, anterior to SCM | Most superficial |
| Type II | Abutting ICA, adherent to IJV | Most common type |
| Type III | Between ICA and ECA | Extends between carotids |
| Type IV | Abutting pharyngeal wall | May extend to skull base |

### Presentation

- Painless, fluctuant, smooth swelling in the upper neck
- Typically at the junction of the upper and middle thirds of the anterior border of SCM
- May present following an upper respiratory tract infection when it enlarges
- May become acutely infected, forming an abscess
- Usually present in the 2nd to 4th decade of life
- On aspiration, the fluid is typically straw-coloured or turbid, containing cholesterol crystals
- Histology shows squamous or columnar epithelium lining with lymphoid tissue in the wall

### Investigation

- Ultrasound confirms the cystic nature and delineates the size and location
- Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) helps distinguish from cystic metastatic lymph node (especially important in older adults)
- MRI is preferable to CT in children; cross-sectional imaging shows close relations particularly for type III or IV presentations
- Renal ultrasound to exclude horseshoe or duplex kidney (especially if bilateral, to exclude branchio-oto-renal syndrome)

### Management

**Surgical excision** is the definitive treatment:
- Surgery should be planned when infection has resolved
- Excision through a transverse skin crease incision
- Careful dissection to identify and preserve the internal jugular vein, hypoglossal nerve and carotid vessels
- The cyst wall is dissected from surrounding structures
- If a tract extends superiorly, it should be followed to its termination and excised completely
- For type IV cysts abutting the pharynx, tonsillectomy should be considered

**Important differential diagnosis in adults**: A cystic mass in the upper neck in an adult over 40 years old must be investigated to exclude a cystic metastatic squamous cell carcinoma from an oropharyngeal primary (particularly HPV-related tonsillar carcinoma) before being labelled as a branchial cyst.

### Preauricular Cysts and Sinuses

The external ear begins to develop at week 6 of gestation. Auricular hillocks, three from the first and three from the second branchial arch, surround the first branchial cleft below the level of the mandible. A blind-ended sinus results from incomplete fusion of the hillocks. Important points:

- A sinus found above the line of the tragus is usually an isolated preauricular sinus/cyst
- A sinus found below the level of the tragus is more likely related to a first branchial cleft anomaly and checking the EAC for fistula is imperative
- Incidence ranges from 0.9% in Western European population to 10% in East Africa
- Majority remain asymptomatic and can be managed conservatively
- If symptomatic, local excision under GA with lacrimal probe exploration, fusiform skin paddle excision, and excision of a sliver of adjacent cartilage reduces recurrence risk

### Branchio-Oto-Renal (BOR) Syndrome

One inherited disorder to be recognized is branchio-oto-renal syndrome:
- Autosomal dominant with variable penetrance
- Involves structural defects of the outer, middle and inner ear
- Branchial cleft anomalies (bilateral preauricular sinuses and bilateral second branchial cleft sinuses may be present)
- Renal abnormalities
- Hearing loss
- Mutations in the EYA1 gene on chromosome 8q13 are found in 40% of patients
- Hearing assessment and ultrasound of renal tracts are recommended management

### Thyroglossal Duct Cyst

The most common congenital abnormality in the head and neck region. The thyroid develops from the foramen caecum at the junction of the anterior two-thirds and posterior one-third of the tongue. In normal development, the thyroid descends through the tongue, passing caudally with an intimate relationship to the hyoid bone and finishing in the anterior neck overlying the trachea.

**Presentation**: Midline swelling (can occur laterally, usually left-sided, in ~10% of cases). The mass elevates on swallowing and tongue protrusion.

**Investigation**: Ultrasound to confirm diagnosis and presence of normal thyroid gland in the usual position. It is possible for the thyroglossal cyst to contain the only functioning thyroid tissue — removal would result in hypothyroidism.

**Sistrunk Procedure**: The definitive operation, described in 1920:
1. Removal of the thyroglossal duct
2. A portion of the hyoid bone (central body)
3. A portion of the raphe joining the mylohyoid muscles
4. A portion of each genioglossus muscle
5. Tissue up to the foramen caecum

If performed as described, recurrence rates can be kept below 5% (confirmed in a 2013 systematic review of 750 patients). Recurrence rates following incomplete excision rise to 27%.

**Extended/Modified Sistrunk Procedure**: Involves wider block dissection incorporating the infrahyoid region to the thyroid isthmus, initially used for recurrent disease but now applied to all patients to minimize complications.

**Complications**: Haematoma, seroma, cyst recurrence, salivary fistula and hypoglossal nerve injury.

## Part D: Down Syndrome Features and Craniofacial Dysostosis Treatment

*Q157: Down syndrome features. Craniofacial dysostosis treatment (2011 Dec)*

### Down Syndrome — ENT Features

Down syndrome is a common and easily recognizable disorder with trisomy of all or a critical portion of chromosome 21. The most common ENT reasons for referral in children with Down syndrome are OME, sleep-disordered breathing and [[Stridor and Laryngomalacia|laryngomalacia]]. There is increased mortality in this group (x6 throughout infancy and x17 until age 9 years) compared to age-matched controls.

**Important clinical considerations:**
- The possibility of atlantoaxial subluxation means any surgical procedure involves very careful handling of the neck
- Immunological aspects of the disease can lead to ventilator tube otorrhoea, persistent rhinorrhoea and recurrent URTIs
- Endotracheal intubation can be difficult and often the child will need a smaller diameter ET tube than age would suggest

#### Otological Aspects

- **Persistent otitis media with effusion (OME)**: Hearing aids should normally be offered to children with Down syndrome who have hearing loss due to OME
- **Narrow and waxy ear canals**: Ventilator tube otorrhoea and early extrusion can be more common
- **Conductive hearing loss**: Secondary to ossicular anomalies
- **Sensorineural hearing loss**: With increased labyrinthine dysplasia — inner ear dysplasia is common in children with Down syndrome
- **Increased incidence of cholesteatoma**: Should be suspected in continuously discharging ears
- **Anatomical abnormalities of the facial nerve**: Tympanomastoid surgery can be especially challenging
- Hearing screening with behavioural testing throughout early childhood should be carried out to establish near-normal hearing with amplification or ventilation tube insertion

#### Airway Aspects

- **[[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|Obstructive sleep apnoea]]**: Due to relative adenotonsillar hypertrophy, upper airway obstruction as part of craniofacial condition including hypoplasia of pharynx and maxillary arch with nasal obstruction, sometimes generalized muscle hypotonia ('floppy baby')
- **Tracheobronchomalacia**: Increased incidence, may need ventilation with/without tracheostomy and CPAP
- **Synchronous airway anomalies**: Including tracheal bronchus, [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] and tracheoesophageal fistula
- Annual screening for OSA until age 3-5 years is recommended
- Even after adenotonsillar surgery, there is a **greater than 50% finding of residual OSA** that will likely need medical intervention (e.g., CPAP)
- These children are unsuitable for day-case adenotonsillectomy and will often require high-dependency care post-operatively

### Craniofacial Dysostosis — Classification and Features

Craniosynostosis is the premature fusion of one or more sutures of the skull. It can be primary or secondary. The syndromic craniosynostoses represent the major craniofacial dysostosis conditions.

**Craniosynostosis — affected sutures and resultant head shapes (Table 19.2):**

| Suture Affected | Resultant Shape |
|---|---|
| Sagittal | Scaphocephaly (elongated) |
| Metopic | Trigonocephaly (wedge-shaped) |
| Unilateral coronal | Frontal plagiocephaly |
| Bilateral coronal | Brachycephaly (short/wide) |
| Unilateral lambdoid | Posterior plagiocephaly |

#### Crouzon Syndrome

- **Incidence**: 1:25,000 live births
- **Genetics**: FGFR2 mutation, autosomal dominant (new mutations frequently arise)
- **Features**: Craniosynostosis (usually bicoronal, brachycephaly) + maxillary hypoplasia + shallow orbits causing ocular proptosis
- Underdeveloped midface results in prolapse of the globes (exorbitism), hypoplasia of malar bones, retromaxillism and malocclusion
- Anomalies of the middle ear and atresia of the external auditory canal may cause conductive hearing loss
- Abnormalities usually confined to the craniofacial region; intelligence usually normal
- Severity is extremely variable, even within the same family
- May range from barely noticeable proptosis/midfacial hypoplasia to cloverleaf skull

#### Apert Syndrome

- **Incidence**: 1:60,000 live births
- **Genetics**: FGFR2 mutations — two-thirds Ser252Trp, one-third Pro253Arg; autosomal dominant, most cases arise from new mutations; link with advanced paternal age
- **Features**: Craniosynostosis (usually bicoronal) + abnormal midfacial development + syndactyly (fusion of digits of hands and feet — the distinguishing feature)
- Brachycephaly and midface retrusion causing anterior open bite (malocclusion)
- Cleft soft palate or bifid uvula in 30%
- Fixation of stapes footplate may cause conductive hearing loss
- Intelligence may vary from normal to significantly impaired
- Non-progressive ventriculomegaly seen commonly but seldom requires CSF diversion

#### Pfeiffer Syndrome

- **Genetics**: FGFR1 and FGFR2 mutations, autosomal dominant (many cases arise as new mutations)
- **Features**: Craniosynostosis + midfacial hypoplasia with shallow orbits + broad thumbs and broad great toes (distinguishing feature)
- Coronal sutures most commonly affected with resultant brachycephaly
- Three clinical subtypes depending on severity
- Skeletal anomalies including fusion of the elbow joint and solid cartilaginous trachea (tracheal cartilaginous sleeve)
- Choanal stenosis or atresia may be present
- **Tracheal cartilaginous sleeve**: A critical FGFR2-associated abnormality. Individual tracheal arches are not formed; instead, there is continuous fused C- or O-shaped cartilage extending from subglottis to bronchi. When C-shaped, the pars membranacea is often notched posteriorly, giving a keyhole appearance. Premature death occurs in almost all patients with this abnormality, but tracheostomy offers a survival advantage

#### Treacher Collins Syndrome (Mandibulofacial Dysostosis)

- **Incidence**: Approximately 1:50,000 live births
- **Genetics**: TCOF1 gene mutation, autosomal dominant; approximately 50% are new mutations with no family history; severity is very variable; intelligence is usually normal
- **Features**: Absence or hypoplasia of zygomatic bones + eyelid abnormalities (down-slanting palpebral fissures, colobomas of lower eyelid) + mandibular hypoplasia + microtia
- Bilateral ear abnormalities including hypoplasia, microtia or anotia, hypoplasia or atresia of EAM, middle ear anomalies — inner ear function usually unaffected, leading to conductive hearing loss
- Cleft palate in 35%
- Mandibular hypoplasia may cause significant upper airway obstruction
- Steep occlusal plane angle makes both intubation and treatment challenging
- The chin point is rotated clockwise towards the larynx/trachea

**Management:**
- Neonatal airway management for respiratory obstruction associated with mandibular hypoplasia or choanal atresia
- Early feeding difficulties may need to be addressed
- Repair of cleft palate
- Provision of bone-conducting hearing aid followed by bone-anchored hearing aid (BAHA)
- Close monitoring and intervention for speech and language development
- Eyelid surgical correction
- Hypoplastic/absent zygomas reconstructed using calvarial bone grafts or alloplastic onlays
- Mandibular deficiency corrected with conventional osteotomies or distraction osteogenesis

### Treatment of Craniofacial Dysostosis

#### Multidisciplinary Team (Table 19.1)

The craniofacial MDT includes:
- Maxillofacial surgery and Plastic surgery (surgical correction)
- Neurosurgery (ICP, hydrocephalus, combined surgery)
- Ophthalmology (ICP, acuity, corneal protection)
- ENT and Audiology (airway, nasal problems, hearing, middle ear disease)
- Anaesthetist (experienced in paediatric craniofacial and neurosurgical anaesthesia, PICU access)
- Genetics (diagnosis, associated conditions, recurrence risk counselling)
- Speech and Language Therapist (feeding, developmental milestones)
- Psychologist (parental anxiety, coping strategies, cognitive assessment)
- Paediatrician (general development, associated abnormalities)
- Orthodontist (craniofacial growth, dental condition, occlusion)
- Specialist nurses and coordinators

#### Airway Management

Respiratory problems may be central (Chiari malformation, raised ICP) or peripheral (obstruction). OSA affects 40-83% of children with syndromic craniosynostosis.

Causes of obstruction are multilevel:
- Congenital bony nasal stenosis (CBNS) — 3D hypoplasia of maxilla causing nasal passage narrowing
- Choanal atresia
- Nasopharyngeal narrowing
- Crowded/narrow oropharynx, thick long soft palate
- Subglottic stenosis
- Tracheal stenosis and tracheal cartilage sleeve

**Management options:**
1. **Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA)**: Successful first-line management in the majority; bypasses narrow nostril, narrow nasopharynx and long thick soft palate; required for 2-48 months; carers undergo training; parents prefer NPA over tracheostomy
2. **Positive pressure ventilation (CPAP)**: Nasal CPAP using mask; fitting the mask onto retruded maxilla or hypoplastic midface can be challenging
3. **Adenotonsillectomy**: Less successful than in general population but improvements seen in up to 60% of patients; not an absolute contraindication with cleft palate — an inferior ridge can be left behind
4. **Tracheostomy**: Reserved for multilevel obstruction unresponsive to other measures; more challenging to perform with tracheal sleeve; may need window rather than vertical slit; smaller tube diameter than expected for age

#### Visual Considerations

- Exorbitism from decreased orbital volume/capacity — ranges from simple exorbitism to globe dislocation
- Corneal abrasions are not uncommon
- Early ophthalmological assessment is imperative
- Regular fundoscopy for papilloedema and optic atrophy from raised ICP
- Tarsorrhaphy may be indicated for globe protection

#### CSF Diversion / Hydrocephalus

- Ventricular dilatation is common in syndromic craniosynostosis (seen in nearly half of cases)
- In Crouzon and Pfeiffer syndrome, ventriculomegaly is progressive and usually requires CSF diversion
- In Apert syndrome, non-progressive ventriculomegaly is common but seldom requires CSF diversion
- Saethre-Chotzen and Muenke syndrome are seldom affected by hydrocephalus
- Etiology is multifactorial: crowded posterior fossa, venous outlet obstruction, increased CSF outflow resistance
- VP shunt with programmable valve and inbuilt antisiphon device is the standard

#### Cranial Surgery for Syndromic Craniosynostosis

1. **Suturectomy**: Initial procedure in some cases, allows continued skull growth; most syndromic patients have a 'pro-ossification' genetic change so sutures often re-fuse

2. **Posterior vault expansion**: Utilizes internal distraction devices to increase posterior vault volume. Usually undertaken after suturectomy fails. Only after posterior vault is corrected does the team address the forehead.

3. **Fronto-orbital advancement and remodelling (FOAR)**: If posterior vault is satisfactory, FOAR is performed. This both increases skull volume and normalizes skull shape. Usually performed at 12-18 months of age — balancing the risk of raised ICP if delayed against risk of restenosis if performed too early. Technique involves bicoronal flap, removal of forehead bone, removal and remodelling of supraorbital bar, replacement in advanced position with resorbable plate fixation.

4. **Midface advancement surgery**: Indicated to improve airway (avoid or remove need for NIV/tracheostomy) and normalize appearance. Usually delayed to age 5-6 years in the UK (allows child weight and blood volume to increase). Three basic operations:
   - **Le Fort III osteotomy**: Advancement of all midfacial structures inferior to frontal bone (nose, zygomas, maxilla en bloc); usually performed by distraction osteogenesis with RED frame
   - **Facial bipartition**: Le Fort III + excision of V-shaped midline bone segment to reduce hypertelorism
   - **Monobloc procedure**: Forehead + midface advancement (Le Fort III combined with FOAR)

5. **Distraction osteogenesis (DO)**: Involves osteotomy without mobilization, followed by application of internal or external distraction device (e.g., RED frame), activated after 1-week latent period, producing 1 mm/day movement. Allows gradual soft-tissue adaptation and osteogenesis without bone grafting. Distractors left in situ for 2-3 times the activation phase for consolidation. Majority of midface advancements are now carried out using distraction.

#### Complications of Craniofacial Surgery

| Complication | Key Points |
|---|---|
| Blood loss | 75-85% require transfusion for FOAR; autologous blood recovery, hypotensive anaesthesia, tranexamic acid used |
| Air embolism | Major danger; can occur during scalp flap raising or opening major sinuses; may cause hypotension, bradycardia, cardiac arrest |
| Cerebral oedema | Minimize with correct positioning, proper ETT, minimal brain retraction; dexamethasone may help |
| Dural tear | Sutured and reinforced with fibrin glue; larger tears need dural grafting |
| CSF fistula | Uncommon; may present as [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]] |
| Infection | Risk when paranasal sinuses communicate; may cause meningitis, intracerebral abscess, subdural empyema |
| Blindness | Risk in surgery around optic apex; hypertelorism procedures and monobloc carry highest risk |
| Restenosis | More common in syndromic than non-syndromic; Saethre-Chotzen has high reoperation rate |

> 7. Third/fourth pharyngeal pouch sinuses are rare (1-2%), almost always left-sided, originate at the pyriform sinus, and are now managed with endoscopic cautery of the pyriform fossa opening as first-line treatment.
>
> 8. Down syndrome ENT features include persistent OME, narrow waxy ear canals, inner ear dysplasia with SNHL, increased cholesteatoma, OSA (>50% residual after adenotonsillectomy), tracheobronchomalacia, and atlantoaxial subluxation risk.
>
> 9. Key craniofacial dysostosis syndromes: Crouzon (FGFR2, craniosynostosis + maxillary hypoplasia + proptosis), Apert (FGFR2, craniosynostosis + syndactyly), Pfeiffer (FGFR1/2, craniosynostosis + broad thumbs + tracheal cartilaginous sleeve), Treacher Collins (TCOF1, absent zygomas + mandibular hypoplasia + microtia).
>
> 10. Tracheal cartilaginous sleeve is an FGFR2-associated abnormality (Crouzon, Pfeiffer) with continuous fused cartilage, keyhole appearance, and high mortality; tracheostomy offers survival advantage but requires window technique and smaller tubes.
>
> 11. Management of syndromic craniosynostosis follows a stepwise approach: airway management (NPA > CPAP > tracheostomy), visual protection, ICP control, followed by cranial surgery (suturectomy/posterior vault expansion > FOAR > midface advancement with distraction osteogenesis).
>
> 12. The Sistrunk procedure for thyroglossal duct cyst involves removal of the cyst, central portion of hyoid bone, mylohyoid raphe, genioglossus muscle and tissue to foramen caecum; recurrence is <5% with complete excision versus 27% with incomplete excision.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always consider a first branchial cleft anomaly in a child with recurrent periauricular or upper neck abscesses, and perform facial nerve monitoring during excision.
>
> - In an adult over 40 years presenting with a "branchial cyst," always exclude cystic metastatic SCC from an oropharyngeal primary (especially HPV-related tonsillar carcinoma) before labelling it as a congenital branchial cyst.
>
> - Bilateral preauricular sinuses or bilateral branchial sinuses should raise suspicion of branchio-oto-renal syndrome (EYA1 gene, chromosome 8q13) — request hearing assessment and renal ultrasound.
>
> - Children with Down syndrome require smaller ET tubes than age suggests, careful neck handling for atlantoaxial subluxation risk, and post-operative HDU care after adenotonsillectomy; over 50% have residual OSA after surgery.
>
> - Tracheal cartilaginous sleeve in Pfeiffer/Crouzon syndrome presents with keyhole-shaped trachea on endoscopy, high mortality, and requires tracheostomy with window technique and smaller-than-expected tube.
>
> - Midface advancement in syndromic craniosynostosis is best performed using distraction osteogenesis (RED frame), which allows gradual soft-tissue expansion and larger movements without bone grafting.
>
> - The Sistrunk procedure must include the central hyoid bone and core of tissue to the foramen caecum — failure to do so raises recurrence from <5% to 27%.
>
> - Endoscopic cautery of the pyriform fossa opening is now first-line treatment for third/fourth pharyngeal pouch sinuses in older children, though neonates still benefit from open surgical approach.
