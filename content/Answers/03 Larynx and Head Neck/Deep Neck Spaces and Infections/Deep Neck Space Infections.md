# Deep Neck Space Infections — Classification, Ludwig's Angina and Necrotizing Fasciitis

> [!question] Questions
> Classify deep neck space infections. Describe the clinical features and management of Ludwig's angina. Discuss the deep neck spaces classification. Describe the clinical features, diagnosis and management of necrotizing cervical fasciitis.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 35 (Surgical Anatomy of the Neck — Fascial Layers and Neck Spaces); Volume 2, Chapter 37 (Cervicofacial Infections)

---

## Introduction

A collection of pus in one of the fascial compartments of the neck is termed a deep neck space infection (DNSI). The deep cervical fascia is arranged to create compartments or spaces between the structures of the neck. Whilst the fascial layers normally limit the spread of infection, these inter-connected spaces can act as routes of transmission if infection breaches the fascial barriers. Deep neck space infections may lead to severe and potentially life-threatening complications, including mediastinitis, septic embolization, dural sinus thrombosis and intracranial abscess. Securing the airway is the first priority in management as collections within the submandibular space and deep neck spaces readily result in airway obstruction.

---

## PART A: Fascial Layers and Deep Neck Spaces

### Layers of the Deep Cervical Fascia

The neck has superficial and deep fascial planes. The deep fascial layer has three distinct layers:

| Layer | Also Known As | Key Attachments | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investing layer | Superficial layer of DCF | Ligamentum nuchae → entire neck | SCM, trapezius, parotid, all nodal zones |
| Pretracheal fascia | Middle/visceral layer | Hyoid → superior mediastinum | Trachea, thyroid, pharynx, oesophagus |
| Prevertebral fascia | Deep layer | Vertebral column → diaphragm | Prevertebral muscles, scalene muscles |

The **carotid sheath** is derived from condensations of all three fascial layers and encloses the common carotid artery, IJV and vagus nerve.

The **alar fascia** is a supplementary layer anterior to the prevertebral fascia, extending from the skull base to the level of the second thoracic vertebra. The **danger space** lies between the alar fascia and the prevertebral fascia.

### Classification of Deep Neck Spaces

**In relation to the hyoid bone:**

| Category | Spaces |
|---|---|
| Suprahyoid only | Peritonsillar, parapharyngeal, submandibular, sublingual, parotid, masticator |
| Infrahyoid only | Visceral (anterior visceral) |
| Entire neck length | Retropharyngeal, danger space, prevertebral, carotid |

### Summary of Key Deep Neck Spaces

| Space | Boundaries | Contents | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submandibular | Between mylohyoid and investing fascia | Submandibular gland, lingual nerve | Ludwig's angina; odontogenic infections |
| Peritonsillar | Between tonsil and superior constrictor | Loose areolar tissue | Quinsy; may spread to PPS |
| Parapharyngeal | Skull base to hyoid; central connector | Pre-styloid: fat, nerves; Post-styloid: ICA, IJV, CN IX–XII | Central hub connecting other spaces |
| Retropharyngeal | Skull base to posterior mediastinum | Retropharyngeal lymph nodes | [[Retropharyngeal Abscess|Retropharyngeal abscess]]; mediastinitis |
| Danger space | Between alar and prevertebral fascia | Loose areolar tissue only | Extends to diaphragm; mediastinitis |
| Prevertebral | Behind prevertebral fascia | Vertebral column, paraspinal muscles | Spinal osteomyelitis; cord compression |

## PART B: Classification of Deep Neck Space Infections

### By Aetiology

- **Odontogenic** — dental abscess, infected molar or premolar (commonest cause in adults)
- **Tonsillar/pharyngeal** — [[Peritonsillar Abscess|peritonsillar abscess]], suppurative lymphadenopathy
- **Salivary gland** — submandibular sialadenitis
- **Lymph node suppuration** — secondary to upper respiratory infections (commonest in children)
- **Traumatic** — pharyngeal or oesophageal perforation, penetrating neck injury
- **Iatrogenic** — post-surgical (frenuloplasty, dental extraction)

### By Severity

- **Cellulitis** — inflammation of subcutaneous or connective tissue characterized by fever, pain, redness, swelling and warmth
- **Phlegmon** — solid mass of inflammatory tissue from coalescence of lymph nodes; typically responds to IV antibiotics
- **Abscess** — accumulation of pus in a confined space; usually requires surgical drainage
- **Necrotizing fasciitis** — aggressive tissue infection spreading rapidly across fascial planes causing necrosis

### Microbiology

The causative organisms are most commonly:
- Group A beta-haemolytic Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus (most common)
- Anaerobes (19%) — Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus
- Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis
- Beta-lactamase-positive organisms
- Mixed aerobic-anaerobic infections are common

## PART C: Ludwig's Angina

### Definition

Ludwig's angina is a life-threatening airway complication of submandibular space infection, characterized by rapid onset of cellulitis of the floor of mouth tissues, causing swelling, induration and tongue elevation resulting in a compromised airway.

### Anatomy of the Submandibular Space

The submandibular space is divided by the mylohyoid muscle into:
- **Superior compartment (sublingual space)** — contains sublingual gland, deep portion of submandibular gland, lingual nerve
- **Inferior compartment** — contains superficial lobe of submandibular gland

These compartments communicate around the free posterior edge of mylohyoid. Because the deep fascia does not split to enclose the submandibular gland, swelling in this space occurs in a cephalic and medial direction — elevating the tongue and compromising the airway.

### Aetiology

- Most commonly **odontogenic** in origin (infected molar or premolar)
- May occur secondary to submandibular sialadenitis, lymphadenitis or trauma
- Reported after frenuloplasty in an adolescent
- Causative organisms: Streptococcus species, Gram-negative rods and anaerobes

### Clinical Features

- Bilateral swelling of the submandibular space and neck ('bull neck' appearance)
- Firm induration of the floor of mouth
- Elevation and protrusion of the tongue
- Drooling and dysphagia
- Trismus
- Fever, toxaemia
- **Airway obstruction** — the most feared complication; may make endotracheal intubation problematic due to distorted and displaced anatomy

### Management

**1. Airway — first priority:**
- Securing the airway is the mainstay of treatment
- Anticipate difficult intubation; fibre-optic intubation or awake [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] may be required
- Surgical airway (tracheostomy) if intubation fails

**2. Medical management:**
- Prompt initiation of intravenous antibiotics is usually sufficient
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics covering aerobes and anaerobes (e.g. IV amoxicillin/clavulanate or piperacillin/tazobactam with metronidazole)
- Fluid resuscitation and analgesia

**3. Surgical intervention:**
- Reserved for persistent or progressive infections despite IV antibiotics, or when there is evidence of abscess formation
- Techniques include intraoral and external approaches to ensure drainage of submaxillary and sublingual spaces
- Dental extraction of the causative tooth

## PART D: Necrotizing Cervical Fasciitis

### Definition

Necrotizing fasciitis is an aggressive tissue infection that spreads rapidly across fascial planes causing necrosis of tissue along the way. In the cervical region, it represents one of the most lethal complications of deep neck space infections.

### Aetiology

- Usually polymicrobial (mixed aerobic-anaerobic infection)
- Group A Streptococcus is the commonest single organism
- Risk factors: diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, alcoholism, IV drug use, malnutrition
- May arise from odontogenic infections, pharyngeal infections, or post-surgical complications
- Can complicate peritonsillar, parapharyngeal or submandibular space infections

### Clinical Features

- Disproportionate pain relative to clinical findings (early)
- Rapidly progressive erythema, induration and oedema
- Crepitus (subcutaneous emphysema from gas-producing organisms)
- Skin necrosis with dusky discolouration or bullae
- Systemic toxicity — high fever, tachycardia, hypotension, multi-organ failure
- May spread along fascial planes to involve the mediastinum (descending necrotizing mediastinitis)

### Diagnosis

- **Clinical suspicion** is paramount — do not delay treatment awaiting investigations
- CT with contrast — demonstrates fascial thickening, fluid collections, gas tracking along fascial planes, soft-tissue oedema
- Laboratory: leucocytosis (or leucopenia in severe sepsis), raised CRP, elevated lactate, coagulopathy

### Management

**1. Resuscitation:**
- Aggressive fluid resuscitation, ICU admission
- Sepsis protocol — blood cultures, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics
- Airway management — early intubation or tracheostomy

**2. Antibiotics:**
- Broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics covering aerobes, anaerobes and Gram-negatives
- Typically: piperacillin/tazobactam or meropenem + clindamycin (anti-toxin effect) + metronidazole
- Adjust based on culture and sensitivity results

**3. Surgical debridement:**
- Urgent and aggressive surgical debridement is the cornerstone of treatment
- All necrotic tissue must be excised — repeated debridements (second-look surgery at 24–48 hours) are usually required
- Wide cervical incisions exposing all involved fascial planes
- Drain all involved deep neck spaces
- Consider thoracotomy or mediastinal drainage if descending mediastinitis is present

**4. Supportive care:**
- Nutritional support (nasogastric or parenteral)
- Wound care with vacuum-assisted closure (VAC) may be beneficial
- Hyperbaric oxygen therapy — has been used as adjunct but evidence is limited
- Reconstruction of soft tissue defects after infection control

## PART E: Lemierre's Syndrome

A septic thrombosis of the internal jugular vein, most often caused by **Fusobacterium necrophorum**. It was common in the pre-antibiotic era with a mortality exceeding 50%.

- Follows an episode of upper respiratory sepsis (usually pharyngitis/tonsillitis)
- High spiking fevers with tenderness and fullness of one side of the neck
- Pulmonary emboli may occur causing lung abscess formation
- Incidence in the UK is rising, perhaps related to changing patterns of antibiotic use
- Diagnosis: Doppler ultrasound or CT with contrast
- Treatment: IV antibiotics and drainage of any abscess; role of anticoagulants is controversial; surgical treatment of the IJV is rarely required

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The deep cervical fascia has three layers — investing, pretracheal (visceral) and prevertebral — knowledge of these fascial planes is essential for safe neck surgery and understanding infection spread
> 2. The [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]] is the central hub connecting the peritonsillar, retropharyngeal and submandibular spaces — it is the commonest site for deep neck abscess formation
> 3. The danger space lies between the alar fascia and prevertebral fascia, extending to the diaphragm — hence the name, as infections can readily spread to the mediastinum
> 4. Ludwig's angina is a rapidly progressive cellulitis of the floor of mouth involving the submandibular space — securing the airway is the first priority in management
> 5. Ludwig's angina is most commonly odontogenic in origin — prompt IV antibiotics are usually sufficient, with surgical drainage reserved for persistent infection or abscess formation
> 6. The distinction between phlegmon and abscess is critical — phlegmons settle with IV antibiotics while abscesses usually require surgical drainage
> 7. Necrotizing cervical fasciitis requires urgent aggressive surgical debridement with repeated second-look operations — antibiotics alone are insufficient
> 8. Lemierre's syndrome is septic thrombosis of the IJV caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum — high spiking fevers with pulmonary emboli after pharyngeal sepsis
> 9. Causative organisms in DNSI are usually Group A Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus, with anaerobes implicated in 19% of cases
> 10. Deep neck space infections can cause mediastinitis, septic embolization, dural sinus thrombosis and intracranial abscess — early recognition and treatment is essential


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - In Ludwig's angina, swelling occurs in a cephalic and medial direction (because the fascia does not split to enclose the submandibular gland) — this elevates the tongue and explains the airway compromise
> - Gas tracking along fascial planes on CT is highly suggestive of necrotizing fasciitis — this should prompt immediate surgical exploration
> - Disproportionate pain relative to visible clinical findings is an early warning sign of necrotizing fasciitis — have a low threshold for CT imaging
> - The parapharyngeal space communicates with the retropharyngeal space posteriorly — untreated parapharyngeal abscess may spread to cause retropharyngeal abscess and subsequently mediastinitis via the danger space
> - Dental abscess from an infected molar or premolar may cause extensive swelling extending into the face and neck — always consider odontogenic causes in the differential of a deep neck space infection
