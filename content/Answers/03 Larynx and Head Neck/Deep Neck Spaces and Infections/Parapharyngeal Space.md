# Parapharyngeal Space — Anatomy, Pathology and Surgical Approaches

> [!question] Questions
> Describe the surgical anatomy of the parapharyngeal space with a diagram. Discuss the surgical approaches to the parapharyngeal space. Describe the benign and malignant lesions of the parapharyngeal space.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 11 (Tumours of the Parapharyngeal Space) and Chapter 35 (Surgical Anatomy of the Neck)

---

## Introduction

The parapharyngeal space is an intriguing compartment of the head and neck. Although it is the site of only 0.5% of all head and neck masses, the unusual pathology and proximity to vital anatomy attracts special interest. Lesions of the parapharyngeal space may involve a wide spectrum of primary pathologies — approximately 70 different histological subtypes have been identified. Pre-operative diagnostic accuracy has improved with the advent of multi-planar radiological imaging (CT and MRI) and image-guided fine-needle aspiration cytology. A systematic review of 1143 parapharyngeal space tumours over a 20-year period found that 82% were benign and 18% malignant.

---

## PART A: Surgical Anatomy of the Parapharyngeal Space

### Shape and Boundaries

The parapharyngeal space is classically described as an inverted pyramidal-shaped potential space filled with fat and areolar tissue. A variety of names have been ascribed to this space including pterygomaxillary, pharyngomaxillary, pterygopharyngeal and lateral pharyngeal space.

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| Base (superior) | Skull base (sphenoid, temporal bones) |
| Apex (inferior) | Greater cornu of hyoid bone |
| Medial | Superior constrictor, buccopharyngeal membrane |
| Lateral | Medial pterygoid, mandibular ramus, deep lobe of parotid |
| Posterior | Prevertebral fascia, carotid sheath |
| Anterior edge | Pterygomandibular raphe |

### Compartments

The parapharyngeal space is divided into two compartments by the fascial condensation called the **aponeurosis of Zuckerkandl and Testut**, which extends from the styloid process to the tensor veli palatini.

**Pre-styloid compartment:**
- Adipose tissue and lymphatics
- Ectopic salivary gland tissue
- Small branch of trigeminal nerve (to tensor veli palatini)
- Branches of ascending pharyngeal artery and pharyngeal venous plexus
- Related to the retromandibular, deep aspect of the parotid gland

**Post-styloid compartment:**
- Internal carotid artery
- Internal jugular vein
- Cranial nerves IX, X, XI and XII
- Cervical sympathetic chain
- Lymph nodes and glomus bodies

### The Stylomandibular Tunnel

A condensation of cervical fascia extends from near the apex of the styloid process to the angle and posterior border of the mandible — the stylomandibular ligament. This ligament (posteriorly) and the mandible (anteriorly) form the stylomandibular tunnel. The tunnel is a deep relation of the deep lobe of the parotid gland. Tumours of the deep lobe can extend into the parapharyngeal space through this tunnel giving rise to a dumbbell-shaped tumour.

### Communications with Other Spaces

The parapharyngeal space is a central connection for other deep neck spaces and as a result is a common site for deep neck abscess formation:
- Communicates medially with the **peritonsillar space**
- Communicates posteriorly with the **retropharyngeal space** (and through this with the posterior mediastinum)
- Communicates inferiorly with the **submandibular space**
- Communicates superiorly with the **infratemporal fossa**

## PART B: Pathology of the Parapharyngeal Space

### Distribution of Tumours

| Category | Proportion | Key Subtypes |
|---|---|---|
| Salivary gland origin | 45% | [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|Pleomorphic adenoma]] (64% of benign) |
| Neurogenic lesions | 41% | [[Glomus Tumour|Paraganglioma]]s (52%), schwannomas (27%) |
| Miscellaneous | 12% | Branchial cysts, haemangiomas, meningiomas |
| Metastatic | 3% | NPC (commonest), tonsil, thyroid |
| Lymphoid origin | 2% | Lymphoma |

Overall: **82% benign, 18% malignant**.

### Salivary Gland Tumours

These are the commonest neoplasms of the parapharyngeal space, arising in the pre-styloid compartment. The majority are benign pleomorphic adenomas. It is important to distinguish between tumours arising from the deep lobe of the parotid gland and those arising from ectopic salivary glandular tissue in the parapharyngeal space, as this determines the surgical approach. Tumours arising from the deep lobe generally require a transparotid approach with a partial parotidectomy, whereas tumours independent of the deep lobe may be suitable for transcervical or transoral resection.

Malignant salivary gland tumours include adenoid cystic carcinoma (commonest malignant), mucoepidermoid carcinoma, adenocarcinoma and acinic cell carcinoma.

### Paragangliomas

Paraganglia are groups of cells of neural crest origin belonging to the extra-adrenal chromaffin and non-chromaffin cell system. Key features:

- About 30% have a genetic origin related to the SDH gene locus (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC)
- Associated with MEN2a and MEN2b syndromes
- About 10% are multicentric — whole body MRI should be performed
- About 3% may be secretory — all patients should be assessed for catecholamine secretion prior to surgery
- Characteristic imaging: 'salt and pepper' appearance on MRI (flow voids); 'Lyre' sign on angiography (carotid body tumours)
- Histology: Zellbalen pattern (clusters of chief cells and sustentacular cells surrounded by vascular sinusoids)
- Difficult to discriminate between benign and malignant on histology alone

### Schwannomas

- Benign neurogenic tumours originating from Schwann cells
- Present in patients aged 30–70 years
- Vagus nerve is the origin in 50% of parapharyngeal schwannomas; cervical sympathetic chain is the next most common source
- Fusiform, sharply circumscribed masses on CT/MRI
- Heterogeneous signal on MRI with no flow voids (differentiates from paraganglioma)

### Metastatic Disease

The parapharyngeal space contains lymph nodes that drain the mucosa of the upper aerodigestive tract. The most common metastases to the parapharyngeal space are from nasopharyngeal cancer (involved in up to 30% of cases). Other primary sites include tonsil, tongue base, maxillary sinus, thyroid, parotid and distant sites (breast, colon, prostate).

## PART C: Clinical Presentation and Evaluation

### Clinical Features

These tumours are usually asymptomatic oropharyngeal masses found on incidental examination. They need to be at least 3 cm before they become apparent and present as a neck mass. Symptoms may include:
- Nasal obstruction or snoring
- Hearing loss (Eustachian tube occlusion)
- Hoarseness, dysphagia or cough (pressure on CN X, XII, IX)
- Horner syndrome (sympathetic chain involvement)

### Examination

- Assessment of airway, speech and swallowing
- Full cranial nerves examination
- Nasopharyngoscopy and examination of middle ear for effusion
- **Bimanual palpation and ballotment** — assesses tumour mobility and size
- Medial displacement of the tonsil suggests a pre-styloid lesion; displacement of the posterior pharyngeal wall suggests a post-styloid lesion

### Imaging

| Imaging Feature | Pre-styloid Lesion | Post-styloid Lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Parapharyngeal fat | Displaced posterolaterally | Displaced anteromedially |
| Internal carotid artery | Displaced posterolaterally | Displaced anteromedially |
| Parotid relationship | May be contiguous with deep lobe | Independent of parotid |
| Typical pathology | Pleomorphic adenoma | Paraganglioma, schwannoma |

- **Fat plane** between the parotid and the lesion differentiates a true parapharyngeal space lesion from a deep lobe parotid extension through the stylomandibular tunnel
- CT shows bony detail and calcification; MRI shows soft tissue extension and perineural spread
- MR angiography and carotid angiography provide additional vascular detail
- Functioning paragangliomas require 24-hour urine screen for metanephrine
- Image-guided FNAC for lesions not accessible transorally

## PART D: Surgical Approaches

Lesions of the parapharyngeal space are predominantly treated by surgery — 96% of patients in a systematic review had surgery. The aim is to remove the lesion with minimal morbidity.

### 1. Transcervical Approach (46% of cases)

The most commonly utilized approach. Ideal for small benign tumours independent of the deep lobe of the parotid gland.

**Technique:**
- Transverse skin crease incision approximately 5 cm below the mandible (to spare the marginal mandibular nerve)
- Platysma divided; superficial fascia of submandibular gland raised with superior flap to protect facial nerve branches
- Submandibular gland mobilized and displaced anteriorly
- Stylomandibular ligament identified and divided; mandible distracted anteriorly
- Parapharyngeal space accessed between the digastric muscle and the periosteum of the mandible
- Most dissection performed by blunt finger dissection staying close to tumour capsule
- External carotid artery may need ligation for large tumours

### 2. Transcervical–Transparotid Approach (27% of cases)

Well suited for lesions arising from the deep lobe of the parotid, vascular tumours and malignant lesions. Performed through a doubly modified Blair's incision (as for routine parotidectomy) with a slight anterior extension. Includes appropriate parotidectomy with facial nerve identification and defence.

### 3. Mandibulotomy (9% of cases)

Combined with transcervical or transcervical–transparotid approaches. Allows access for excision of infiltrating malignancy, multifocal recurrent benign tumours and better visual access to the superior parapharyngeal space and skull base. Can be performed in the standard paramedian position, or laterally with defence of the inferior alveolar nerve using an 'inverted L' osteotomy. Patients may require a [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] and have a longer hospital stay.

### 4. Transoral Robotic Surgery (TORS)

Represents a paradigm shift in parapharyngeal space surgery. The da Vinci robotic system provides three-dimensional, high-resolution visual access with magnification, tremor filtration and motion scaling.

**Indications:**
- Small benign tumours independent of the deep lobe of the parotid
- Lesions with adequate fat plane between tumour and carotid
- Tumours with a thick capsule amenable to blunt dissection

**Contraindications for TORS:**
- Tumours arising from the deep lobe of the parotid (require transparotid approach)
- Vascular tumours encircling the internal carotid artery
- Malignant lesions requiring wide excision

**Outcomes:** University of Pennsylvania series — 25 cases with no recurrences and no patients requiring mandibulotomy or tracheostomy.

### 5. Other Approaches

- Infratemporal fossa approach — for lesions with skull base extension
- Transmastoid approach — for high vagal paragangliomas
- Transpalatal approach — rarely used

### Adjuvant Therapy

- [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]] reserved for malignant lesions or recurrent benign lesions with a high risk of recidivism
- Chemotherapy when indicated by specific histology (e.g. rhabdomyosarcoma), positive margins, perineural or lymphovascular spread
- Pre-operative embolization 24–48 hours before excision of vascular tumours
- Balloon occlusion testing for lesions encircling the ICA

---

## PART E: Prognosis

- Benign tumours: 5-year progression-free survival of **93%**
- Malignant tumours: 5-year progression-free survival of **61%**
- Recurrence of benign tumours remains possible, particularly with capsule disruption during surgery

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The parapharyngeal space is an inverted pyramid-shaped potential space — its base is at the skull base and apex at the greater cornu of the hyoid
> 2. The aponeurosis of Zuckerkandl and Testut divides the space into pre-styloid (salivary tumours) and post-styloid (neurogenic tumours) compartments
> 3. The post-styloid compartment contains the ICA, IJV, CN IX–XII, sympathetic chain and glomus bodies
> 4. 82% of parapharyngeal space tumours are benign — salivary gland tumours (45%) and neurogenic lesions (41%) are the commonest
> 5. A fat plane between the parotid and the lesion on MRI differentiates a true parapharyngeal space tumour from a deep lobe parotid extension through the stylomandibular tunnel
> 6. Pre-styloid lesions displace the ICA and parapharyngeal fat posterolaterally; post-styloid lesions displace them anteromedially
> 7. The transcervical approach is the commonest (46%) — the stylomandibular ligament is divided and the mandible distracted anteriorly for access
> 8. All patients with paraganglioma must be assessed for catecholamine secretion prior to surgery and screened for multicentric disease
> 9. The parapharyngeal space communicates with the peritonsillar, retropharyngeal and submandibular spaces — it is a common site for deep neck abscess formation
> 10. TORS represents a paradigm shift for suitable small benign tumours — 3D visualization and tremor filtration address previous concerns about transoral approaches


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Deep lobe parotid tumours extending through the stylomandibular tunnel produce a characteristic dumbbell-shaped mass — these require a transparotid approach, not transoral resection
> - Schwannomas produce unique pain on FNAC that is not usually reported with other parapharyngeal space tumours — this can be a diagnostic clue
> - Paragangliomas show the characteristic 'salt and pepper' appearance on MRI due to flow voids, while schwannomas lack flow voids — this is the key imaging distinction
> - About 30% of paragangliomas have a genetic basis (SDH gene mutations) — genetic counselling and screening of family members should be offered
> - Nasopharyngeal cancer is the commonest source of metastatic disease to parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal lymph nodes — always check for a primary in patients with parapharyngeal lymphadenopathy
