# Retropharyngeal Abscess — Etiopathogenesis and Management

**Question:** *Describe the etiopathogenesis and management of retropharyngeal abscess in a child. Discuss the surgical anatomy of the deep neck spaces. Describe the clinical features, diagnosis and management of retropharyngeal abscess in an adult.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 35 (Surgical Anatomy of the Neck — Neck Spaces); Volume 2, Chapter 37 (Cervicofacial Infections)

---

## Introduction

Retropharyngeal abscess is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening [[Deep Neck Space Infections|deep neck space infection]] that should be recognized because of its potential to cause fatal airway obstruction. The retropharyngeal space lies between the two [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]]s, posterior to the pharynx and anterior to the prevertebral fascia. In children, retropharyngeal abscesses arise most commonly from suppuration of retropharyngeal lymph nodes secondary to upper respiratory infections. In adults, the aetiology is different — pharyngeal or oesophageal perforation (traumatic or iatrogenic) is the predominant cause. Retropharyngeal infection may spread to the posterior mediastinum via direct extension or through the danger space, making early recognition and treatment essential.

---

## PART A: Anatomy of the Retropharyngeal Space

### Boundaries

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| Superior | Skull base |
| Inferior | Continuous with posterior mediastinum (to level of carina) |
| Anterior | Buccopharyngeal fascia (encasing pharyngeal constrictors) |
| Posterior | Alar fascial component of prevertebral fascia |
| Lateral | Communicates with parapharyngeal spaces bilaterally |

### Contents

The only contents of the retropharyngeal space are the **retropharyngeal lymph nodes** (nodes of Rouvière). These nodes typically receive lymphatic drainage from the nasopharynx, adenoids, tonsils, posterior pharyngeal wall and middle ear. The retropharyngeal nodes are prominent in children aged 1–5 years and typically regress by adulthood.

### The Danger Space

Posterior to the retropharyngeal space, between the alar fascia and the prevertebral fascia itself, lies the **danger space**. This extends from the skull base to the level of the diaphragm. It is only visible radiologically if distended by a collection. The name refers to the potential for neck infections to readily spread into the thorax and cause mediastinitis.

### Communication with Other Spaces

- The two parapharyngeal spaces communicate with the retropharyngeal space posteriorly — infection from the parapharyngeal space can readily spread into the retropharyngeal space
- The peritonsillar space communicates with the parapharyngeal space, providing an indirect route from tonsillar infection to the retropharyngeal space
- Inferiorly, infection can extend into the posterior mediastinum or via the danger space to the level of the diaphragm

## PART B: Etiopathogenesis

### In Children

In paediatric patients, deep neck space infections are typically caused by the suppuration and perforation of retropharyngeal lymph nodes, which serve as a drainage pathway for adenoid and tonsillar infections in children aged 1–5 years. The retropharyngeal, parapharyngeal and peritonsillar spaces are therefore more frequently involved in children.

**Sequence of events:**
1. Upper respiratory tract infection (pharyngitis, tonsillitis, adenoiditis, otitis media)
2. Reactive retropharyngeal lymphadenitis
3. Suppuration of retropharyngeal nodes
4. Perforation of node capsule → retropharyngeal abscess formation

**Microbiology:** Group A beta-haemolytic Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus are the commonest causative organisms. Anaerobes, Haemophilus influenzae and beta-lactamase-positive organisms may also be implicated.

### In Adults

Retropharyngeal lymph nodes typically regress by adulthood, so lymph node suppuration is an uncommon cause in adults. The aetiology in adults includes:

- **Pharyngeal or oesophageal perforation** — traumatic (foreign body, instrumentation) or iatrogenic (endoscopy, intubation)
- **Spread from adjacent spaces** — parapharyngeal abscess tracking posteriorly
- **Vertebral osteomyelitis** — Pott's disease (tuberculous spondylitis) or pyogenic vertebral infection
- **Penetrating neck trauma**
- **Immunosuppression** — HIV, diabetes, chronic steroid use

Radiological evidence of retropharyngeal lymphadenopathy in adults should raise the suspicion of head and neck malignancy, as these nodes may harbour metastatic disease from nasopharyngeal, oropharyngeal or thyroid primaries.

## PART C: Clinical Features

### In Children

- Usually febrile, systemically unwell
- Drooling and dysphagia
- **Characteristic posture** — neck flexed with head extended ('sniffing' position)
- Stridor and airway compromise (anterior displacement of posterior pharyngeal wall)
- Neck stiffness and irritability
- Muffled voice ('hot potato' voice)
- Refusal to eat
- Torticollis (neck held stiffly to one side)

### In Adults

- Sore throat and dysphagia (often severe, out of proportion to visible pharyngeal findings)
- Neck stiffness and pain on neck extension
- Fever and toxaemia
- Drooling
- Trismus (if parapharyngeal space involved)
- Posterior pharyngeal wall swelling (visible on oral examination or flexible nasendoscopy)
- Stridor and respiratory distress (late)

### Potential Complications

- **Airway obstruction** — the most dangerous immediate complication
- **Mediastinitis** — descending infection via direct extension or the danger space
- **Aspiration pneumonia** — if abscess ruptures spontaneously
- **Jugular vein thrombosis** (Lemierre's syndrome)
- **Septicaemia and multi-organ failure**
- **Spinal osteomyelitis and epidural abscess** — if prevertebral fascia is breached

---

## PART D: Diagnosis

### Clinical Assessment

A high index of clinical suspicion is essential. The diagnosis should be considered in any child with fever, drooling, neck stiffness and a characteristic extended posture following an upper respiratory infection.

### Imaging

| Modality | Key Features |
|---|---|
| Lateral neck X-ray | Widened prevertebral soft tissue shadow (>7 mm at C2, >14 mm at C6 in children; >22 mm at C6 in adults); loss of normal cervical lordosis; air-fluid level |
| CT with contrast | Modality of choice; distinguishes abscess from cellulitis; defines extent and identifies complications; may require GA in small children |
| MRI | Rarely adds useful information acutely; useful for vertebral osteomyelitis or spinal cord involvement |
| Ultrasound | Limited role; may help identify cystic vs solid nature but not routinely used |

**Important:** Neither ultrasound nor CT is absolutely accurate in diagnosing an abscess — in the presence of strong clinical features, surgical exploration should be considered.

### Laboratory Investigations

- Full blood count (neutrophilia or leucopenia)
- CRP and ESR (elevated; useful for monitoring response to treatment)
- Blood cultures
- Pus for culture and sensitivity (including anaerobic culture)

---

## PART E: Management

### Principles

The child with suspected retropharyngeal abscess must be admitted for intravenous antibiotics, rehydration and careful observation. The key priorities are:

1. **Airway management** — maintain and protect the airway
2. **Medical therapy** — IV antibiotics
3. **Surgical drainage** — when indicated

### Airway Management

- Keep the child calm; avoid distressing examination
- Monitor for signs of airway compromise
- If intubation is required, the patient should be carefully intubated by a skilled paediatric anaesthetist
- Arrangements should be made for surgical drainage if indicated as soon as imaging has been completed
- [[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]] may be necessary if intubation is not possible

### Medical Management

- **IV antibiotics** — broad-spectrum, covering Streptococcus, Staphylococcus and anaerobes:
  - IV amoxicillin/clavulanate or co-amoxiclav
  - IV cefuroxime + metronidazole
  - Clindamycin (if penicillin-allergic; also covers MRSA and provides anti-toxin effect)
- **IV fluids and analgesia**
- **Serial clinical observation** — monitor for deterioration or signs of airway compromise
- Some cases of retropharyngeal cellulitis (without formed abscess) may resolve with IV antibiotics alone

### Surgical Drainage

**Indications:**
- Confirmed abscess on CT (rim-enhancing collection)
- Failure to improve with 48 hours of IV antibiotics
- Airway compromise
- Large abscess (>2 cm)

**Approaches:**

**1. Transoral (intraoral) drainage:**
- Patient positioned supine with slight head extension
- Endotracheal intubation secured first
- Oral cavity packed to prevent aspiration of pus
- Vertical incision through posterior pharyngeal wall mucosa at the site of maximal swelling
- Blunt dissection with sinus forceps into the abscess cavity
- Thorough suctioning of pus
- Send pus for culture and sensitivity (aerobic and anaerobic)

**2. External (transcervical) drainage:**
- Preferred for large, complex or laterally placed abscesses
- May be required for parapharyngeal space extension
- Incision along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid
- Dissection behind the carotid sheath to reach the retropharyngeal space
- Placement of drain

### Post-operative Care

- Continue IV antibiotics for 48–72 hours after drainage, then switch to oral antibiotics
- Monitor for recurrence or complications
- Nutritional support
- Follow-up imaging if clinical progress is not satisfactory
- Investigate for underlying cause in adults (e.g. foreign body, vertebral osteomyelitis, malignancy)

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The retropharyngeal space extends from the skull base to the posterior mediastinum — infection can spread to cause life-threatening mediastinitis
> 2. Retropharyngeal lymph nodes (nodes of Rouvière) are prominent in children aged 1–5 years and typically regress by adulthood — explaining the different aetiology in children vs adults
> 3. In children, retropharyngeal abscess typically follows suppuration of retropharyngeal lymph nodes secondary to upper respiratory infections
> 4. The characteristic posture is neck flexed with head extended — the child is febrile, drooling and in respiratory distress
> 5. CT with contrast is the imaging modality of choice — it distinguishes abscess from cellulitis and defines the extent of disease
> 6. Lateral neck X-ray shows widened prevertebral soft tissue shadow — but CT is required for definitive diagnosis and surgical planning
> 7. The danger space lies between the alar and prevertebral fascia, extending to the diaphragm — it represents the route by which retropharyngeal infection reaches the mediastinum
> 8. Airway management is the first priority — careful intubation by a skilled anaesthetist with arrangements for immediate surgical drainage
> 9. Transoral drainage is the standard approach — with endotracheal intubation secured first and oral cavity packed to prevent aspiration of pus
> 10. In adults, retropharyngeal lymphadenopathy should raise suspicion of head and neck malignancy (NPC, oropharyngeal, thyroid) — do not assume an infective cause


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A child with fever, drooling and neck stiffness in the 'sniffing position' after a recent upper respiratory infection should be assumed to have a retropharyngeal abscess until proven otherwise — admit and investigate urgently
> - Spontaneous rupture of a retropharyngeal abscess may cause aspiration pneumonia or sudden airway obstruction — the patient should be nursed with the head slightly raised and tilted forward
> - Neither ultrasound nor CT is absolutely accurate in diagnosing an abscess — if clinical features are strong, surgical exploration should be considered even if imaging is equivocal
> - The two parapharyngeal spaces communicate with the retropharyngeal space, creating a 'horseshoe' of interconnected spaces around the pharynx — infection in any one space may spread to involve all three
> - In adults, tuberculous retropharyngeal abscess (from Pott's disease of the cervical spine) presents insidiously with a 'cold abscess' lacking the typical signs of acute inflammation
