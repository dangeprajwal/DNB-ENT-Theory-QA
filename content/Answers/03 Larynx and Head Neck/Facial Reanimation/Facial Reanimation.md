## Part A: The Clinical Problem and Aims of Facial Reanimation

*Q21: Facial reanimation surgery aims. Techniques for rehabilitation after paralysis (2021 Jun)*

### The Clinical Problem

Facial paralysis causes a highly visible deformity with profound consequences from both a functional and psychosocial standpoint. The absence or dysfunction of facial musculature leads to:

**Functional problems:**
- **Eye**: Inability to close eyelids, risking corneal exposure, ulceration and blindness. Bell's phenomenon (upward rotation of globe on forced eye closure) is a significant risk factor for corneal exposure. Paralysis of orbicularis oculi disrupts the lacrimal pump apparatus, reducing tear drainage. Weakness may produce paralytic ectropion, allowing the lacrimal punctum to fall away from the globe, worsening tear drainage.
- **Mouth**: Oral incompetence producing disfiguring asymmetry and difficulty eating and drinking. Biting the inside of the mouth is a common complaint. Speech is affected — difficulty with plosive sounds ('b' and 'p').
- **Nose**: Overactivity of muscles on the normal side pulls the nasal tip and philtrum across the midline. Collapse of internal and external nasal valves due to paralysis of dilator nares and levator labii alequae nasi muscles.
- **Brow**: More commonly a problem in older patients. Weight of forehead pushes eyebrow down, producing asymmetry and obstructing upward gaze. Overactivity of contralateral frontalis worsens asymmetry.

**Psychosocial problems:**
- Lack of mimetic facial expression produces difficulties in communication
- Affects self-confidence, professional advancement and social interaction
- Can produce depression

### Aetiology of Facial Nerve Dysfunction (Table 95.1)

| Intracranial | Intratemporal | Extratemporal |
|---|---|---|
| Vascular abnormalities | Bacterial/viral infection | Malignant parotid tumour |
| CNS degenerative disease | [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]] | Trauma (lacerations, GSW) |
| Intracranial/brain tumour | Trauma (temporal bone fracture, GSW) | Iatrogenic |
| Congenital abnormalities/agenesis | Tumours invading middle ear/mastoid/FN | Primary FN tumour |
| | Iatrogenic | Skin/mandibular malignancy |

### Aims of Treatment

Treatment should be individualized. However, the aims of facial reanimation surgery are:

1. **Protection of the eye** — prevention of corneal exposure and blindness
2. **Symmetry at rest** — restoring facial balance
3. **Correction of synkinesis** — addressing aberrant muscle movements
4. **Oral continence** — preventing drooling and eating difficulties
5. **Dynamic facial movement** — restoring a smile
6. **Improvement of speech** — particularly plosive sounds
7. **Improvement of nasal deviation and airway patency** — correcting nasal valve collapse

### Physiology of Nerve Repair — Classification of Injuries

**Seddon's Classification:**

| Type | Description | Recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Neuropraxia | Temporary conduction block, no axon discontinuity | Complete recovery once cause removed |
| Axonotmesis | Axonal damage with Wallerian degeneration, endoneurium intact | Usually satisfactory; regeneration at 1-4 mm/day |
| Neurotmesis | Nerve fibre severed, endoneurium disrupted | Recovery unlikely |

**Sunderland's Classification:**

| Degree | Pathology | Prognosis |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Conduction delay only, no anatomical disruption | Full recovery |
| 2nd | Axonal disruption with Wallerian degeneration; endoneurium intact — axon regrows within same endoneurial tubule | Good recovery; same muscles reinnervated |
| 3rd | Endoneurium also disrupted — regenerating axons may enter any endoneurial tubule | Incomplete recovery; synkinesis/mass movement |
| 4th | Perineurium also disrupted | Decreased axonal regeneration; weak function + synkinesis |
| 5th | Epineurium also disrupted (complete transection) | No spontaneous recovery |

**Critical timeline**: Time from axonal degeneration to irretrievable muscle atrophy is approximately **18 months**, although successful reinnervation up to 20 years later has been reported. As it takes 6-9 months for nerve regeneration following nerve transfer, this must be factored into timing decisions.

### Assessment of the Patient

**House-Brackmann Grading Scale (Table 95.2):**

| Grade | Description | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| I | Normal | Normal function all areas |
| II | Mild dysfunction | Slight weakness on close inspection; may have slight synkinesis; complete eye closure with minimal effort |
| III | Moderate dysfunction | Obvious but not disfiguring difference; noticeable synkinesis/contracture; complete eye closure with effort |
| IV | Moderately severe | Obvious weakness/disfiguring asymmetry; no forehead movement; incomplete eye closure |
| V | Severe dysfunction | Barely perceptible motion; asymmetry at rest |
| VI | Total paralysis | No movement |

**Sunnybrook Facial Grading System (Table 95.3):** Allows assessment of resting symmetry, voluntary movement and synkinesis — particularly useful for tracking recovery or progression.

**Clinical assessment** should include:
- Brow position and forehead rhytids
- Palpebral aperture measurement
- Bell's phenomenon testing
- Lower lid snap test (lid pulled away and released — audible snap confirms adequate tone)
- Lower lid distraction test (>6 mm from globe on traction = laxity)
- Inferior canalicular punctum position
- Nasal deviation and valve collapse on forced inspiration
- Static asymmetry (philtrum shift, nasolabial fold depth, jowling, platysmal bands)
- Active movements (oral commissure excursion, degree of synkinesis)
- Rubin Smile Classification

### Investigations

- **MRI**: Only modality demonstrating facial nerve comprehensively from pons to parotid gland; with gadolinium shows inflammatory changes
- **CT temporal bone**: Bony detail, middle ear cleft involvement
- **Electrophysiological tests**: Testing delayed 72 hours post onset (for Wallerian degeneration); repeated every 3-5 days up to 21 days; most clinically helpful for objectively assessing function once spontaneous motion is lost

## Part B: Non-Surgical Management

### Eye Management
- Adequate ocular lubricants during the day
- Taping lids closed at night to prevent desiccation
- Eye irritation mandates ophthalmology review

### Facial Therapy
The cornerstone of management of facial paralysis. Neuromuscular retraining by experienced therapist can:
- Improve aberrant activity on the unaffected side
- Treat synkinesis and hypertonicity on the affected side
- Modalities include biofeedback, electromyography and self-directed mirror exercises
- Strong consensus exists for the importance of facial therapy in multidisciplinary management
- Evidence that tailored facial exercises improve function in moderate paralysis and chronic cases, and reduce recovery time and negative sequelae in acute cases

## Part C: Surgical Management — Techniques for Rehabilitation

### 1. Brow

**Direct brow lift**: Provides long-lasting correction in elderly patients with loss of skin elasticity and brow descent obstructing vision.

**Other techniques**: Endoscopic brow-lifts, transblepharoplasty brow-pexy (useful for small ptoses in partial weakness but limited in profound palsy).

**Botulinum toxin**: Injections to contralateral frontalis to reduce asymmetry — requires careful discussion with patient.

### 2. Upper Eyelid

**Gold weight eyelid loading (lid loading):**
- Specially made gold weights, smoothly rounded, fixed to the tarsal plate
- Fixation to tarsal plate is essential (significantly reduces extrusion and migration)
- Use as light a weight as will effectively close the eye — rarely more than 1 g required
- 1.5 g weights are heavy and eventually the levator weakens, producing ptosis
- Closure sufficient to cover the cornea reported in **78% of cases**
- Resolution of keratitis in **62-100% of cases**
- Easy to insert and largely reliable

**Other methods for lagophthalmos:**
- Lengthening the levator by division or inserting a strip of temporalis fascia
- Insertion of magnets in upper and lower lids
- Morel-Fatio palpebral spring
- Classic Gillies procedure (transfer of temporalis muscle slips to eyelids) — fallen out of favour due to unpredictability compared with simpler lid loading

**Congenital vs acquired palsy considerations:**
- In congenital unilateral palsy in children, upper lid weighting is not required — children seem to have minimal corneal exposure problems and dislike the narrowed palpebral fissure
- In acquired adult palsy, eye protection is mandatory immediately

### 3. Lower Eyelid

- **McLaughlin's lateral tarsorrhaphy**: Aesthetic and effective for minor degrees of scleral show; large procedures narrow the palpebral fissure excessively
- **Palmaris tendon sling**: Between medial canthal ligament and supraorbital margin laterally — provides good elevation
- **Conchal cartilage grafts**: Set between infraorbital margin and lower edge of tarsal plate to support the lower eyelid

### 4. Nasal Airway

- Elevation of nasal base by tendon slings fixed to maxillary periosteum
- Septal deviations, if present, should be corrected
- Supportive cartilage grafts to alar rim

### 5. Smile Reconstruction — Static Techniques

For patients who want to look symmetrical in photographs and/or are not candidates for more complex procedures or prolonged recovery:

**Fascial/tendon slings:**
- **Fascia lata** (from lateral thigh) — most commonly used
- **Tendon grafts** (plantaris or palmaris longus) — said to stretch less over time
- Grafts inserted into the corner of mouth at nasolabial fold
- Anchored to deep temporal fossa or zygomatic periosteum using permanent sutures or Mitek Bone Anchors
- Contralateral normal side used as guide for vector
- Sufficient tension to just overcorrect to allow for post-operative relaxation
- Can restore a surprising degree of resting facial symmetry

### 6. Smile Reconstruction — Dynamic Techniques

#### Regional Muscle Transfers

- Transfer of muscles of mastication to mouth to replace lost zygomaticus/levator anguli oris function
- **Advantages**: Immediate results (no reinnervation required)
- **Disadvantages**: Produces voluntary rather than spontaneous smile; abnormal movements during chewing

**Masseter transfer**: Produces little movement and an unsightly cheek bulge.

**Temporalis transfer (Gillies technique)**: Turnover of strips of temporalis muscle and deep temporal fascia over the zygoma, inset into corner of mouth and/or eyelids. May produce unsightly bulge over zygoma.

**Prograde temporalis transfer (Labbé and Huault)**: More recently popular technique. Regional muscle transfers have now largely been superseded by free muscle transfers.

#### Free Muscle Transfers (Microsurgical Functioning Muscle Transfer)

Has become the **mainstay of facial reanimation** in the developed world over the past three decades.

**Muscles used:**
- **Gracilis** and **pectoralis minor** — most popular choices
- **Latissimus dorsi** and **rectus abdominis** — for single-stage procedures (require long nerve segment)

**Two-Stage Procedure (Cross-Facial Nerve Graft — CFNG):**

*Stage 1:*
1. Comprehensive exploration of contralateral facial nerve branches
2. A redundant buccal branch selected as donor nerve using bipolar nerve stimulation
3. Sural nerve graft harvested from the leg
4. Graft placed onto redundant buccal branch and tunnelled across midline to the paralyzed side
5. Growth of facial nerve followed clinically by Tinel's sign

*Stage 2 (6-9 months later, after successful regeneration):*
1. Free functioning muscle transfer (usually gracilis)
2. Muscle inset into nasolabial fold and commissure of the mouth
3. Revascularized using facial vessels or superficial temporal vessels
4. **Advantage**: Potential for spontaneous smile (innervation from contralateral facial nerve provides emotional drive)

**Single-Stage Procedure (Masseteric Nerve):**

Use of the masseteric branch of CN V (trigeminal) to power muscle transplants in a single operative stage has recently become widely used.

**Advantages of masseteric nerve:**
- Single-stage procedure (no 6-9 month wait)
- Greater axonal load delivered to the muscle
- Stronger contraction of transplanted muscle
- More normal excursion of the mouth
- Preserves the contralateral facial nerve
- Allows a more normal reconstructed smile

**Disadvantage**: Need to relearn to smile by biting.

**Important finding**: Although initially thought that only cross-facial nerve graft could produce spontaneous facial activity, spontaneous activity has been observed in a significant proportion of masseteric nerve transplants. This is ascribed to cortical plasticity and also the observation that the normal masseteric nerve shows activity during smiling on EMG studies.

### 7. Lower Lip

- **Anterior belly of digastric muscle transfer**: For dynamic reanimation of lower lip (variable results)
- **Platysmal muscle transfer**: Also variable results
- **Selective weakening of contralateral depressor labii inferioris**: Simpler and more predictable
  - Temporarily by Botox injection
  - Permanently by direct excision through intra-oral approach

### 8. Adjuncts

Other cosmetic interventions as required:
- Facelifts
- Midface lifts
- Rhinoplasty
- Correction of platysmal bands

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Facial paralysis produces functional problems (corneal exposure, oral incompetence, nasal valve collapse, speech difficulty) and profound psychosocial consequences affecting communication, self-confidence and social interaction.
>
> 2. The aims of facial reanimation are: eye protection, symmetry at rest, correction of synkinesis, oral continence, dynamic facial movement, speech improvement and nasal airway patency.
>
> 3. Sunderland's classification grades nerve injury from 1st degree (conduction delay only) to 5th degree (complete transection including epineurium). Seddon's classification: neuropraxia (temporary), axonotmesis (Wallerian degeneration but endoneurium intact, regeneration at 1-4 mm/day), neurotmesis (nerve severed, recovery unlikely).
>
> 4. The time from axonal degeneration to irretrievable muscle atrophy is approximately 18 months. Since nerve transfer takes 6-9 months for regeneration, this must be factored into surgical timing decisions.
>
> 5. The House-Brackmann scale (Grades I-VI) and Sunnybrook Facial Grading System are used to document severity and track recovery. Electrophysiological testing is delayed 72 hours post onset and repeated every 3-5 days.
>
> 6. Gold weight eyelid loading is the standard for upper lid management: fixation to tarsal plate essential, rarely more than 1 g required, 78% achieve corneal coverage, 62-100% resolution of keratitis.
>
> 7. Static techniques (fascia lata or tendon slings anchored to deep temporal fossa/zygomatic periosteum) provide resting symmetry but no dynamic movement — suitable for patients not candidates for complex procedures.
>
> 8. Free functioning muscle transfer (gracilis or pectoralis minor) has become the mainstay of facial reanimation: the two-stage cross-facial nerve graft procedure uses a contralateral redundant buccal branch and sural nerve graft, followed 6-9 months later by muscle transfer.
>
> 9. The single-stage masseteric nerve procedure (CN V) provides greater axonal load, stronger contraction, more normal excursion and preserves the contralateral facial nerve — with the disadvantage of needing to relearn smiling by biting. Spontaneous smiling has been observed due to cortical plasticity.
>
> 10. Neuromuscular retraining (biofeedback, EMG, mirror exercises) is the cornerstone of facial paralysis management and should be part of all multidisciplinary treatment plans.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A diagnosis of idiopathic [[Facial Nerve|Bell's palsy]] should only be made with a classical history — any atypical features should prompt further investigation. MRI is the only modality demonstrating the facial nerve comprehensively from pons to parotid.
>
> - Gold weights heavier than 1 g (especially 1.5 g) should be avoided — the levator eventually weakens, producing iatrogenic ptosis. In congenital facial palsy in children, upper lid weighting is actively disliked and unnecessary.
>
> - The lower lid snap test (pulling lid away from globe and releasing — audible snap confirms tone) and distraction test (>6 mm = laxity) are essential components of the examination.
>
> - Free muscle transfer innervated by masseteric nerve is increasingly preferred over the two-stage cross-facial nerve graft procedure due to single-stage surgery, stronger contraction and observed spontaneous smiling from cortical plasticity.
>
> - For lower lip asymmetry, selective weakening of the contralateral depressor labii inferioris (Botox or intraoral excision) provides a simpler and more predictable result than dynamic muscle transfer.
>
> - Reinnervation can only succeed for approximately 18 months following onset of paralysis — early referral to a specialist facial reanimation team is critical for optimal outcomes.
