## Part A: Foreign Bodies in the Airway — Types, Incidence & Presentation

### Incidence and Epidemiology

Foreign body incidents in the EU in children aged 0–14 years number 50,000 per annum, with 1% being fatal. The largest active registry is the Susy Safe project. Up to 22% of patients have a repeat foreign body incident, emphasizing the importance of education.

**Distribution by anatomical site (Susy Safe project, 8593 incidents):**
- Ear: 25.9%
- Nose: 27.4%
- Pharynx and larynx: 4.3%
- Trachea, bronchus and lungs: 12.8%
- Mouth, oesophagus and stomach: 29.7%

Foreign bodies were food in 26% of cases. For food-related foreign bodies specifically, the distribution shifts dramatically: trachea/bronchus/lungs constitute 50%, while ear drops to 7%.

### Types of Foreign Bodies in the Air Passage

Most inhaled foreign bodies occur in the under 3 years age group and are more common in boys than girls.

**By Age:**
- Children < 3 years: typically organic (food) — peanuts, seeds, beans
- Children > 5 years: more often inorganic — plastic toys, beads, pins

**By Nature:**
- **Organic:** Peanuts (most common and most dangerous — oil release causes granulation), dried beans (swell and cause inflammation), seeds, food particles
- **Inorganic:** Coins, pins, safety pins, plastic parts, beads, toy components
- **Special hazards:** Button batteries (corrosive injury within 2–2.5 hours), magnets (attraction across tissues causing perforation)

**Risk Factors:**
- Lack of molar teeth (reduced ability to chew food) in young children
- Tendency of young children to explore with their mouths
- Impaired behavioural development, neurological conditions, ADHD
- In adults: poor dentition, alcohol, old age, neurological/psychological factors

### Location within the Airway

From meta-analysis, in decreasing frequency: bronchus (right more frequent than left) > trachea > larynx > lung. Laryngeal foreign bodies are relatively infrequent (2–12% of cases).

### Clinical Presentation

Inhaled foreign body presentation varies with the exact site of obstruction:

**Symptoms and Signs:**
- Choking episode (often witnessed — parent present in 49% of incidents, child was eating in 34%)
- Coughing (persistent, unexplained)
- Hoarseness (laryngeal FB)
- Shortness of breath, wheeze
- Increased work of breathing
- Stridor (any level within the large respiratory airway)
- Cyanosis
- Asphyxiation and death

**Important Clinical Points:**
- Even if the child is symptom-free, if no object is fully expelled by coughing after a choking episode, further evaluation is indicated
- A low threshold is essential so that the asymptomatic or occult foreign body is not missed
- Delayed presentation is not uncommon — in almost 40% of cases, diagnosis was delayed for more than 24 hours
- May be misdiagnosed and treated as asthma due to low-grade cough and noisy breathing
- If present for < 1 month: all patients recover completely; if present > 3 months: only 30% recover (remainder develop chronic cough/wheeze or bronchiectasis)

**Laryngeal Foreign Bodies:**
- Partial obstruction: hoarseness, stridor, dyspnoea, prolonged atypical croup, odynophagia — may be misdiagnosed
- Complete obstruction: hypoxia, laryngospasm, death by asphyxiation — requires the Heimlich manoeuvre
- Shape is either spheroid or flat
- Sharp metallic items (safety pins) that penetrate the vocal cord or tracheal wall present a surgical challenge

### Radiological Investigation

- Chest X-ray: often normal (11–26%) unless a radio-opaque foreign body is present
- Features when abnormal: atelectasis, hyperinflation (ball-valve effect), mediastinal shift, pneumonia, pneumothorax
- **Ball-valve effect:** On inspiration, intrathoracic pressure decreases allowing air to enter past the FB. On expiration, intrathoracic pressure increases compressing the bronchus around the FB, trapping air → hyperinflation of the affected lung
- Sensitivity and specificity of radiographs for airway FBs: 73% and 45% respectively — should NOT deter an airway endoscopy
- Fluoroscopy improves detection but still does not identify a significant proportion
- **CT virtual bronchoscopy:** Highly sensitive at identifying FB within the airway, but if this requires GA, most would consider rigid bronchoscopy directly as the patient's condition may change suddenly while anaesthetized. In asymptomatic patients, CT virtual bronchoscopy may be a reasonable option but cannot replace endoscopy

### First Aid — Heimlich Manoeuvre

Published by Heimlich in 1975 to expel laryngeal foreign bodies. This manoeuvre may be responsible for reducing deaths from FB inhalation in the under-15 age group. Complications include oesophageal-gastric rupture, diaphragmatic hernia, and subcutaneous/mediastinal emphysema — but doing nothing commands a greater risk.

Children are often able to clear their own airways, but this ability is less well developed in younger children. In one report, 85% of inhaled objects were cleared before emergency services arrived.

## Part B: Management of Inhaled Foreign Bodies — Bronchoscopy

### Rigid vs Flexible Bronchoscopy

**Rigid bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia is the mainstay for foreign body removal in children.** Some surgeons advocate flexible bronchoscopy via a laryngeal mask or endotracheal tube with very high success rates. However:

| Parameter | Rigid Bronchoscopy | Flexible Bronchoscopy |
|---|---|---|
| Ventilation | Can ventilate patient during procedure | Cannot ventilate; causes significant obstruction |
| Anaesthesia | General anaesthesia (controlled) | Possible without GA in older children/adults |
| FB removal | Superior for most FB types; multiple instruments available | Flexible approach angle; urological basket can be used |
| Safety in children | Mainstay — controlled conditions | Limited; role as adjunct |
| Airway control | Full airway control maintained | Limited airway control |

**Flexible bronchoscopy role:** Genuine value in the repertoire for FB removal, especially when rigid bronchoscopy has failed (using urological basket) and for diagnostic purposes.

### Microlaryngotracheobronchoscopy (MLTB) — Technique

MLTB should be performed to diagnose and remove the foreign body without undue delay. The level of urgency depends on the clinical condition and resources available.

**Timing:**
- Best performed during daylight hours
- If child presents during evening with possible FB inhalation and is well, it is reasonable to delay endoscopy until morning (somewhat controversial)
- **Exceptions requiring urgent intervention:** unstable symptoms, peanut (oil release causes granulation), broken seeds, dried beans (cause swelling and inflammation), batteries

**Anaesthetic Technique for MLTB:**
- Best performed using **inhalation anaesthesia** (sevoflurane — non-irritant, rapid onset, no pungency) — associated with fewer complications
- Best performed **without endotracheal intubation** in children — small risk that FB lodged at larynx/subglottis may be pushed further by ETT causing complete airway obstruction
- The rigid bronchoscope should be ready for use
- **Paralysis is NOT advised** until the airway has been assessed and secured
- Topical anaesthetic on the larynx prevents laryngospasm
- Spontaneous respiration maintains muscle tone, promotes gas exchange, and is essential to detect dynamic conditions

**Key Procedural Points:**
- Topical adrenaline useful to decongest the area around the FB, especially if granulation is present with inherent bleeding risk
- On successful removal — immediate second look is necessary to ensure no retained fragments
- Steroids typically administered to reduce oedema from airway instrumentation
- Most patients require post-operative observation until the following day

### Alternative Strategies When Endoscopy Fails

If it is impossible to remove an FB using endoscopy:
- **Tracheal fissure** (as [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] incision) for tracheal foreign bodies
- **Thoracotomy** for bronchial foreign bodies
- ECMO may be useful to maintain oxygenation when the patient is too unstable and tracheal FB removal risks complete airway obstruction and cardiac arrest

### Complications of Endoscopic FB Removal

Life-threatening complications occur in up to 4% of cases:
- Pneumothorax (unilateral and bilateral) — can also occur as delayed complication post-operatively
- Tracheal laceration
- Pneumonia
- Haemorrhage into the airway
- Cardiac arrest due to hypoxia

**Risk factors for hypoxia during MLTB:**
- Child less than 1 year of age
- Certain types of foreign body (seeds, organic material)
- Longer duration of procedure (indicates increased difficulty)
- Pre-existing pneumonia
- Fruit seeds

**Mortality:** Death during endoscopy for FB removal reported at 0.42%, with more recent USA nationwide data indicating nearer 1.8%.

**A negative MLTB is not a failure** — the threshold to suspect FB inhalation must be low. The negative rate has been reported as high as 60%.

## Part C: Paediatric Bronchoscopy — Indications, Techniques & Equipment

### Equipment Standards (Box 28.4)

Essential equipment for paediatric airway endoscopy includes:
- Adjustable laryngeal suspension system
- Operating microscope with 400f lens
- Selection of age-appropriate paediatric laryngoscopes of varying size and viewing angles
- Dedicated laryngeal microsurgical instruments (microscissors, probes, cupped and straight forceps)
- Selection of age-appropriate **ventilating bronchoscopes** with Hopkins rod telescopes to fit
- Suitable light source with fibre-optic cable
- Camera and monitor with image-capture facilities (video and still)
- Selection of forceps for removal of foreign bodies
- Optical forceps with rigid telescope(s) of corresponding length
- Selection of fibre-optic bronchoscopes and nasopharyngoscope
- Full range of paediatric tracheostomy tubes

### Ventilating Bronchoscope

The ventilating bronchoscope provides a means of actively ventilating the patient if required. It consists of a hollow tube with:
- Holes for ventilation
- A bevelled tip
- Working channel for suction/instruments
- Connection for anaesthetic gas
- Port for Hopkins rod telescope insertion
- Light source connections

With spontaneous ventilation, a smaller diameter rigid Hopkins rod telescope can be used with less trauma and reduced splinting of the airway.

### Indications for Paediatric Bronchoscopy

1. **Foreign body removal** — the primary indication in acute setting
2. **Assessment of stridor** — laryngotracheobronchoscopy is the gold standard
3. **Evaluation of airway stenosis** — [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]], tracheal stenosis
4. **Tracheobronchomalacia** assessment (must be observed without positive airway pressure)
5. **Recurrent pneumonia** — to exclude anatomical cause
6. **Suspected congenital airway anomalies** — webs, cysts, clefts
7. **Persistent atelectasis** — to exclude bronchial obstruction
8. **Haemoptysis** in the paediatric age group
9. **Therapeutic** — removal of secretions, granulation tissue

### Laryngotracheobronchoscopy (LTBS) Technique

**Positioning:** Small sandbag or pad under shoulders; sandbags laterally to support the head. Mayo table supports the laryngostat clear of the chest.

**Laryngeal Examination:**
- Insert lubricated suspension laryngoscope gently, protecting teeth and lips, keeping tongue central
- Check overall appearance of pharynx and supraglottis during introduction
- Gently lift epiglottis forward (ensure it does not curl up preventing anterior commissure view)
- Examine with ETT in situ for overall assessment, then with ETT removed for superior view
- Use a probe to move arytenoids independently to assess cricoarytenoid joint mobility
- Exclude posterior laryngeal cleft by passing probe between arytenoids
- Pass through vocal cords with great care if using a rigid endoscope to inspect the subglottis

**Bronchoscopy:**
- Use ventilating bronchoscope or smaller Hopkins rod telescope under video control
- Subglottis, trachea, carina, and main bronchi systematically examined
- Tracheomalacia must be observed with a small bronchoscope withdrawn from the area without positive airways pressure to avoid splinting
- Record videographs or digital images

**Documentation:** Minimum "four-shot view" of:
1. Supraglottis
2. Glottis
3. Subglottis and trachea
4. Carina and main bronchi

Video recording of dynamic pathologies (vocal cord movement, [[Stridor and Laryngomalacia|laryngomalacia]], tracheobronchomalacia) is recommended.

### Anaesthetic Considerations for Paediatric Airway Endoscopy

**Induction:**
- Premedication: atropine or glycopyrrhonium bromide (dry field, improve topical anaesthesia efficacy)
- Pre-operative steroids if significant stenosis or increased inflammation suspected
- IV induction for older children; gas induction for infants, younger children, poor IV access, precarious airway
- Topical lignocaine applied to vocal cords (carefully measured to avoid overdosage in children)

**Maintenance:**
- Two techniques: volatile agent (sevoflurane) or TIVA (propofol + remifentanil)
- Sevoflurane: non-irritant, rapid onset, high concentrations possible; rapid termination during hypoventilation
- Most units use spontaneous respiration rather than paralysis
- Advantages of spontaneous respiration: maintains muscle tone, promotes gas exchange, essential to detect dynamic conditions

**Airway tube technique:** ETT used as nasopharyngeal airway/"prong" to provide O2 and volatile agent. Advantage: view of airway not altered by ETT passage. Airway control can be regained with intubation or bronchoscope at any time.

**Jet ventilation:** Allows paralysis, prevents coughing/gagging. Risk of barotrauma (pneumothorax, air trapping) in children — few centres use this in paediatrics. Dynamic conditions (malacia, cord palsy) cannot be well identified.

**LMA:** Useful for fibre-optic tracheobronchoscopy, particularly if patient is difficult to intubate (mandibular hypoplasia).

## Part D: Button Battery Foreign Bodies — Risks, Presentation & Management

### Epidemiology

There has been a significant and increasing rise in battery FB incidents since the earliest reports in the 1970s (camera mercury battery removed by gastrostomy). Between 1985 and 2009 there was a **6.7-fold increase** in battery FB incidents, attributed to increased usage of small electronic devices, the size/shape/smoothness of button batteries, and the extent of injury due to battery power output. Male preponderance (57%).

**Anatomical distribution (Susy Safe project, 348 battery cases):**
- Mouth, oesophagus, stomach: 188/348
- Nose: 112/348
- Ear: 44/348
- Inhaled (tracheobronchial): only 1 case

### Mechanisms of Tissue Injury

Batteries cause harm through four mechanisms:

1. **Electrical circuit formation:** Soft tissue forms an electrical circuit between the terminals (sides) of the button battery, **hydrolyzing tissue by forming hydroxide at the negative terminal** — this is the most important mechanism
2. **Alkaline leakage:** Leakage of alkaline content from the battery in a saline environment
3. **Pressure necrosis:** If compressed at a particular site (e.g., oesophageal narrowing)
4. **Release of toxic compounds:** e.g., mercury, causing systemic toxicity

**Critical Facts:**
- Burns occur within **2–2.5 hours** of ingestion
- The majority of batteries are the alkaline variety
- **Highest risk:** 20 mm battery in children under 4 years old
- **54% of fatal cases were misdiagnosed** — emphasizing the importance of urgent X-ray investigation and removal
- Temperature and pH rise when battery is ingested

### Clinical Presentation

- **30% had dysphagia**
- **26% had fever and cough** (perhaps surprisingly)
- May present with drooling, pain, refusal to eat
- In nose: immediate discharge, septal ulceration
- In ear: skin ulceration, serosanguinous discharge, tympanic membrane perforation

### Investigation

- **Urgent X-ray** is mandatory
- **X-ray feature of button batteries:** A **double contour** (step-off sign or "halo" appearance) — distinguishes from a coin which has a single smooth edge
- The 20 mm battery gets stuck at sites of anatomical narrowing (e.g., aortic arch level in oesophagus)
- If the battery is past the stomach, serial abdominal X-rays may be appropriate (will likely pass spontaneously)

### Management

**Button batteries require removal WITHOUT DELAY — the sun should never set on a battery FB.**

**Surgical Management:**
1. **Pharyngo-oesophagoscopy** (rigid) for battery retrieval from the oesophagus
2. MLTB is also of value during pharyngo-oesophagoscopy to rule out **tracheo-oesophageal fistula** or prodromal granulation within the airway
3. If the battery has become embedded in the oesophageal wall surrounded by dense granulation (as reported in a case of 8-month impaction), an **external approach (posterolateral thoracotomy)** may be needed

**Post-operative Management:**
- Initial post-op chest X-ray to identify pneumothorax (~0.8%) or pneumomediastinum
- Options include: nasogastric tube, proton pump inhibitors, repeat endoscopy, contrast swallow a few days to a week later
- Steroids have NOT been shown to confer clinical benefit following caustic ingestion (despite theoretical benefit of reducing fibrosis/stricture)
- NG tube placement serves for nutrition and perhaps stenting, but blind placement in a corroded oesophagus may present increased risk

### Complications of Button Battery in the Oesophagus

- Oesophageal perforation
- Haemorrhage (potentially fatal — erosion into aorta)
- [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|Vocal cord palsy]]
- Ulceration and necrosis
- Tracheo-oesophageal fistula
- Oesophageal stricture
- Death

### Button Battery in Nose and Ear

- **Nasal:** Septal ulceration, [[Septal Perforation Repair|septal perforation]], inferior turbinate ulceration and necrosis — require removal without delay
- **Ear:** Skin ulceration, serosanguinous discharge, tympanic membrane perforation

## Part E: Ingested Foreign Bodies — Rigid vs Flexible Endoscopy

### Types of Ingested Foreign Bodies

Peak incidence in children less than 4 years old (up to 75%). The most common item ingested is the coin (up to 70% of cases). Symptoms include drooling, pain, dysphagia, and odynophagia.

**Sites of lodgement (reducing order):**
- Fish/chicken bones: tonsil > tongue base > cricopharyngeus > pharyngeal wall
- Larger items: cricopharyngeus > upper oesophagus above aortic arch
- Underlying oesophageal pathology (stricture, Schatzki ring, eosinophilic oesophagitis) can cause FB impaction

### Comparison: Rigid Oesophagoscopy vs Flexible Endoscopy

| Parameter | Rigid Oesophagoscopy | Flexible Endoscopy |
|---|---|---|
| Perforation rate | Higher: 0.2–1.2% | Lower: 0.02–0.05% |
| Success rate | Similar | Similar |
| Anaesthesia | General anaesthesia required | Can be done under sedation |
| Best for | Pharynx and cervical oesophagus; sharp FBs | Investigation of choice; allows therapeutic intervention |
| Control | Direct visualization and manipulation | Better for distal oesophagus |
| Disadvantages | Higher perforation rate | Cannot access pharynx well |

**Key Points:**
- Flexible endoscopy is the investigation of choice and allows therapeutic intervention with a lower risk of perforation
- Rigid oesophagoscopy in experienced hands is appropriate for impacted objects in the pharynx and cervical oesophagus
- **Pushing a food bolus distally without having first passed the endoscope beyond the bolus should be avoided** — increases risk of perforation
- Early intervention reduces complications, particularly for sharp FBs such as bones and pins
- Button batteries and sharp objects are indications for urgent intervention

### Complications of Ingested FBs

- Perforation (pharyngeal or oesophageal)
- Mediastinal infection/abscess
- [[Retropharyngeal Abscess|Retropharyngeal abscess]]
- Oesophageal stenosis
- Delaying intervention beyond 24 hours may increase duration of therapeutic endoscopy

### Rigid Bronchoscope vs Oesophagoscope — Key Differences

| Feature | Rigid Bronchoscope | Rigid Oesophagoscope |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Airway FB removal; airway assessment | Oesophageal/pharyngeal FB removal |
| Design | Ventilating; ports for gas delivery | Larger lumen; no ventilation ports |
| Tip | Bevelled (for passing through cords) | Open (for grasping FBs) |
| Ventilation | Can ventilate the patient | No ventilation capability |
| Telescopes | Hopkins rod for visualization | Direct vision or telescope |
| Working channel | For suction/instruments | Larger for instrument passage |
| Location of use | Larynx, trachea, bronchi | Pharynx, cricopharyngeus, oesophagus |

### Anaesthetic Challenges in Foreign Body Removal

1. **Shared airway:** Both anaesthetist and surgeon share the airway — requires high level of communication and cooperation
2. **Risk of complete airway obstruction:** FB may be dislodged during induction or intubation, converting partial to complete obstruction
3. **Cannot paralyse initially:** Paralysis not advised until airway assessed and secured
4. **Inhalation anaesthesia preferred:** Sevoflurane allows spontaneous respiration; maintains muscle tone; essential for detecting dynamic conditions
5. **Risk of laryngospasm:** Topical anaesthetic on larynx essential to prevent this
6. **Hypoxia risk:** Higher in children < 1 year, with organic FBs, longer procedures, pre-existing pneumonia
7. **Balance between access and safety:** Ventilating bronchoscope provides ventilation but is larger; Hopkins rod telescope causes less trauma but does not ventilate
8. **Post-removal monitoring:** Most patients require overnight observation; delayed pneumothorax can occur

## Part F: Special Topics

### Air Bronchogram Sign

The air bronchogram sign is a radiological sign seen on chest X-ray or CT where air-filled bronchi (appearing dark/lucent) become visible within opacified/consolidated lung parenchyma. Normally, air-filled bronchi are not visible against the aerated lung parenchyma. When the surrounding alveoli are filled with fluid/exudate (as in pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, or atelectasis), the air-filled bronchi stand out as dark branching structures against the white (opacified) lung.

**Significance in Context of Foreign Bodies:**
- In atelectasis from FB obstruction, air bronchograms may be absent if the bronchus itself is obstructed (resorptive atelectasis)
- Presence of air bronchograms suggests non-obstructive cause (pneumonia, surfactant deficiency)
- Absence of air bronchograms in a collapsed lobe raises suspicion for endobronchial obstruction (FB, tumour, mucus plug)

**Clinical Use:**
- Differentiating causes of lung opacification
- Atelectasis from FB obstruction typically shows NO air bronchograms (complete bronchial obstruction)
- Pneumonic consolidation typically SHOWS air bronchograms (bronchi remain patent)

### Virtual Bronchoscopy

CT virtual bronchoscopy uses helical/multidetector CT with multiplanar and 3D reconstruction to provide non-invasive visualization of the tracheobronchial tree. It creates a computer-generated endoluminal view similar to what would be seen during actual bronchoscopy.

**Characteristics:**
- Highly sensitive at identifying foreign bodies within the airway
- Non-invasive imaging modality
- Generated from CT data using software reconstruction

**Advantages:**
- Can detect FBs that are radiolucent on plain X-ray
- Useful in asymptomatic patients where clinical suspicion exists
- Avoids risks of endoscopy in the assessment phase
- Better definition of fixed tracheal lesions

**Limitations:**
- If it requires general anaesthesia in a child, most would proceed directly to rigid bronchoscopy (patient's condition may change suddenly while anaesthetized)
- Cannot replace endoscopy — a diagnostic and therapeutic tool
- Cannot assess dynamic changes (primary and secondary tracheobronchomalacia not well evaluated)
- In asymptomatic patients, it may be a reasonable screening option
- Does not allow therapeutic intervention

### Dysphagia Lusoria

Dysphagia lusoria refers to dysphagia caused by **vascular compression of the oesophagus** due to malformation of the great vessels. The term "lusoria" derives from "lusus naturae" (freak of nature).

**Causes:**
- **Aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria)** — most common cause. The aberrant artery arises from the aortic arch distal to the left subclavian artery and courses behind the oesophagus, compressing it posteriorly
- **Double aortic arch** — forms a complete vascular ring around the trachea and oesophagus
- **Anomalous left pulmonary artery (pulmonary sling)** — passes between the trachea and oesophagus

**Clinical Features:**
- Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing, particularly solids)
- May present in infancy or adulthood
- Stridor may occur if tracheal compression is present (especially double aortic arch)
- Many patients are asymptomatic

**Diagnosis:**
- Extrinsic compression noted on **barium swallow** (posterior indentation of the oesophagus)
- **CT angiography or MR angiography** is diagnostic — defines the vascular anomaly precisely
- Echocardiography can demonstrate most abnormal vasculature

**Management:**
- Conservative if asymptomatic
- Surgical division of the aberrant vessel or vascular ring if symptomatic

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Most inhaled foreign bodies occur in children under 3 years; they are organic (food) in this age group and inorganic in older children; right bronchus is the most common site
> 2. Rigid bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia is the mainstay for foreign body removal in children — flexible bronchoscopy cannot ventilate the patient and presents a significant obstruction
> 3. MLTB is best performed using inhalation anaesthesia (sevoflurane) without endotracheal intubation and without paralysis until the airway is assessed and secured
> 4. Button batteries cause tissue injury within 2–2.5 hours primarily through hydroxide formation at the negative terminal (electrical hydrolysis) — the highest risk is a 20 mm battery in children under 4 years
> 5. Button battery X-ray shows a double contour (step-off sign) distinguishing it from a coin; 54% of fatal cases were misdiagnosed, emphasizing urgency of X-ray and removal
> 6. Complications of button battery in the oesophagus include perforation, haemorrhage, vocal cord palsy, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, and death — MLTB should be done during oesophagoscopy to rule out TOF
> 7. Rigid oesophagoscopy has a higher perforation rate (0.2–1.2%) than flexible endoscopy (0.02–0.05%) but both have similar success rates; flexible endoscopy is the investigation of choice for most ingested FBs
> 8. Chest X-ray for inhaled FB may be normal in 11–26% of cases; sensitivity 73%, specificity 45% — a normal X-ray should NOT deter bronchoscopy with appropriate history
> 9. CT virtual bronchoscopy is highly sensitive for FB detection but cannot replace endoscopy; best used as screening in asymptomatic patients
> 10. Dysphagia lusoria is caused by vascular compression of the oesophagus, most commonly by aberrant right subclavian artery; diagnosed on barium swallow (posterior oesophageal indentation) and CT/MR angiography
> 11. Air bronchogram sign: air-filled bronchi visible within opacified lung; absence in a collapsed lobe suggests endobronchial obstruction (FB, tumour)
> 12. Complications of endoscopic FB removal include pneumothorax, tracheal laceration, pneumonia, airway haemorrhage, and cardiac arrest (up to 4% life-threatening complication rate)

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - "The sun should never rise or set on a button battery foreign body" — removal must be immediate; burns occur within 2–2.5 hours
> - A negative MLTB is NOT a failure — the threshold for suspecting FB inhalation must be low; negative rate reported as high as 60%
> - If FB present < 1 month: all patients recover completely; if > 3 months: only 30% recover — the remainder develop bronchiectasis or chronic wheeze
> - In children, do NOT intubate before MLTB for FB — the ETT may push a laryngeal/subglottic FB causing complete airway obstruction
> - Always perform a "second look" after FB removal to ensure no retained fragments
> - Peanuts are particularly dangerous because oil release causes granulation formation — they require urgent removal; dried beans swell and cause inflammation
> - Food bolus impaction in a child is uncommon and should raise the possibility of eosinophilic oesophagitis — take three lower oesophageal biopsies and request examination for eosinophils
> - When performing oesophagoscopy for button battery removal, always combine with MLTB to rule out tracheo-oesophageal fistula or prodromal granulation within the airway
