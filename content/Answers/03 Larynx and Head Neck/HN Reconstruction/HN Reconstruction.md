## Part A: Principles of Head & Neck Reconstruction

Defects following head and neck surgery may be closed by direct suture when small and local conditions allow adequate mobile tissue. For larger defects, options include free grafts, local skin flaps, pedicled flaps (axial cutaneous or musculocutaneous), or free tissue transfer. The choice depends on defect size, location, tissue requirements, and patient factors.

**Gillies' Principles of Reconstructive Surgery:**

Sir Harold Gillies formulated commandments that remain fundamental to head and neck reconstruction:

1. Losses must be replaced in kind
2. Treat the primary defect first
3. Thou shalt provide thyself with a lifeboat (always have a backup plan)
4. Thou shalt not throw away a living thing (preserve viable tissue)
5. Replace things into their normal position by recreation of the defect

**Reconstructive Ladder:**

The reconstructive ladder provides a hierarchy of options from simple to complex:

- Direct primary closure
- Skin grafts (split or full thickness)
- Local flaps (random or axial pattern)
- Regional pedicled flaps (distant axial or myocutaneous)
- Free tissue transfer (microvascular)

While free tissue transfer has become the gold standard for complex head and neck defects, pedicled flaps retain important roles in specific situations including salvage surgery, when microsurgical expertise is unavailable, and as "lifeboats" when [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]]s fail.

**Types of Flap in Head & Neck Reconstruction:**

| Category | Examples | Blood Supply |
|---|---|---|
| Local random | Cervical skin flaps | Subdermal plexus |
| Local axial | Forehead, nasolabial, submental, FAMM, TPF | Named vessel |
| Distant axial | Deltopectoral, supraclavicular | Perforating branches |
| Myocutaneous | Pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi | Musculocutaneous perforators |
| Free transfer | Forearm, fibula, scapular, ALT, DCIA | Microsurgical anastomosis |

## Part B: Pedicled Flaps in Head & Neck Surgery — Classification, Axial Flaps & PMMC Flap Technique

### Axial Flaps

An axial flap is based on a named arteriovenous pedicle that runs within the skin superficial to the underlying muscle layer, parallel to the overlying skin. Axial flaps have an extremely good blood supply determined not only by their length-to-breadth ratio but also by the vascular territory of the vessels that supply them. Because of this, they can be raised to a much greater length than random flaps and can move skin over greater distances.

**Key Local Axial Flaps:**

**Nasolabial Flap:** Based on distal branches of the facial artery. Usually designed with an inferior base. The medial limit of the flap is placed in the nasolabial fold with the superior limit approximating the medial canthus. Extremely reliable when based inferiorly with a relatively inconspicuous donor site. Greatest intraoral application is for the anterior floor of mouth and gingiva where its use is both simple and effective.

**Submental Island Flap:** Supplied by a branch of the facial artery that passes either over or through the submandibular gland, traversing medially on the mylohyoid muscle. Usually designed in the midline, just below the mandible margin. Unique advantage is its colour match with facial skin and relatively inconspicuous donor scar. Can be tunnelled under the mandible for oral reconstruction or rotated onto the face for soft tissue coverage. Problems with venous congestion may occur, particularly when tunnelled through the submandibular space.

**FAMM Flap (Facial Artery Myomucosal):** Composed of oral mucosa and buccinator muscle, based on branches of the facial artery. May be harvested as inferiorly based (antegrade flow) or superiorly based (retrograde flow). A flap of 7–8 cm can be harvested with a thickness of 8–10 mm. Ideally suited for reconstruction of small mucosal defects in the oral cavity, particularly lip mucosa, and can be rotated across the alveolus to close small defects of floor of mouth, tongue, or palate.

**Temporoparietal Fascial (TPF) Flap:** Arterial supply from the superficial temporal artery (terminal branch of external carotid). The fascia lies just under the subcutaneous tissue of the lateral scalp. Has a remarkably robust vascular supply with a very thin and pliable flap with minimal donor site morbidity. Applications include orbital reconstruction, auricular reconstruction (microtia), palate reconstruction, and buccal mucosal reconstruction. Care must be taken not to injure the frontal branch of the facial nerve during dissection.

### Distant Axial Flaps

**Deltopectoral Flap:** Described by Bakamjian and Littlewood in 1964. An axial pattern flap designed on the anterior chest wall between the clavicle and the anterior axillary fold. Vascular supply arises from the upper three or four perforating branches of the internal mammary artery. Boundaries are the clavicle superiorly, the acromion laterally, and a line through the anterior axillary fold to above the nipple inferiorly. The flap has an anomalous pivot point at the medial end of the upper limb, not the lower limb. The donor site is usually covered with a split skin graft. Uses include one-stage reconstruction of anterior neck skin, bridge flap reconstruction, and reconstruction of large lower face and upper neck defects.

**Supraclavicular Artery Island Flap (SAI):** A local fasciocutaneous flap harvested from the shoulder and supraclavicular area. First described by Lamberty in 1979. Blood supply from a branch of the transverse cervical artery, identified within a triangle bounded anteriorly by the posterior border of SCM, posteriorly by the external jugular vein, and inferiorly by the clavicle. Flaps up to 30 cm in length and 12 cm in width can be harvested. Dissection starts distally and is carried subfascially over the deltoid until the anterior border of trapezius is reached. Broad application for external skin defects from base of neck to parotid, and for oral and oropharyngeal defects.

### Myocutaneous Pedicled Flaps

The skin over most parts of the body receives its blood supply from small musculocutaneous arteries (perforating vessels) that enter it from the underlying muscle. This principle enables transposition of skin with its underlying muscle vascularized by its dominant blood supply.

**Patterns of Vascular Anatomy (Mathes & Nahai Classification):**

| Type | Vascular Pedicle | HN Example |
|---|---|---|
| I | One pedicle | — |
| II | Dominant + minor pedicles | SCM, Trapezius, Platysma |
| III | Two dominant pedicles | Temporalis |
| IV | Segmental pedicles | — |
| V | One dominant + segmental | Pectoralis major, Latissimus dorsi |

### Pectoralis Major Myocutaneous (PMMC) Flap — Detailed Technique

**Anatomy:** The muscle originates from the clavicle, sternum, and slips from the upper seven ribs, with a variable origin from the aponeurosis of the external oblique. It inserts into the bicipital groove of the humerus. The muscle has three major segmental subunits: clavicular, sternocostal, and external (most lateral).

**Blood Supply:** The main arterial supply comes from the pectoral branch of the acromiothoracic artery, which arises from the first part of the axillary artery. The pectoral branch emerges from the clavipectoral fascia along with the lateral pectoral nerve, medial to the insertion of pectoralis minor on the coracoid process. The surface marking of the vascular hilum is 2–3 cm medial to the coracoid process. The vessels run on the deep surface of the muscle in a downward and medial direction. The pectoralis major has a **Type V vascular pedicle** — one dominant pedicle plus secondary segmental pedicles. In approximately 50% of cases, the external segment may be supplied exclusively by the lateral thoracic vessels from the second part of the axillary artery.

**Advantages:**
- Large skin territory (the whole skin overlying the muscle may be raised)
- Rich vascular supply; highly reliable
- Can be transferred without prior delay
- Large arc of rotation — can reach up to the zygomatic arch
- Can be harvested in the supine position
- Can be transferred as muscle-only, skin/muscle paddle, or two epithelial surfaces
- Primary donor site closure is easily achieved
- Useful as a "lifeboat" for salvage surgery and in the presence of infection

**Disadvantages:**
- Large bulky muscle that is relatively immobile
- Suited best to providing well-vascularized tissue to fill large defects where mobility is not paramount
- Violates the breast in the female (latissimus dorsi is an appropriate alternative)

**Surface Markings:**
1. A dotted line from the acromion to the xiphoid process
2. A perpendicular line from the sternal notch to bisect the first line
3. The bisection point = location where the vascular pedicle meets the first line
4. The pedicle runs from acromion towards xiphisternum along the first line
5. The clavipectoral fascia is marked at a point two-thirds along the clavicle from sternum to coracoid process

**15-Step Elevation Technique:**

1. Start the dissection inferiorly
2. Incise the skin down to the underlying muscle
3. Define the inferior and lateral borders of pectoralis major
4. Incise and mobilize the inferior attachments to gain access to the subpectoral plane (relatively avascular)
5. Divide the inferior muscle attachments from the ribs or rectus sheath
6. Mobilize in an upward direction
7. At this point the pectoralis minor will be visualized
8. If the skin paddle is thick or excessively mobile, place sutures from the dermis of the skin island to the muscle surface to prevent shearing and flap loss
9. Combine mobilization in an upward direction, first laterally and then medially
10. Identify the lateral border of the external segment of the muscle
11. Identify and ligate the major branch of the lateral thoracic artery to increase arc of rotation
12. Continue the dissection upwards
13. Divide the sternal insertion of the muscle at the level of the anterior axillary line — continue in a vertical direction
14. **Do not curve the scissors medially at this point or you may cut the pedicle — the ultimate disaster**
15. The muscle will now be rotated and the vascular pedicle clearly identified

After dividing the sternal origin, the flap is elevated by blunt dissection in the subpectoral fascial space between pectoralis major and minor. The sternal edge of the muscle may be divided up to the level of the clavicle with the pedicle in clear view.

**Key Technical Points:**
- Minimum skin paddle size: 5 × 3 cm (palm of adult hand) to ensure adequate perforator capture
- Skin paddle borders should lie between the lateral edge of sternum medially and the nipple laterally
- Extend incision into the axilla for access
- Even when skin paddle fails, underlying muscle usually survives and can granulate, heal by secondary intention, or be covered with a skin graft
- Check for congenital absence of pectoralis major (1:11,000 incidence; sternocostal head absence is part of Poland syndrome)
- In females, the inframammary fold incision is the preferred approach

**Modifications:** Muscle-only flap for bulk; double skin paddles with de-epithelialized segments for inner and outer lining; myo-osseous flap incorporating fifth rib or sternum (precarious blood supply, newer techniques preferred).

### Latissimus Dorsi Flap

**Anatomy:** Large triangular muscle arising from sacrum, lumbar vertebrae, thoracolumbar fascia, posterior iliac crest, and lower six thoracic vertebrae, with slips from the lower three ribs. Inserts into the intertubercular groove of the humerus forming the posterior wall of the axilla. It is a **Type V muscle**.

**Blood Supply:** The thoracodorsal artery (continuation of the subscapular artery from the axillary artery). The circumflex scapular artery arises about 4 cm from the origin of the subscapular artery. Within the muscle, the thoracodorsal vessels divide into superior and lateral branches, allowing the muscle to be split into two for either two separate flaps or a single flap preserving some muscle.

**Advantages:**
- Large amounts of tissue can be transferred
- Can be used as pedicled or free tissue transfer
- Cosmetic advantage — does not violate the breast (advantage in females)
- Versatile: may be tubed, multiple paddles, osseous components
- When pedicled, can reach upper face, scalp, and top of the head
- Reliable with a long pedicle (>10 cm, lengthened by dividing circumflex scapular artery)
- Multiple flaps from the same pedicle (latissimus dorsi + serratus anterior + scapular flap)

**Disadvantages:**
- Very bulky
- Occasional donor site dehiscence and late wound seroma
- Reduction in upper limb power
- May require repositioning of the patient to harvest

**Applications:** Large defects of external neck skin, salivary fistula repair, total glossectomy defect (volume fills mucosal defect and dead space), scalp reconstruction.

### Other Myocutaneous Flaps

**Sternomastoid Flap:** Segmentally supplied by branches of the occipital artery (upper half) and superior thyroid artery (lower half). Not routinely recommended due to unreliable blood supply to skin paddle, oncological significance of the muscle ends, and availability of superior options. Limited role as muscle-only flap pedicled superiorly for small pharyngeal/oral defects, or split lengthwise and rotated anteriorly to cover vessels in the compromised neck.

**Trapezius Flaps:** Three basic types — upper (occipital artery), lateral (superficial cervical artery), and lower (dorsal scapular artery). The lower trapezius myocutaneous flap has the most utility for head and neck reconstruction, with an arc of rotation appropriate for neck and face resurfacing. Mainly used as a salvage option when free tissue transfer has failed or no recipient vessels are available.

**Platysma Flap:** Blood supply from submental branch of facial artery (upper) and transverse cervical artery (lower). Rarely used today due to unreliable blood supply, previous surgery that may preclude its use, and removal of platysma interfering with overlying skin blood supply.

## Part C: Free Tissue Transfer — Flap Options for Head & Neck Reconstruction

Free tissue transfer has dramatically affected outcomes for patients with head and neck deformity over the past 30 years. The introduction of microvascular techniques has largely superseded pedicled flaps for complex reconstructions. Success rates in experienced centres exceed 95%.

### Forearm (Radial) Free Flap

**Anatomy:** Based on the radial artery with venous drainage via the venae comitantes or branches to the cephalic vein. The artery lies between the tendons of brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis in the distal third. Perforating vessels extend vertically through the overlying fascia to supply the skin. Can incorporate a sensory nerve (lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve). The osseocutaneous version incorporates perforators to the distal radius (max bone length 10–12 cm).

**Advantages:**
- Remarkably consistent surgical anatomy — relatively easy to harvest
- Extremely thin skin on distal third — ideal for oral tongue and floor of mouth
- Best sensate flap available for oral reconstruction
- Can be harvested simultaneously with ablative procedure
- Long vascular pedicle

**Disadvantages:**
- Limited volume for defects with considerable dead space
- Poor skin colour match (except in South Asian or African American patients)
- Donor site requires skin graft (relatively unsightly scar on volar forearm)
- Possible wrist stiffness, cold sensitivity, tendon exposure
- Osseocutaneous version: risk of distal radius fracture; short bone height; limited length; not easily osteotomized for symphysis/angle defects

**Applications:** Oral tongue and floor of mouth (workhorse), buccal mucosa (best flap), palate (small central defects), oropharyngeal (tonsil–tongue base–soft palate), total lip reconstruction (with palmaris longus tendon sling), total nasal reconstruction (lining and infrastructure). An Allen's test to evaluate collateral flow through the ulnar artery is mandatory before harvest.

### Lateral Arm Flap

**Anatomy:** Based on the radial collateral artery (from deep brachial artery). The posterior branch enters the lateral intermuscular septum between brachialis and triceps. Skin island up to 14 × 20 cm available, but donor sites wider than 6 cm cannot be closed primarily.

**Advantages:** Donor site relatively inconspicuous; primary closure in most patients; excellent colour match to facial skin (even in Caucasians); can be harvested as a sensate flap.

**Disadvantages:** Small calibre pedicle (max 1.5 mm) — technically difficult microsurgical anastomosis; short vascular pedicle (4–8 cm); excessive fat volume in obese patients.

**Applications:** Floor of mouth and lateral tongue; recontouring radical [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]] defects; narrow lateral face/neck defects.

### Scapular System of Flaps

**Anatomy:** The axillary artery gives rise to the subscapular artery, which descends in the posterior axilla to give rise to the circumflex scapular artery and the thoracodorsal artery. The circumflex scapular artery provides nutrient and periosteal supply to the lateral scapula, then divides into two perforating branches (transverse and parascapular perforators). The thoracodorsal artery provides branches to serratus anterior, latissimus dorsi, and the angular artery (supplies distal one-third of scapula).

This system provides the greatest variety of reconstructive options from a single donor site: skin (scapular/parascapular), muscle (latissimus dorsi, serratus anterior, teres major), bone (lateral scapula from circumflex scapular or scapular tip from angular artery), or combinations thereof.

**Advantages:**
- Very large skin island available (up to 18 × 10–12 cm) with primary closure
- Good colour match to face and neck, even in Caucasians
- Well-tolerated in elderly; vessels spared in peripheral vascular disease
- Skin island can be rotated independently of bone flap
- Consistent vascular anatomy

**Disadvantages:**
- Two-team simultaneous harvest impossible (proximity to head/neck)
- Bone technically difficult to harvest; bone length limited (10–12 cm)
- Thick dermis and dense subcutaneous fat — inflexible for floor of mouth/tongue/palate
- Relatively short pedicle when bone nutrient artery is included
- Restriction of shoulder motion if teres major and subscapularis not properly repaired

### Rectus Abdominis / DIEP Flap

**Anatomy:** Based on the deep inferior epigastric artery (from external iliac artery). Pedicle length 8–14 cm; vessel diameter 2–3 mm. Can be harvested as myocutaneous (with rectus muscle) or perforator-based (DIEP — preserving muscle and sheath).

**Advantages:** Consistent vascular anatomy; large skin available with primary closure; two-team procedure possible; aesthetic benefit of abdominal lipectomy.

**Disadvantages:** Donor site hernia (especially below arcuate line); colour match poor in Caucasians; excessive subcutaneous fat volume in obese patients.

**Applications:** Large volume reconstructions of oral cavity, oropharynx, and lateral skull base; total glossectomy defect (fills dead space, protects carotid sheath).

### Anterolateral Thigh (ALT) Flap

**Anatomy:** Fasciocutaneous flap based on musculocutaneous and septocutaneous perforators of the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery. Pedicle length 8–16 cm. Large skin islands (15–20 cm × 10 cm) available with primary closure.

**Advantages:** Distant site allows two-team harvest; limited donor site morbidity; can be harvested as perforator flap with or without fascia lata or vastus lateralis; in thin patients, excellent for oral tongue and floor of mouth.

**Disadvantages:** Poor colour match in Caucasians; variable and unpredictable anatomy; single perforating vessel makes salvage difficult; poor choice for reconstructions requiring tunnelling of pedicle; excessive fat in some ethnic populations.

**Applications:** Widely used in Asian countries for oral cavity reconstruction due to superior donor site compared to forearm; can be tubed for total pharyngeal reconstruction; external head and neck defects.

## Part D: Mandibular Reconstruction — Options, Free Fibular Flap, and CAD

### Issues in Mandibular Reconstruction

The key issues are: length of bone required, whether transferred bone is amenable to secondary osseointegration, and donor site morbidity. Secondary issues include correction of disparities in bone height and condylar reconstruction. Currently, the flap of choice in most centres is the free fibular transfer.

### Osseocutaneous Flaps for Mandibular Reconstruction (Table 93.2)

| Flap | Bone Length | Pedicle Length | Donor Complications | Osseointegration | Skin Reliability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fibula | 16–20 cm | 6–8 cm | Low (+++) | ++ | ++ |
| Scapula | 8–12 cm | 4–8 cm | Low (++) | ++ | +++ |
| Iliac crest (DCIA) | 10–14 cm | 6–8 cm | High (+) | +++ (best) | + |
| Radius | 8–12 cm | 8–10 cm | High (+) | + | +++ |

(+++ = best; ++ = intermediate; + = poor)

### Free Fibular Flap — Flap of Choice

**Anatomy:** The osseocutaneous fibular flap is based on the **peroneal artery** (branch of the tibioperoneal trunk). The artery passes distally with paired venae comitantes lying on the medial aspect of the fibula between the tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus. The peroneal artery provides perforating branches to surrounding musculature and nutrient artery and periosteal supply to the fibula. In the distal half of the leg, perforating branches run laterally through the flexor hallucis longus, usually within or deep to the intermuscular septum separating the peroneus longus and soleus muscles.

- **Bone available:** 18–24 cm (depending on leg length)
- **Skin island:** 12–16 cm × 8–10 cm
- **Pedicle length:** depends on bone length required (longer bone = shorter pedicle)
- **Vessel diameter:** 3–4 mm (artery); 4–5 mm (vein)

**Advantages:**
- Bone length available will almost reconstruct the entire mandible
- Skin island of appropriate thickness for most oral cavity and oropharyngeal reconstructions
- Can be osteotomized into multiple segments to recreate mandibular contour
- Low donor site morbidity compared to iliac crest
- Long vascular pedicle available (advantage for midface reconstruction)
- Suitable for secondary osseointegration with dental implants

**Disadvantages:**
- Not a good option in patients with established peripheral vascular disease (harvesting a single vessel in an arterially compromised leg could be catastrophic)
- Some reports of relatively high failure rates with the skin island (reducible with attention to harvest technique)
- Donor site problems in patients with venous insufficiency
- Some patients experience prolonged ankle/leg stiffness and occasional ankle instability
- Short vertical bone height — disparity between native mandible and reconstructed segments
- If large cuff of flexor hallucis harvested — flexion deformity of great toe

**Technique — Key Steps:**
1. Pre-operative non-invasive vascular imaging of the leg (colour Doppler, MRA, or CTA)
2. Doppler the perforating branches to skin to ensure appropriate skin island placement
3. Harvest usually under tourniquet control via the lateral approach
4. Incise the skin island; reflect peroneus longus and brevis anteriorly off the fibula
5. Enter and open the anterior compartment over the length of the fibula, avoiding the anterior tibial artery
6. Divide the posterior interosseous membrane, visualizing the tibialis posterior
7. Divide the fibula distally and proximally — leave at least **6 cm of fibula at the ankle and knee** to avoid ankle instability and prevent injury to the common peroneal nerve
8. Divide the distal peroneal artery; divide the tibialis posterior; visualize the full course of the peroneal artery
9. Harvest a cuff of flexor hallucis longus and soleus to protect perforators to the skin and ensure venous drainage
10. Osteotomize the fibula into multiple segments to recreate the shape of the mandible
11. Use a reconstruction plate to lag bone segments (single plate or multiple small plates)
12. Harvest from the leg contralateral to the mandibular defect — this positions the pedicle appropriately for recipient vessel access and keeps the skin island in the correct orientation for oral defect insetting

**"Double-barreling"** — placing one bone segment on top of another — has been advocated to maximize vertical height for secondary osseointegration.

### DCIA (Iliac Crest) Flap

**Anatomy:** Based on the deep circumflex iliac artery and venae comitantes. The DCIA arises from the lateral aspect of the external iliac artery, just above the inguinal ligament. Runs parallel to the inguinal ligament along the inner aspect of the crest. Gives off a large ascending branch that pierces the transversus abdominis to lie on the deep surface of internal oblique. Pedicle 6–8 cm; artery and vein 2–3 mm calibre.

**Advantages:** Ideal bone stock for mandibular reconstruction — can be shaped to recreate both mandibular body and vertical ramus. Excellent corticocancellous proportions and vertical height/breadth. Ideally suited for **primary or secondary osseointegration**. When transferred with internal oblique, provides excellent vascularized reconstruction for osteoradionecrosis.

**Disadvantages:**
- Skin paddle unreliable (fragile, unpredictable perforator supply requiring thick muscle cuff, producing inflexible reconstruction)
- Donor site morbidity: frank hernia formation or chronic abdominal wall laxity
- Painful donor site — perioperative mobilization issues, especially in elderly
- Pedicle length can be problematic for maxillary reconstruction (may need vein grafting)
- Lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh may be sacrificed (numbness)

**Applications:** Hemimandibular reconstruction (Taylor's original description); bone length 8–14 cm adequate for most defects. Most commonly harvested as myo-osseous flap with internal oblique for oral closure. Also used for maxillary defects — flap turned horizontally to replace hard palate, with bone allowed to mucosalize secondarily.

### Computerized Assisted Design (CAD) in Mandibular Reconstruction

The development of computerized assisted design with three-dimensional planning of defect creation and reconstruction represents a major advance in mandibular reconstruction. Data from medical scanners (CT, MRI) can be used to reproduce physical models of human anatomy via additive layer manufacture (ALM) or rapid prototyping (RP).

**Stereolithography:** The basic principle is to build an object in layers using CT data and an optical scanning system that deposits a polymeric resin material one layer at a time onto the "build bed" until the desired object is completed. Each layer of resin is cured using a high-intensity UV light, ensuring the strength of the model.

**Applications of CAD/3D Planning:**

1. **Pre-bent reconstruction plates:** Surgeons are supplied with a reconstruction plate pre-bent to the patient's mandibular contour, eliminating intraoperative bending errors
2. **Cutting guides:** Custom guides for both the fibula/scapula osteotomies and the ablative mandibular resection, ensuring precise fit
3. **Individual implant templates:** For either soft tissue or direct bone contact to allow exact implant placement
4. **3D anatomical models:** Act as visual aids for the surgical team, indicating resection lines, allowing trial surgery, and enabling pre-surgical planning
5. **Obturator prostheses:** For maxillary defects
6. **Cranioplasties:** For skull defect planning and reconstruction

**Advantages of CAD Technology:**
- Improved aesthetic and functional outcome
- Particularly beneficial when comprehensive dental reconstruction with osseointegrated implants is planned
- Reduces operative time and improves accuracy of bone segment placement
- Enables virtual surgical planning and trial surgery preoperatively
- Allows precise angular positioning of osteotomized bone segments

While currently expensive, this technology offers the opportunity for improved accuracy in mandibular reconstruction, especially for defects requiring multiple osteotomies and precise three-dimensional contouring.

## Part E: Surgical vs Prosthetic Rehabilitation in Head & Neck Defects

### Comparison of Surgical and Prosthetic Rehabilitation (Table 97.1)

| Parameter | Surgical | Prosthetic |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue used | Patient's own (autogenous) | Silicone, acrylic materials |
| Timing | Same time as resection possible | Immediate temporary reconstruction |
| Defect monitoring | No visual access for inspection | Allows inspection of defect site (tumour patients) |
| Number of procedures | May require multiple procedures | Single procedure |
| Aesthetic result | Variable; depends on host site quality | Predictable results |
| Donor site | Separate donor site required | No donor site |
| Maintenance | No prosthetic maintenance needed | Long-term follow-up; replacement prosthesis required |
| Acceptability | More acceptable to patients | Requires skilled prosthetist |
| Modifiability | Difficult to modify | Easily modifiable |
| Future options | — | Does not preclude future surgical reconstruction |
| Tissue character | Mobile, living tissue | Non-mobile tissue |

### Patient Selection for Prosthetic Rehabilitation

The decision should be made only after considering all available options. Prosthetics should complement, not replace, reconstructive surgery. Factors to consider:

- **Medical factors:** Patient's physiological status and general health
- **Patient factors:** Age, dexterity, hygiene, motivation, compliance
- **Defect considerations:** Availability of autogenous tissue, soft-tissue cover, skin condition, defect shape, availability of bone for implant placement, facial movement during function, previous [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]]

### Osseointegrated Implants in Prosthetics

Brånemark first placed a modified extraoral implant for a bone-anchored hearing aid ([[Bone Conduction MEI Devices|BAHA]]) in 1977 and for a bone-anchored auricular prosthesis in 1979. Osseointegrated implants provide superior retention compared to adhesive or spectacle-retained prostheses.

**Advantages of Implant-Retained Prostheses:**
- Better retention with confident prosthesis positioning
- No skin irritation from adhesive
- Thinner, feathered edges blending better into skin (enhanced aesthetics)
- Reduced deterioration of prosthetic material (no adhesive cleaning)
- Greater patient acceptance due to security, comfort, and convenience
- Reduced need for numerous complex surgical reconstructive procedures

**Primary Implant Placement:** Placing fixtures at the time of ablative surgery reduces time before prosthetic rehabilitation can commence. Fixtures placed before radiotherapy have increased osseointegration and survival rates. Implants are best placed in areas of uncompromised, thick bone.

For large defects, a multidisciplinary approach combining flap reconstruction with implant-retained prosthetic rehabilitation achieves optimal results.

### Defect-Specific Prosthetic Options

**Nasal Defects:** The goal is an aesthetically pleasing and functional nose. Smaller defects are reconstructed autogenously. Larger defects or those with compromised tissue may benefit from implant-retained nasal prostheses. Five aesthetic units of the nose: dorsum, lateral tip, tip, alar lobule, and soft-tissue triangle.

**Orbital Defects:** Three types of orbital surgery require prostheses:
- Evisceration (intraocular contents removed; sclera and muscles preserved) → cosmetic shell
- Enucleation (globe and portion of optic nerve removed) → artificial eye
- Exenteration (entire orbital contents removed) → orbital prosthesis with implants

Facial prosthesis with three dental implants is the method of choice. Osseointegrated implants reduce limitations associated with adhesives and provide high long-term success rates.

**Auricular Defects:** Choice between autogenous (cartilage framework) vs prosthetic reconstruction depends on the clinical situation, surgeon training, patient preference, and availability of tissue. Implants are most reliable in the auricular region. Two implants reduce cantilevering and prevent rotation. The auricular region is the most dependable implant site.

**Oral Cavity:** While surgical reconstruction is treatment of choice, prosthetics is suitable when surgical reconstruction has failed or is impractical. For maxillectomy defects, an obturator prosthesis immediately restores speech and mastication and provides support for soft tissue.

### Digital Applications in Prosthetics

- CT data used for 3D simulation of reconstructive methods including osteotomy and distraction
- Stereolithography produces 3D anatomical models for surgical planning
- Individual implant templates for exact placement
- 3D models enable trial surgery, indicate resection lines, and allow pre-bending of reconstruction plates
- Applications in facial reconstruction, obturator prostheses, and cranioplasty planning

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Gillies' commandments remain fundamental: replace losses in kind, treat primary defect first, provide a lifeboat, do not discard living tissue
> 2. Pectoralis major is the workhorse pedicled myocutaneous flap — Type V vascular pedicle (pectoral branch of acromiothoracic artery), can reach the zygomatic arch, 15-step elevation technique; the critical error to avoid is curving scissors medially when dividing sternal insertion (may cut the pedicle)
> 3. The free fibular flap is the flap of choice for mandibular reconstruction in most centres — based on the peroneal artery, 18–24 cm bone available, can be osteotomized into multiple segments; leave at least 6 cm at ankle and knee
> 4. The DCIA (iliac crest) flap provides the best bone stock for osseointegration, can recreate body and vertical ramus, but has an unreliable skin paddle and significant donor site morbidity
> 5. The free forearm (radial) flap is the best flap for oral tongue and floor of mouth reconstruction — thin, pliable, best sensation; Allen's test mandatory
> 6. CAD with 3D planning provides pre-bent reconstruction plates and cutting guides for precise mandibular reconstruction; stereolithography creates anatomical models for trial surgery
> 7. Prosthetic rehabilitation allows immediate reconstruction, predictable results, easy modification, and defect inspection for tumour surveillance; osseointegrated implants (Brånemark) provide superior retention
> 8. Table 97.1 comparison: surgical uses autogenous tissue and is more acceptable to patients; prosthetic allows defect inspection, is easily modifiable, and does not preclude future surgery
> 9. The deltopectoral flap (Bakamjian, 1964) is based on perforating branches of the internal mammary artery with an anomalous pivot point
> 10. The latissimus dorsi (thoracodorsal artery from subscapular) can reach the top of the head, does not violate the breast, and can provide combined flaps from the subscapular system
> 11. The scapular system provides the greatest variety of flap options from a single vascular pedicle (subscapular artery), but is limited by short bone pedicle and inability to allow two-team harvest
> 12. Minimum skin paddle size for myocutaneous flaps (PM and LD) is 5 × 3 cm to capture adequate perforators

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always check for congenital absence of pectoralis major before surgery (1:11,000; Poland syndrome)
> - For the PMMC flap: "Do not curve the scissors medially at step 14 or you may cut the pedicle — the ultimate disaster"
> - Harvest the fibula from the leg contralateral to the mandibular defect to position the pedicle correctly and orient the skin island for oral insetting
> - Pre-operative vascular imaging (CTA/MRA/Doppler) of the leg is essential before fibular flap harvest; avoid in patients with peripheral vascular disease
> - The scapular system of flaps is well tolerated in the elderly and relatively spared in patients with peripheral vascular disease, making it an excellent alternative to the fibula
> - For maxillectomy defects, placing osseointegrated implants at the time of ablative surgery (before radiotherapy) increases osseointegration and survival rates
> - When the free forearm flap fails, the forehead flap (based on anterior branch of temporal artery) serves as a "lifeboat" for oral/oropharyngeal reconstruction
> - The lower trapezius flap is the principal salvage option when free tissue transfer has failed and no recipient vessels are available for anastomosis
