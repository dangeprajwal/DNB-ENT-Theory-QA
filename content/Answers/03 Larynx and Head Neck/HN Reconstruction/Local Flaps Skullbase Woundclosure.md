## Introduction

Wound closure and flap reconstruction are fundamental skills in head and neck surgery. The reconstructive ladder provides a logical framework for selecting the simplest effective method for each defect. Local flaps — which bring adjacent tissue with its own blood supply into the defect — are the mainstay of facial plastics, offering superior colour and texture match compared to distant options. In endoscopic skull base surgery, the Hadad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap has revolutionized closure of large anterior skull base defects. Knowledge of flap principles, local flap design and the complications of major neck surgery is essential for every ENT surgeon.

## Part A — Methods of Wound Closure and the Reconstructive Ladder

### The Reconstructive Ladder

The reconstructive ladder is a hierarchical framework guiding the surgeon from the simplest to the most complex reconstructive option. The surgeon starts at the bottom rung and ascends only as far as necessary.

| Rung (Bottom → Top) | Method |
|----------------------|--------|
| 1 | Secondary intention healing |
| 2 | Primary intention (direct closure) |
| 3 | Delayed primary closure |
| 4 | Skin grafts (STSG / FTSG) |
| 5 | Tissue expansion |
| 6 | Local tissue transfer (local flaps) |
| 7 | Free tissue transfer |

The **reconstructive elevator** concept recognizes that sometimes rungs should be skipped — for example, exposed great vessels after neck dissection warrant a vascularized flap rather than a skin graft, even though a graft might technically "take."

### Methods of Wound Closure

**Direct (Primary) Closure** is used when the defect is small and sufficient local tissue exists. In the head and neck, incisions should be placed parallel to **relaxed skin tension lines (RSTLs)**, which run perpendicular to the underlying muscle fibres. Scars placed along these lines are under the least tension and produce the best cosmetic result.

**Wedge Excision** is used for lesions at the free edge of composite tissues — the eyelid (conjunctiva, tarsal plate, orbicularis, skin), the lip (mucosa, orbicularis oris, skin) and the helical rim. Each composite layer must be repaired individually to optimize both function and appearance.

**Skin Grafts** replace tissue without importing a blood supply (see [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|Pedicled Flap]]s & Skin Grafts file for detailed coverage of graft types and healing).

**Local Flaps** carry their own blood supply and are used when vascularity is poor, vital structures are exposed, skin grafts would give poor cosmesis, or post-operative [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] is planned (flaps are less susceptible than grafts to radiation effects).

### Cosmetic Subunits of the Head and Neck

The face is divided into named cosmetic subunits (forehead, temple, brow, periorbital, nasal, cheek, lip, chin, ear). Reconstructive principles dictate that individual subunits should be reconstructed separately; scars should not cross subunit boundaries if possible. If more than 50% of a cosmetic subunit is involved, it may be preferable to excise the remaining tissue and reconstruct the entire subunit.

## Part B — Basic Principles of Flap Surgery and the Rintala Flap

### Flap Classification

Flaps are classified by four parameters:

| Parameter | Options |
|-----------|---------|
| Circulation | Random (unnamed vessel in base) or Axial (named artery) |
| Composition | Cutaneous, fasciocutaneous, myocutaneous, muscle-only, osseous |
| Contiguity | Local, regional, distant, or free |
| Contour (movement) | Advancement or pivot (transposition / rotation) |

A **local flap** is composed of tissue adjacent to the defect. A **regional flap** is from the same anatomical region (e.g., forehead flap for nasal reconstruction). A **distant flap** is from a different body region (e.g., PMMC). A **[[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]]** is completely detached and anastomosed to recipient vessels.

### Key Flap Design Principles

- **Random flap ratio**: Base breadth to flap length should not exceed 1:1; in the well-vascularized head and neck this may be extended to 1:1.5. Axial flaps can be much longer.
- **Primary defect** — the site into which the flap is inset. **Secondary defect** — the donor site from which the flap is raised; this is closed directly, skin-grafted, or reconstructed with another flap.
- **Reverse planning** — a template of the primary defect is reflected from a fixed pivot point onto the donor area to assess required flap dimensions and minimize donor morbidity.
- Donor sites should be from the same cosmetic unit or an adjacent one; scars from secondary defect closure should lie along RSTLs.

### The Rintala Flap

The Rintala flap is a **modified advancement flap** used to reconstruct defects of the nasal dorsum and nasal tip.

**Principle:** A rectangular flap of glabellar and forehead skin is advanced inferiorly toward the nasal tip. Because simple advancement would leave standing cutaneous cones ("dog ears") at the base, **Burow's triangles** are excised at either side of the flap base to equalize the length between the flap edges and the wound margins, thereby facilitating smooth advancement.

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Type | Modified advancement flap |
| Blood supply | Random (rich glabellar vasculature) |
| Indication | Dorsum of nose defects, nasal tip |
| Key modification | Burow's triangles excised at base |
| Donor site | Glabella/lower forehead |
| Closure | Direct closure of secondary defect |
| Advantage | Excellent skin match; scar in glabellar crease |

**Other Modified Advancement Flaps:**
- **V–Y advancement flap** — a triangular ("V"-shaped) flap is advanced and the secondary defect closed directly, leaving a "Y"-shaped scar. The nasolabial V–Y advancement flap is an axial flap based on the superior labial or angular artery, useful for alar and nasolabial defects.

## Part C — Local Flaps in Facial Plastics

### Enumeration of Local Flaps

**Advancement Flaps:**
- Simple advancement flap
- Rintala flap (with Burow's triangles)
- V–Y advancement flap (nasolabial V–Y)
- Hatchet flap (random rotation flap with back cut — for forehead)

**Pivot Flaps — Transposition:**
- Rhomboid (Limberg) flap
- Dufourmentel flap (modified rhomboid)
- Banner / flag flap (small transposition, e.g., glabellar flap)
- Bilobed flap

**Pivot Flaps — Rotation:**
- Rotation flap (large semicircular flap)
- Cervicofacial rotation flap
- Cheek rotation flap

**Bipedicled Flaps:**
- Tripier flap (upper eyelid to lower eyelid)

**Axial Local Flaps:**
- Median forehead flap (supratrochlear artery)
- Nasolabial flap (facial artery branches)
- Glabellar flap (supratrochlear artery)
- FAMM flap (facial artery — for oral cavity)
- Submental island flap (submental artery)
- Temporoparietal fascial flap (superficial temporal artery)

### Rhomboid (Limberg) Flap

The rhomboid flap is a **transposition flap** in which the donor defect is closed directly. It was originally described by Limberg and remains one of the most versatile local flaps in facial reconstruction.

**Design:**
1. The defect is designed as (or converted to) a rhomboid shape.
2. All four limbs of the rhomboid must be of **equal length**.
3. The angles of the rhomboid are **120° and 60°** (opposing pairs).
4. A flap of identical dimensions is designed adjacent to the defect, so that when transposed into the defect, the donor site closes directly.
5. The donor-site closure scar must be oriented parallel to the RSTLs — this is the key to selecting which of the four possible rhomboid flap orientations to use.

**Theoretically, four different rhomboid flaps can be designed for any single defect** — one along each of the four short diagonals. The surgeon selects the orientation whose secondary closure line best aligns with the RSTLs.

**Variations:**
- **Dufourmentel flap** — a modified rhomboid with equal limb lengths but angles of **150° and 30°** instead of 120° and 60°. This variation allows closure of wider or narrower defects.
- **"Square peg in the round hole"** — for circular defects (the clinical norm), the rhomboid flap is stretched to fill the round defect without requiring excision of extra skin, which is especially useful in cosmetically sensitive areas.

| Feature | Rhomboid | Dufourmentel |
|---------|----------|--------------|
| Angles | 120° and 60° | 150° and 30° |
| Limb lengths | All equal | All equal |
| Donor closure | Direct | Direct |
| Orientations | 4 possible per defect | 4 possible per defect |

**The Bilobed Flap:**
The bilobed flap consists of two transposition flaps. The first flap transposes into the primary defect; the second flap transposes into the secondary defect (the first flap's donor site); the tertiary defect (the second flap's donor site) is closed directly. The tertiary closure line should be parallel to the RSTLs. This design uses less tissue than any other wound closure method and minimizes tension at the primary defect. It is most commonly used for **nasal tip defects**, where tissue is tight and skin must not be distorted.

## Part D — Local Flaps for Skull Base Reconstruction and the Hadad-Bassagasteguy Flap

### Skull Base Defects — Context

Endoscopic approaches to the anterior skull base and sellar/parasellar regions are increasingly employed for a variety of pathological processes. These create defects through which CSF may leak. Small defects can be repaired with free mucosal, fascial or fat grafts; larger defects require vascularized tissue.

### Enumeration of Local Flaps for Skull Base Reconstruction

| Flap | Type | Blood Supply | Application |
|------|------|-------------|-------------|
| Nasoseptal (Hadad) flap | Vascularized pedicled | Nasoseptal artery (branch of SPA) | Large anterior skull base, sella, clivus |
| Middle turbinate flap | Vascularized pedicled | Branches of SPA | Small-medium anterior skull base |
| Inferior turbinate flap | Vascularized pedicled | Inferior turbinate artery | Small defects, limited reach |
| Pericranial flap | Vascularized pedicled | Supraorbital/supratrochlear arteries | Anterior skull base (transcranial/combined) |
| Temporoparietal fascia flap | Vascularized pedicled | Superficial temporal artery | Lateral skull base, temporal bone |
| Palatal flap | Vascularized pedicled | Greater palatine artery | Clival, sellar defects |

**Free Grafts (Non-vascularized):**
- Free fat graft, fascia lata, acellular dermis, collagen matrix (Duragen), septal mucosal/bone/cartilage grafts — used for smaller defects or as part of multilayer closure.

### The Hadad-Bassagasteguy Nasoseptal Flap (NSF)

The Hadad-Bassagasteguy flap, described by **Hadad and Bassagasteguy**, is a **vascularized posterior pedicled nasoseptal flap** that has revolutionized endoscopic skull base reconstruction. Kassam et al. reported its general utility in 75 patients undergoing endonasal endoscopic cranial base surgery.

**Blood Supply:**
The flap is based on the **nasoseptal artery**, which is a branch of the **posterior septal artery**, itself arising from the **sphenopalatine artery (SPA)**. The SPA arises from the terminal branch of the internal maxillary artery and exits through the sphenopalatine foramen. The nasoseptal artery courses along the posterior septum, giving branches that supply the mucoperiosteum and mucoperichondrium of the nasal septum. This arterial pedicle provides robust vascularity allowing a very large flap to be raised.

**Composition:**
The flap consists of septal **mucoperiosteum** (over bone) and **mucoperichondrium** (over cartilage), raised from one side of the nasal septum. It is a mucosal flap — thin, pliable and large enough to cover defects from the [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]] to the clivus.

**Indications:**
- Large anterior cranial base defects following endoscopic resection of skull base tumours (meningiomas, esthesioneuroblastomas, chordomas)
- Sellar and parasellar defects (pituitary surgery with large CSF leaks)
- Clival defects
- Planum sphenoidale defects
- Any endoscopic skull base defect where free grafts alone are insufficient

**Surgical Technique:**
1. The flap is ideally raised at the beginning of the procedure before posterior septectomy, to preserve the vascular pedicle.
2. An inferior horizontal incision is made along the junction of the nasal floor and septum, extending from the anterior septum posteriorly to the choana.
3. A superior horizontal incision is made just below the olfactory epithelium (inferior to the cribriform plate), extending posteriorly.
4. An anterior vertical incision connects the two horizontal incisions.
5. The mucoperiosteum/mucoperichondrium is elevated from anterior to posterior in a subperichondrial/subperiosteal plane.
6. The pedicle (nasoseptal artery) is preserved at the posterior end near the sphenopalatine foramen.
7. The flap is tucked into the nasopharynx during the main surgical procedure.
8. After tumour removal and CSF leak identification, the flap is rotated superiorly and laid over the skull base defect.
9. A multilayer closure is often employed — an inlay graft (fascia lata or collagen matrix) is placed intradurally/epidurally, followed by the vascularized NSF as an overlay.
10. Fibrin glue may reinforce adherence. Nasal packing (Merocel/Gelfoam) provides temporary support.

**Important Considerations:**
- In **revision pituitary surgery**, the previous transseptal approach may have damaged the SPA or posterior septal artery, compromising the NSF. Alternative flaps (middle turbinate, inferior turbinate, pericranial) must be considered.
- The conventional transseptal microscopic approach risks [[Septal Perforation Repair|septal perforation]] or compromise of the SPA/posterior septal artery — therefore, in patients previously operated via this approach, NSF viability may be unreliable.

## Part E — Complications of Radical Neck Dissection and Their Prevention

### Classification of Complications

| Timing | Complications |
|--------|--------------|
| Intra-operative | Haemorrhage, air embolism, nerve injury (accessory, vagus, hypoglossal, phrenic, marginal mandibular, sympathetic chain, lingual), thoracic duct injury, pneumothorax |
| Early post-operative | Haematoma, wound infection, wound breakdown, flap necrosis, chyle leak, seroma |
| Late | Shoulder dysfunction, lymphoedema, cosmetic deformity, hypothyroidism, dysphagia |

### Specific Complications and Their Prevention

**1. Haemorrhage and Vascular Injury**
- Internal jugular vein (IJV) tear — most common major vascular injury. Prevention: careful dissection, bipolar diathermy, proximal and distal ligation before division.
- Carotid artery injury — rare but catastrophic. Prevention: meticulous dissection along the adventitial plane; avoid carotid resection unless genuine invasion. Studies show that even with radiological evidence of invasion, dissection off the carotid is feasible in >70% of cases.
- Post-operative haematoma — prevention: meticulous haemostasis, suction drain placement, avoidance of Valsalva manoeuvres in the immediate post-operative period.

**2. Nerve Injury**
- **Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI)**: sacrificed in RND; shoulder dysfunction (inability to abduct arm >90°, "drooping shoulder," trapezius wasting) is inevitable. In MRND, the nerve is preserved but may suffer neuropraxia from devascularization and stretching. Even in SND, dissection of the submuscular recess (level IIb) can stretch the nerve.
- **Marginal mandibular nerve (CN VII)**: at risk during level I dissection. Prevention: identify and retract using Hayes-Martin manoeuvre (ligate facial vein low, reflect superiorly with overlying nerve).
- **Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII)**: identified deep to digastric tendon; carefully preserved by dissection along its course.
- **Vagus nerve (CN X)**: lies within carotid sheath; at risk during IJV dissection.
- **Phrenic nerve**: at risk in level V dissection; injury causes ipsilateral diaphragmatic paralysis.
- **Sympathetic chain**: lies deep to carotid sheath; injury causes Horner's syndrome (miosis, ptosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos).
- **Lingual nerve**: at risk during level I/submandibular dissection.

**3. Chyle Leak (Chylous Fistula)**
An uncommon but major complication, particularly with left-sided surgery involving the supraclavicular fossa. The incidence is 0.5–1.4% for thyroidectomy and 2–8% for neck dissection. The thoracic duct crosses between the IJV and the anterior scalene muscle and terminates at the junction of the left IJV and left subclavian vein. Approximately 5% of chyle leaks occur on the right from the right lymphatic duct.

- A triglyceride level >110 mg/dl in drain fluid is diagnostic.
- **Low-output leaks** (<500 ml/24 hours): managed conservatively with dietary modification — fat-free diet or medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) products. MCTs are absorbed directly into the portal system, bypassing chyle formation.
- **High-output leaks** (>500 ml/24 hours): more likely to require surgical re-exploration and ligation. Parenteral nutrition may be needed for very high volume leaks (>1000 ml). Alternatives include octreotide and orlistat.
- **Prevention**: careful identification and ligation of the thoracic duct intra-operatively; ask the anaesthetist to perform a Valsalva manoeuvre before wound closure to identify leaks; use clips or ligatures rather than diathermy alone.

**4. Wound Complications**
- Wound infection: prevention with prophylactic antibiotics, careful tissue handling, avoiding dead space, suction drainage.
- Wound breakdown and flap necrosis: risk increased in previously irradiated necks, diabetics, smokers, and malnourished patients. Prevention: careful flap design with adequate vascularity; avoid trifurcation point; use PMMC or free flap cover for high-risk wounds.
- When performing salvage neck dissection in a previously irradiated neck, the use of a pedicled PMMC flap or free flap should be seriously considered to reduce [[Wound Healing Phases Types Factors|wound healing]] complications.

**5. Shoulder Dysfunction**
The most significant long-term morbidity following neck dissection. Problems are greatest with RND (accessory nerve sacrificed) and decrease progressively with MRND (nerve preserved but devascularized) and SND (least morbidity). Even MRND causes dysfunction due to nerve devascularization and stretching. SNDs clearing levels II–IV can also affect the shoulder by stretching the accessory nerve during dissection of the submuscular recess (level IIb). If level V is not dissected, shoulder morbidity is significantly reduced; however, the trade-off may be post-operative radiotherapy which also contributes to shoulder dysfunction.

**6. Other Complications**
- Pneumothorax — risk with level IV/V dissection near the lung apex.
- Air embolism — rare but life-threatening; occurs when large neck veins are opened.
- Lymphoedema — particularly facial/submental; more common after bilateral procedures.
- Hypothyroidism — if thyroidectomy performed concurrently or post-operative radiotherapy given.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The reconstructive ladder ascends from secondary intention through primary closure, skin grafts, tissue expansion, local flaps and free tissue transfer; the "reconstructive elevator" concept permits skipping rungs when clinical circumstances demand it (e.g., vascularized flap for exposed great vessels)
> 2. Relaxed skin tension lines run perpendicular to underlying muscle fibres; scars placed parallel to these lines are under the least tension and produce the best cosmetic outcome
> 3. If more than 50% of a facial cosmetic subunit is involved, excision and reconstruction of the entire subunit may produce a superior aesthetic result
> 4. Random flaps in the head and neck may be extended to a 1:1.5 base-to-length ratio (vs 1:1 elsewhere) due to the rich vascularity; axial flaps can be designed much longer
> 5. The Rintala flap is a modified advancement flap for nasal dorsum/tip defects, using Burow's triangles at the base to facilitate advancement without dog-ear formation
> 6. The rhomboid (Limberg) flap requires a defect with 120° and 60° angles and equal limb lengths; four possible orientations exist per defect — the surgeon selects the one whose secondary closure aligns with RSTLs; the Dufourmentel modification uses 150° and 30° angles
> 7. The bilobed flap uses two transposition flaps in sequence; it is the flap of choice for nasal tip defects as it uses the least tissue and minimizes distortion
> 8. The Hadad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap is based on the nasoseptal artery (branch of SPA) and provides vascularized mucoperiosteal/mucoperichondrial coverage for large anterior skull base defects
> 9. Chyle leak occurs in 2–8% of neck dissections; triglyceride level >110 mg/dl is diagnostic; low-output leaks (<500 ml/day) are managed with MCT-based diet; high-output leaks require surgical re-exploration
> 10. Shoulder dysfunction is the most significant long-term morbidity of neck dissection; severity correlates with extent of accessory nerve sacrifice/manipulation — worst in RND, intermediate in MRND, least in SND


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Reverse planning is essential for local flap design** — reflect a template of the primary defect from the pivot point onto the donor area to confirm that flap dimensions provide adequate coverage without excessive donor morbidity
> - **For rhomboid flaps on the face, always choose the orientation whose secondary closure scar aligns with RSTLs** — this is the single most important factor in achieving an aesthetic result; on the cheek, the bias is usually toward the nasolabial fold
> - **The Hadad flap must be raised BEFORE posterior septectomy** — if the septum is removed first, the vascular pedicle (nasoseptal artery from SPA) will be divided and the flap rendered non-viable; always plan skull base flap harvest at the start of the procedure
> - **In previously operated transseptal pituitary patients, the NSF may be unavailable** — prior microscopic approaches can damage the SPA/posterior septal artery; alternative flaps (middle turbinate, pericranial) must be considered during pre-operative planning
> - **Valsalva manoeuvre before closing a neck dissection wound identifies chyle leaks** — ask the anaesthetist to perform positive-pressure ventilation; milky fluid indicates thoracic duct injury requiring immediate clip ligation
> - **Salvage neck dissection in previously irradiated necks warrants vascularized tissue cover** — wound complication rates are significantly higher in irradiated tissue; a PMMC or free flap should be planned proactively to cover exposed vessels and reduce breakdown risk
