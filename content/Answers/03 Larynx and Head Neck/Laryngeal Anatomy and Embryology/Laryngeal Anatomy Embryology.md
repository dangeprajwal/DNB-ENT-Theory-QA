# Laryngeal Anatomy, Embryology, Nerve Supply and Barriers to Cancer Spread

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Larynx embryology. Intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the larynx — nerve supply, actions. Barriers to cancer spread within the larynx. Laryngocoele. Developmental anatomy. Left RLN anatomy and steps to prevent damage during surgery.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 58 (Anatomy of the Larynx and Tracheobronchial Tree) and Chapter 69 (Acute Infections of the Larynx — laryngocoele section)

---

## Introduction

The human larynx protects the lower respiratory tract, provides a controlled airway, allows phonation and generates high intrathoracic pressure for coughing and lifting. To fulfil these complex functions the larynx must be mobile, coordinated and sensate. The larynx is divided anatomically into the supraglottis, glottis and subglottis by the false and true vocal folds. In rehabilitating the damaged larynx there is often a need to make a trade-off between optimum airway, voice and swallowing. The infantile larynx is proportionally smaller, funnel-shaped, and its narrowest part is at the subglottis (contrast: the narrowest part of the adult larynx is the glottis). The laryngeal cartilages are softer in infants and collapse more easily on forced inspiration due to the Bernoulli effect. Knowledge of laryngeal embryology, anatomy, spaces and nerve supply is essential for safe laryngeal surgery and understanding patterns of tumour spread.

## PART A: Embryology of the Larynx

At four weeks of embryonic development, an outgrowth of the primitive foregut forms the primordial respiratory system. The epithelium of the larynx, trachea and bronchi is of **endodermal** origin. The cartilage, muscles, connective tissue and vasculature arise from the surrounding **splanchnic mesoderm** (fourth and sixth branchial arches).

### Key Embryological Events

- Arytenoid swellings appear on both sides of the tracheobronchial diverticulum, enlarging to occlude the aperture of the larynx until the third month, when the lumen is restored (failure of recanalisation produces laryngeal webs/atresia)
- The hypobranchial eminence becomes the epiglottis
- The **thyroid cartilage** develops from the ventral ends of the **fourth pharyngeal arch** cartilages (two lateral plates, each with two chondrification centres)
- The **cricoid cartilage** and tracheal cartilages develop from the **sixth arch** during the sixth week
- Each arch receives afferent and efferent nerve supply — the fourth and sixth arches are supplied by the **superior** and **recurrent laryngeal** branches of the vagus nerve respectively

### Embryology of the Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve

The primitive RLN enters the sixth visceral arch below the sixth aortic arch artery on each side:

| Side | Relationship | Adult Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Left | Sixth arch artery retained | Loops under ligamentum arteriosum (aortic arch) |
| Right | Sixth arch artery disappears | Loops under fourth arch artery (subclavian artery) |

**Non-recurrent laryngeal nerve:** Occurs when the proximal portion of the right fourth arch artery disappears — the nerve passes directly from the vagal trunk to the larynx without recurring. This is associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria).

### Embryology of the Trachea and Bronchial Tree

The trachea and two lateral bronchial buds form from the lung bud. At the fifth week each bronchial bud develops into a main bronchus — right forms three secondary bronchi, left forms two. By the end of the sixth month there are approximately 17 generations of subdivisions; an additional 6 divisions occur postnatally.

## PART B: Framework of the Larynx

### Cartilages

**Thyroid cartilage:** Two laminae fused anteriorly at the laryngeal prominence. Angle of fusion is approximately 90° in men and 120° in women. Superior and inferior cornua posteriorly. The oblique line on the external surface marks attachment of thyrohyoid, sternothyroid and inferior constrictor muscles.

**Cricoid cartilage:** The only complete cartilaginous ring in the airway. Deep broad lamina posteriorly, narrow arch anteriorly. Articulates with the inferior cornua of the thyroid cartilage (cricothyroid joint) and with the arytenoid cartilages on its sloping shoulders.

**Arytenoid cartilages:** Irregularly shaped three-sided pyramids. **Vocal process** (anterior) — attachment of vocal folds. **Muscular process** (lateral) — attachment of PCA and lateral cricoarytenoid muscles.

**Epiglottis:** Thin, leaf-like sheet of elastic fibrocartilage projecting upwards behind the tongue. Attached to the thyroid cartilage by the thyroepiglottic ligament and to the hyoid by the hyoepiglottic ligament.

**Corniculate cartilages** (of Santorini) — sit atop arytenoids. **Cuneiform cartilages** (of Wrisberg) — in the aryepiglottic folds, providing structural rigidity.

### Ligaments and Membranes

The fibroelastic membrane beneath the mucosa forms an internal framework divided by the laryngeal ventricle into:

- **Upper (quadrilateral membrane):** Upper margin forms the aryepiglottic fold; lower margin thickened as the vestibular ligament (false vocal fold)
- **Lower (conus elasticus/cricovocal ligament):** Upper free border constitutes the vocal ligament (true vocal fold). Midline anterior thickening forms the cricothyroid ligament

## PART C: Muscles of the Larynx

### Extrinsic Muscles

Maintain the position of the larynx in the neck. The infrahyoid muscles (depressors) work in synergy with the suprahyoid muscles (elevators).

| Group | Muscles | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Suprahyoid (elevators) | Mylohyoid, geniohyoid, stylohyoid, digastric, stylopharyngeus | Elevate hyoid/larynx |
| Infrahyoid (depressors) | Sternohyoid, sternothyroid, thyrohyoid, omohyoid | Depress hyoid/larynx |

Under normal conditions, descent of the larynx is due to elastic recoil of the trachea and lower respiratory tract — relaxation of the suprahyoid muscles is the only requirement.

### Intrinsic Muscles

All intrinsic muscles are paired and control the overall position, shape, elasticity and viscosity of the vocal folds.

| Muscle | Origin | Insertion | Action | Effect on VF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Posterior cricoarytenoid (PCA) | Back of cricoid lamina | Muscular process of arytenoid | **Only abductor** | Opens glottis; elongates and thins VF |
| Lateral cricoarytenoid | Lateral arch of cricoid | Muscular process | Adducts | Lowers, elongates and thins VF |
| Transverse arytenoid (unpaired) | Posterior surface of one arytenoid | Same on opposite arytenoid | Adducts | Closes posterior glottis |
| Oblique arytenoid | Muscular process | Apex of opposite arytenoid | Adducts | Weak sphincter of inlet |
| Thyroarytenoid (vocalis) | Back of thyroid prominence | Vocal process and arytenoid body | Shortens and thickens VF | Rounds VF edge; actively stiffens body |
| Cricothyroid | Anterior arch of cricoid | Lower border of thyroid lamina and inferior cornu | Lengthens VF (visor-like motion) | Elongates, thins, stiffens VF; sharpens edge |

### Nerve Supply of Intrinsic Muscles

**All intrinsic muscles are supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve EXCEPT the cricothyroid, which is supplied by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve.**

The unpaired transverse arytenoid and paired oblique arytenoids make up the interarytenoid muscle. The PCA is arguably the most important muscle in the body as it is the sole abductor of the vocal cords.

## PART D: Nerve Supply of the Larynx

### Superior Laryngeal Nerve (SLN)

Arises from the inferior ganglion of the vagus. Divides at the level of the greater horn of the hyoid into:

- **Internal branch (sensory):** Pierces the thyrohyoid membrane — supplies sensation above the vocal folds (epiglottis, vallecula, vestibule, aryepiglottic fold, piriform fossa mucosa). Also carries afferent fibres from neuromuscular spindles
- **External branch (motor):** Supplies the cricothyroid muscle

Galen's anastomosis — a purely sensory connection between the internal branch of the SLN and the RLN.

### Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve (RLN)

| Feature | Right RLN | Left RLN |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Vagus at right subclavian artery | Vagus at aortic arch |
| Course | Loops under subclavian artery | Loops under aortic arch and ligamentum arteriosum |
| Groove | Ascends in tracheo-oesophageal groove | Ascends in tracheo-oesophageal groove |
| Entry | Deep to inferior constrictor, behind cricothyroid joint | Deep to inferior constrictor, behind cricothyroid joint |

**Relationship to inferior thyroid artery:** Variable — the nerve may cross in front of, behind, or between the terminal branches of the artery. On the right, equal chance of being in any of three locations relative to the artery; on the left, more likely to lie posterior.

### Steps to Prevent RLN Damage During Surgery

1. Identify the nerve early in the procedure — use consistent anatomical landmarks (tracheo-oesophageal groove, inferior thyroid artery, cricothyroid joint)
2. The nerve enters the larynx behind the cricothyroid joint — this is a reliable landmark for identification
3. Capsular dissection of the thyroid gland staying close to the gland minimises risk
4. Ligate branches of the inferior thyroid artery individually close to the gland, not on the trunk (risk of including the nerve)
5. Avoid excessive traction, clamping, cautery or suction near the nerve
6. Preserve the posterior branch of the inferior thyroid artery where possible to maintain parathyroid blood supply
7. Use intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM) — though this supplements but does not replace visual identification
8. Be aware of non-recurrent laryngeal nerve on the right side (associated with aberrant right subclavian artery)
9. In revision surgery, approach from below the area of scarring to identify the nerve in virgin tissue

## PART E: Spaces Within the Larynx — Barriers to Cancer Spread

### Pre-Epiglottic Space

A wedge-shaped space bounded anteriorly by the thyrohyoid ligament and hyoid bone, posteriorly by the epiglottis, and superiorly by the hyoepiglottic ligament. Tumour may spread into this area through small perforations in the epiglottis or through the hyoepiglottic ligament. The pre-epiglottic space is continuous laterally with the paraglottic space — no anatomical boundary separates them.

### Paraglottic Space

Bounded laterally by the thyroid cartilage, medially by the conus elasticus and quadrangular membrane, and posteriorly by the piriform fossa mucosa. Contains the laryngeal ventricles and saccules. Tumours may spread extensively in this submucosal space without significant mucosal changes.

### Barriers to Cancer Spread

| Structure | Barrier Function |
|---|---|
| Conus elasticus | Separates glottis from paraglottic space — limits VF cancer from reaching paraglottic fat |
| Quadrilateral membrane | Separates supraglottic structures from paraglottic space |
| Vocal ligament | VF firmly bound — no lymphatics in this plane; early glottic cancers do not spread to lymph nodes |
| Anterior commissure tendon (Broyle's ligament) | Connects VF to thyroid cartilage — potential pathway for tumour to reach thyroid cartilage |
| Thyroid cartilage | Barrier to extralaryngeal spread (may be breached at ossified areas) |
| Laryngeal ventricle | Separates supraglottic from glottic compartments |

### Lymphatic Drainage (Cancer Implications)

The vocal folds are firmly bound to the underlying vocal ligament and there are **no lymphatics in this plane** — early cancers of the vocal folds do not readily spread to lymph nodes. The lymphatic drainage is separated by the vocal folds into upper and lower drainage systems:

- **Above vocal folds:** Drain through the thyrohyoid membrane to upper deep cervical nodes
- **Below vocal folds:** Drain to lower deep cervical chain through prelaryngeal (Delphian) and pretracheal nodes

## PART F: Laryngocoele

A laryngocoele is an abnormal dilatation of the lateral-most region of the laryngeal ventricle. The sac is lined with pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium, maintaining a connection to the ventricle. Dilatation of the saccule results in formation of a laryngocoele.

### Classification

| Type | Extent |
|---|---|
| Internal | Does not extend laterally through the thyrohyoid membrane |
| External | Exists lateral to the thyrohyoid membrane |
| Mixed | Both internal and external components |

### Aetiology and Epidemiology

More common in men, increasing with age. Rare — incidence 1 per 2.5 million per year. Factors that increase intraglottic pressure (glass blowing, trumpet playing, weight lifting) are suggested as having a role. Important association with laryngeal SCC within the ventricle — endoscopic examination is advisable.

### Clinical Features

- **External:** Neck lump that may inflate on Valsalva or empty with external pressure (Bryce's sign)
- **Internal:** Hoarseness, dysphagia, globus; smooth swelling of the false cord on flexible nasendoscopy
- **Laryngopyocoele:** Forms when the neck of the sac becomes blocked and infected — presents with pyrexia, dysphagia and potential airway compromise

### Management

Surgical excision is advised prior to evolution into a laryngopyocoele. Can be via classical lateral external approach or endoscopic CO2 laser excision (effective for both internal and external components). Laryngopyocoele requires IV antibiotics and dexamethasone, needle aspiration of external component if feasible, and delayed excision after resolution of infection.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The laryngeal epithelium is endodermal in origin; cartilage, muscles and vasculature arise from the mesoderm of the fourth and sixth branchial arches — the thyroid cartilage from the fourth arch, the cricoid from the sixth
> 2. The left RLN loops under the aortic arch (ligamentum arteriosum), the right under the subclavian artery — a non-recurrent right laryngeal nerve occurs when the right fourth arch artery disappears (associated with aberrant subclavian artery)
> 3. All intrinsic muscles are supplied by the RLN except the cricothyroid (external branch of SLN) — the PCA is the only abductor and is arguably the most important muscle in the body
> 4. The pre-epiglottic and paraglottic spaces are continuous with no anatomical boundary — tumours may spread unrestricted between them in the submucosal plane
> 5. The vocal folds have no lymphatics — early glottic cancers do not readily metastasise to lymph nodes; lymphatic drainage is divided into upper (above VF, to upper deep cervical) and lower (below VF, through Delphian nodes)
> 6. Broyle's ligament (anterior commissure tendon) is a potential pathway for anterior commissure tumours to reach the thyroid cartilage — it is a weak point in the laryngeal barriers to cancer spread
> 7. The cricothyroid muscle is the only intrinsic muscle that lies outside the cartilaginous framework — it exerts a visor-like action approximating the thyroid and cricoid cartilages to lengthen and tension the vocal folds
> 8. The relationship between the RLN and inferior thyroid artery is variable — the nerve may pass anterior, posterior or between the terminal branches; on the left it is more commonly posterior to the artery
> 9. A laryngocoele is an abnormal dilatation of the saccule of the laryngeal ventricle — classified as internal, external or mixed; association with laryngeal SCC within the ventricle mandates endoscopic examination
> 10. The infantile larynx is funnel-shaped with its narrowest point at the subglottis (contrast: adult larynx narrowest at the glottis) — the softer cartilages collapse more easily on forced inspiration due to the Bernoulli effect

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The pre-epiglottic space is a wedge with its point inferiorly — tumour spread through the fenestrated epiglottis or hyoepiglottic ligament gives access to this space, which connects freely with the paraglottic space laterally
> - Galen's anastomosis between the internal SLN and RLN is purely sensory — the existence of variable anastomoses may explain the variable clinical presentations of laryngeal nerve injuries and patterns of recovery
> - The vocal fold has no mucous glands — it is lubricated by mucus from the saccular glands; radiation destroys these glands, leading to desiccation of the squamous epithelium
> - The cricoid cartilage is the only complete ring in the airway and is key to laryngeal health — luminal mucosal necrosis leads to circumferential scarring and debilitating [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]]
> - In conservation laryngeal surgery, the conus elasticus, quadrilateral membrane and ventricle serve as natural boundaries defining the limits of endoscopic resection — understanding these barriers is essential for oncologically sound surgery
