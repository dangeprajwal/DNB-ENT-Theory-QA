# Mode of Spread of [[Laryngeal Malignancy Etiopathogenesis Staging Management|Laryngeal Carcinoma]] and T1N1M0 Management

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Mode of spread of laryngeal carcinoma. Management planning for T1N1M0 laryngeal cancer.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 14 (Tumours of the Larynx), Chapter 27 (Open Conservation Surgery)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma has characteristic patterns of spread depending on the site of origin. Understanding these pathways is critical for staging, surgical planning, and predicting outcomes. The larynx is divided into three subsites — glottis, supraglottis, and subglottis — each with distinct spread patterns, barriers, and lymphatic drainage. T1N1M0 disease (Stage III) represents early primary tumour with nodal metastasis, requiring treatment of both the primary and the neck.

---

## PART A: PATHWAYS OF SPREAD BY SUBSITE

### Glottic Cancer — Routes of Spread

| Direction | Structures Involved |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Anterior | Anterior commissure, contralateral cord |
| Posterior | Arytenoid, cricoarytenoid joint, posterior commissure |
| Lateral | Thyroid cartilage, paraglottic space |
| Superior | Supraglottic extension, ventricle |
| Inferior | Subglottis, trachea, cricothyroid membrane, thyroid gland |
| Deep | Paraglottic space → extralaryngeal spread |

- The anterior commissure (AC) is a critical landmark — involvement affects surgical planning and is an adverse predictor of outcome
- Paraglottic space invasion indicates advanced T stage (T3)
- Vocal cord fixation indicates deeper invasion — may be due to mass effect, intrinsic muscle invasion, cricoarytenoid joint involvement, or RLN invasion
- Glottic cancer has a LOW rate of nodal metastasis: T1 has very small risk of occult nodes; T2 has ~7% risk of occult spread

### Supraglottic Cancer — Routes of Spread

- Approximately 50% of supraglottic cancers spread to the glottic region
- Epiglottic foramina make the epiglottis LESS of a barrier to spread than other cartilages
- Hyoid invasion is rare (2-4%) — can often be retained during surgery
- "Suprahyoid supraglottic" carcinomas invade: pre-epiglottic space → deep tongue muscles → piriform fossae (rather than paraglottic space)
- HIGH rate of nodal metastasis: >60% positive nodes in advanced disease; propensity for BILATERAL nodal metastasis
- First presentation with neck lump is common (may be asymptomatic until large)

### Subglottic Cancer — Routes of Spread

- Extends caudally and circumferentially
- 50% invade the cricoid cartilage
- 75% have already extended OUTSIDE the larynx at diagnosis
- Clinically detectable nodal metastasis is uncommon, but microscopic incidence of 1 in 3 → requires elective nodal dissection including paratracheal/mediastinal nodes
- Thyroid gland invasion: overall incidence 10.7%; significantly higher with subglottic tumours (RR 7.5) and subglottic extension (RR 4.3)

### Transglottic Cancer

- Spans all three laryngeal subsites
- Involves superficial mucosa and paraglottic space
- Said to originate in the laryngeal ventricle (though precise origin is impossible to determine)
- By definition, at least T3 at presentation

### Summary Table: Spread Patterns

```
                SUPRAGLOTTIS
                     |
          Pre-epiglottic space
          Tongue base / Vallecula
          Piriform fossa
                     |
     GLOTTIS ← Paraglottic Space → EXTRALARYNGEAL
       |              |
  Ant. commissure    Thyroid cartilage
  Contralateral cord  Cricothyroid membrane
       |
   SUBGLOTTIS
       |
   Trachea / Thyroid gland
   Cervical oesophagus
```

## PART B: LYMPHATIC DRAINAGE

### Nodal Metastasis Rates by Subsite

| Subsite | Clinically +ve Nodes (Advanced) | Occult Metastasis | Bilateral Risk |
|---------|------|------|------|
| Glottic | ~20% | T1: very small; T2: ~7% | Low |
| Supraglottic | >60% | >15% | High |
| Subglottic | Uncommon clinically | Microscopic 1 in 3 | Via paratracheal |

- Supraglottic cancers drain to levels II-III bilaterally
- Glottic cancers: T1 — no elective neck treatment; T2 — no consensus on neck treatment
- Subglottic cancers: must include paratracheal/mediastinal nodes (level VI) in dissection
- Supraglottic cancers: neck must ALMOST ALWAYS be addressed even if clinically N0 (occult rate >15%); exception: small cancers of epiglottic tip with minimal invasion

## PART C: MANAGEMENT OF T1N1M0 LARYNGEAL CANCER

T1N1M0 is **Stage III** disease. Management must address both the T1 primary tumour and the N1 cervical node.

### Management of the Primary (T1)

**Glottic T1:**
- TLM (first choice for T1a)
- RT (first choice for T1b, or equal to TLM)
- OPL (third choice)

**Supraglottic T1:**
- RT or TLM/TORS (first choice)
- OPL (second choice)

### Management of the Neck (N1)

For N1 disease, the neck must be treated concurrently:

**If primary treatment is surgical (TLM/OPL):**
- Selective [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] (levels II-IV) on the involved side
- Consider contralateral neck dissection for supraglottic primaries (bilateral drainage)

**If primary treatment is radiation:**
- Include the involved neck in the radiation field
- Ipsilateral levels II-IVa treated to therapeutic dose (65-66 Gy)
- Contralateral at-risk nodal levels treated to prophylactic dose (54 Gy)

### Combined Modality Considerations

| Approach | Primary | Neck | Notes |
|----------|---------|------|-------|
| Surgery + adjuvant RT | TLM or OPL | Neck dissection | Adjuvant RT if adverse features |
| Primary RT | RT to larynx + neck | - | IMRT-based, 30 fractions |
| CRT | Concurrent cisplatin + RT | - | For adverse factors |

### Adjuvant Treatment After Surgery
Post-operative RT is indicated for:
- Positive or close margins
- Extracapsular nodal spread
- Multiple positive nodes

Post-operative CRT (cisplatin) is indicated for:
- Positive margins AND extracapsular spread (highest risk)

### Follow-up
- Residual neck nodes after CRT in N2b/N2c/N3: PET scan at 10-12 weeks → salvage neck dissection if positive/equivocal

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Glottic cancer has characteristic LOW nodal rate (20% advanced) vs supraglottic (>60% advanced)
> 2. Anterior commissure involvement is an adverse prognostic factor affecting treatment choice and local control
> 3. Paraglottic space invasion = T3 staging; vocal cord fixation = T3
> 4. 50% of supraglottic cancers spread to glottic region; epiglottic foramina are NOT a barrier
> 5. Subglottic cancer: 50% invade cricoid, 75% are already extralaryngeal at diagnosis; thyroid gland invasion RR 7.5
> 6. T1N1M0 is Stage III — requires treatment of BOTH primary and neck
> 7. For T1a glottic cancer: TLM is first choice; for T1b: RT or TLM
> 8. Neck treatment: selective neck dissection (levels II-IV) for surgical primary; include in RT field for non-surgical
> 9. Supraglottic primaries: neck must almost always be treated (even N0) due to >15% occult rate with bilateral propensity
> 10. Transglottic cancer is by definition at least T3 — originates in ventricle, spans all three subsites


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The T2 glottic tumour with impaired cord mobility has reduced local control similar to T3 with fixation — consider aggressive treatment
> - Thyroidectomy is NOT routine in [[Laryngectomy Rehabilitation Voice Prosthesis|total laryngectomy]]; reserve for subglottic tumours or subglottic extension (overall incidence 10.7%)
> - Tracheal SCC is exceedingly rare despite same carcinogen exposure as larynx — thought to be due to highest density of immunologically active cells in tracheal mucosa
> - Hyoid invasion in supraglottic cancer is only 2-4% — hyoid can often be retained during surgery
> - Neck dissection for subglottic cancer must include paratracheal nodes (level VI) — unlike glottic/supraglottic disease
