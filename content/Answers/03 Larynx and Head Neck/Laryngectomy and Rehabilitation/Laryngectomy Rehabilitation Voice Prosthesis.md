## PART A: SEQUELAE OF TOTAL LARYNGECTOMY

Total laryngectomy is major life-changing surgery that results in permanent alterations to breathing, swallowing and voice production. The consequences are extensive and rehabilitation must begin **before surgery** with pre-operative counselling by the Speech and Language Therapist (SLT) and Clinical Nurse Specialist (CNS). Patients and their close relatives need time, repeated explanations aided by information sheets, diagrams, photos and videos, and the opportunity to meet other carefully selected laryngectomees.

### Anatomical and Physiological Sequelae

| System | Sequelae |
|--------|----------|
| Airway | Permanent neck stoma; loss of nasal airway for breathing |
| Voice | Loss of vocal cords and normal voice production |
| Swallow | Altered swallow mechanics (loss of laryngeal protection) |
| Respiration | Loss of nasal humidification, filtration and warming of air |
| Olfaction | Loss/reduction of smell (bypass of nasal airflow) |
| Other | Reduced air resistance → suboptimal gaseous exchange; reduced cough effectiveness; psychosocial impact |

### Loss of Nasal Functions

The loss of the normal nasal airway pathway means air is taken in directly via the stoma, bypassing the nose. This results in loss of humidification, filtration and warming of inspired air. The loss of height from nose to stoma reduces air resistance within the lungs, leading to less optimal gaseous exchange. This leads to increased mucus production, crusting, recurrent chest infections and chronic coughing.

### Smell Rehabilitation

Loss of smell occurs because air no longer passes through the nasal cavity during normal breathing. The nasal airflow induced olfaction technique (NAIM — "polite yawning" technique) can be taught, where the patient creates a negative pressure in the oral cavity by lowering the jaw and tongue, drawing air through the nose and over the olfactory epithelium. This allows partial restoration of olfactory function.

### Heat and Moisture Exchange (HME)

Essential areas for rehabilitation include respiration, swallowing and voice production. The HME works as an artificial nose, reducing mucus over-production due to loss of nasal humidification, warming and filtering the air, thus reducing coughing and assisting sleep.

The HME consists of a small filter contained in a cassette, attached in front of the stoma either by a base plate stuck to the parastomal skin or inserted into a laryngectomy tube. Different designs exist, with specifications for daily activity, sleep and exercise.

**Critical timing**: An HME should be provided within a few days of surgery to minimize the risk of becoming accustomed to the reduction in respiratory pressure, which can be difficult to re-educate later.

Failure to use an HME is mostly related to getting it to attach and remain attached. Difficulties may be due to post-treatment effects on the neck tissue or because the sternocleidomastoid muscles were not divided at laryngectomy to provide a flattened parastomal area. The increased need for pedicled or [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]] reconstruction of laryngectomy defects after chemo[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] also distorts the anatomy around the stoma making the use of standard base plates difficult. Several randomized studies have shown clear benefit from HME use in reducing pulmonary symptoms and improving lung function.

## PART B: METHODS OF VOICE REHABILITATION

Three methods of voice production are available after total laryngectomy:

### 1. Oesophageal Speech

Oesophageal speech requires air to be taken into the top of the oesophagus either by injection or inhalation, and then forced out again causing the newly reconstructed pharynx (the pharyngo-oesophageal or PE segment/vibratory segment) to vibrate in response to the flow of air and produce sound. This sound is amplified by the resonating cavities above (pharynx, nose and mouth) and modified into speech by the articulators — tongue, teeth, lips and soft palate.

- **Advantages**: No equipment needed, both hands free
- **Disadvantages**: Difficult to learn, limited air reservoir (short phrases), quieter voice
- **Success rate**: Only a minority achieve fluent oesophageal speech

### 2. Electrolarynx (Artificial Larynx)

The electrolarynx produces a sound that is then transmitted into the resonating cavities either by direct pressure of the machine against the skin of the upper neck or through a tube directly into the oral cavity.

- **Advantages**: Easy to use, immediate communication possible
- **Disadvantages**: Robotic quality voice, requires one hand to hold the device, battery dependent

### 3. Surgical Voice Restoration (SVR) — Tracheo-oesophageal Puncture (TEP)

SVR has largely replaced the other methods, being easier to learn and producing better quality voice. It involves the creation of a tracheo-oesophageal puncture (TEP) at the time of primary surgery (primary TEP) or at a later date (secondary TEP). The TEP is a fistula through the posterior tracheal wall into the oesophagus, stented open with a Foley catheter or a one-way valve made of medical grade silicone. Air can pass into the oesophagus for voice production when the stomal exit is temporarily covered, but food and fluid are not aspirated.

### Comparison of Speech Production Methods

| Feature | Normal Voice | Oesophageal Speech | SVR (TEP) |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Initiator | Air from lungs | Air from oesophagus | Air from lungs |
| Vibrator | Vocal cords | PE segment | PE segment |
| Resonators | Nose, mouth, pharynx | Nose, mouth, pharynx | Nose, mouth, pharynx |

## PART C: TEP — PRINCIPLE, TECHNIQUE & PROSTHESIS TYPES

### Principle of TEP Prosthesis

The voice prosthesis is a one-way silicone valve placed in the TEP. When the patient occludes the stoma (by finger or tracheostoma valve), exhaled air is diverted through the prosthesis into the oesophagus, making the PE segment vibrate and produce voice. The one-way valve prevents aspiration of food and fluid from the oesophagus into the trachea.

### Surgical Technique of TEP Creation

#### Position
The puncture is positioned in the **midline**, approximately **10–15 mm below the cut edge of the posterior tracheal wall**.

#### Technique
1. The tip of a pair of curved artery forceps is inserted through the pharyngeal defect and advanced into the upper oesophagus just as far as the puncture site, tenting up the mucosa
2. A scalpel is used to incise minimally and horizontally through the mucosa and muscle onto the tip of the forceps
3. The forceps are advanced into the tracheal lumen and opened to grasp the tip of a 14 French NG tube or Foley catheter
4. The forceps and catheter are withdrawn through the fistula tract
5. The tip of the catheter is passed distally down the oesophagus
6. The catheter is anchored to the skin above the stoma at the end of the procedure

**Important**: Absorbable sutures between the oesophageal wall and the posterior aspect of the trachealis on either side of the proposed puncture position secures the party wall and avoids inadvertent separation. An alternative technique is to insert a valve at the time of surgery through the newly created puncture.

### Optimizing the Surgical Technique for Speech

#### Cricopharyngeal Myotomy
- Carried out once the larynx is removed
- **Purpose**: To avoid hypertonicity and spasm of these muscles during attempted phonation, and to allow expansion of the upper oesophagus providing an air 'reservoir' below the PE segment
- Hypertonicity or spasm is caused by reflex contraction of cricopharyngeal and constrictor muscles when the upper oesophagus is distended with air — a cause of TEP speech failure in **10–12% of patients**
- **Technique**: A short posterior midline myotomy over a distance of **4–5 cm**, starting from just below the level of the TEP site, extending cranially into the fibres of the thyropharyngeus. This divides the circular muscle fibres in the upper oesophagus and the cricopharyngeus. **All muscle fibres must be cut**
- One of the conclusions of the European multicentre audit was that **myotomy improved voice outcomes**
- There seems little contraindication to doing a myotomy on all patients as it may also improve swallow

#### Pharyngeal Mucosa Preservation
- Conserve as much pharyngeal mucosa as possible — postcricoid region, piriform fossae and valleculae (provided safe tumour clearance)
- A transverse, unstretched mucosal width of **at least 6 cm** at the narrowest point is necessary for adequate swallowing and effortless TEP speech
- If residual mucosal strip is less than 6 cm → augment with flap reconstruction

#### Pharyngeal Closure
- Horizontal closure is considered to produce a wider pharynx above the PE segment and may improve resonance for speech
- An alternative technique is the 'T' closure
- Interrupted closure of the constrictor muscle creates a suitable 'tonic' PE segment at the optimal site with a good reservoir below and a wide resonating pharyngeal segment above
- Repairing the thyropharyngeus provides the vibratory segment

#### Stoma Reconstruction
- Size, shape and contour of the stoma and surrounding skin are important for digital occlusion and optimal base plate adhesion for tracheostoma valve housing
- Avoid unnecessary tracheal retraction — do not transect the trachea too low
- Tracheal margins can be sutured to the medial margins of the sternomastoid muscle
- Prominent sternomastoid tendons can be safely divided to ensure a flatter area for better base plate adhesion

### Types of Voice Prosthesis

**"One size does NOT fit all"** — the Blom-Singer and Provox prostheses range include valves of different diameters, lengths, opening pressures and design as well as custom-made varieties, so rarely is it not possible to find an appropriate one.

| Feature | Exdwelling Prosthesis | Indwelling Prosthesis |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Changed by | Patient or carer | Healthcare professional only |
| Hospital visits | Reduced — patient independence | Regular changes required |
| Duration in situ | Changed more frequently | Can remain in situ over extended periods |
| Examples | Blom-Singer duckbill | Provox range, Blom-Singer indwelling |
| Maintenance | Removed, washed, sterilized overnight in 3% H2O2 | Brushed and flushed daily in situ |

### Adjustable Tracheostoma Valve (ATSV) — Hands-Free Speech

SVR voice is produced when the stoma is temporarily sealed by a finger placed in front of it or over the HME cassette. The patient may later use an ATSV allowing hands-free speech. The ATSV seals when the expiratory breath is strong enough to close its valve and remains shut as long as this pressure is maintained. Once the air pressure falls, the ATSV reopens and a new breath is taken in.

## PART D: AIR INSUFFLATION TEST & SECONDARY VOICE RESTORATION

### Indications for Secondary TEP
The technique of TEP with prosthetic valve and voice restoration was originally developed for patients who had failed to achieve adequate oesophageal speech. However, present-day indications are mainly for patients who have:
- Complex resections and reconstructions
- Undergone salvage surgery

### Assessment for Secondary Voice Restoration
1. **PE Segment Tonicity Assessment**: The first step is assessment of PE segment tonicity
2. **Videofluoroscopy**: A preliminary videofluoroscopy will determine whether it is necessary to inject botulinum toxin into the cricopharyngeus
3. **Stoma Assessment**: If the end tracheostoma is too narrow for valve maintenance, a stoma revision may also be indicated at the time of the TEP
4. **Lung Function**: The patient should be judged to have adequate lung function to open the valve and produce voice

### Air Insufflation Test
The air insufflation test assesses the vibratory potential of the PE segment before secondary TEP placement. A catheter is passed transnasally into the upper oesophagus and air is insufflated. If the PE segment vibrates and produces sound, the patient is a candidate for SVR. If no voice is produced, the PE segment may be hypertonic (requiring botulinum toxin injection) or atonic (poor candidate for SVR).

### Timing of Valve Placement
Timing varies — choices are placement of a sterile prosthesis either during surgery or post-operatively once healing has occurred. The length of the TEP must be carefully measured with a sizing gauge and the overall form and function of the site assessed before selecting an appropriate prosthesis from the extensive range available.

## PART E: TONICITY — HYPERTONIC AND HYPOTONIC PE SEGMENT

For optimal outcomes, the newly created reconstructed/vibratory segment must dilate on swallow and vibrate for voice. This area must be surrounded by **tonic muscles** to produce optimum voice.

### Hypotonicity

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Cause | Total pharyngolaryngectomy with reconstruction; thyropharyngeus not repaired |
| Voice quality | Low, weak, whispery, often wet |
| Swallow | Functional |
| Management | Digital pressure against outside of neck, artificially increasing the tone |
| Mechanism | Minimal or absent muscle in the wall to create a PE segment |

### Hypertonicity

| Aspect | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Incidence | 10–12% of patients |
| Cause | Reflex contraction of cricopharyngeal and constrictor muscles when upper oesophagus distended with air |
| Voice quality | High pitched, effortful, difficult or impossible to maintain |
| Swallow | Requires increased pressure |

### Management of Hypertonicity — Stepwise Approach

1. **Primary prevention**: Cricopharyngeal myotomy at the time of surgery (4–5 cm, posterior midline, ALL muscle fibres must be cut)
2. **Low opening pressure prosthesis**: For less severe hypertonicity
3. **Diagnostic workup**: Full videofluoroscopic assessment of swallow and voice, followed by lignocaine injection to confirm the diagnosis
4. **Botulinum toxin injection**: Injected unilaterally at **three sites** along the same short myotomy line. Dosage influenced by degree of hypertonicity, neck fibrosis and stricture, whether a primary myotomy was performed, and the surgical reconstruction. A single treatment is often sufficient to allow continued tonic voice. Can be repeated if necessary
5. **Open myotomy**: Rarely performed since the introduction of botulinum toxin, as it may cause hypotonicity and involves the risk of perforation. Involves reopening the neck and dividing all the muscles including those of the reconstructed/vibratory segment

**Note**: The myotomy should cut through all the muscles in the lower third of the reconstructed segment extending down to the level of the puncture. The upper two-thirds of the vibratory segment is spared leaving these muscles tonic for good voice production.

**Historical alternative**: Unilateral pharyngeal plexus neurectomy — 3–5 branches of the plexus entering the lateral wall of the pharynx are exposed and identified with a nerve stimulator before division with cautery. This technique has not gained widespread use.

## PART F: CAUSES OF VOICE FAILURE AND LEAKING TEP

### Causes of Voice Failure After TEP

1. **Hypertonicity/spasm of PE segment** (10–12%) — most important cause
2. **Hypotonicity of PE segment** — weak/absent vibratory segment
3. **Prosthesis malfunction** — Candida colonization, fouling, blockage
4. **Inadequate lung function** — insufficient expiratory pressure to open the valve
5. **Poor stoma** — too narrow for valve maintenance, poor base plate adhesion
6. **Technical issues** — incorrect prosthesis size/type, TEP malpositioning
7. **Pharyngeal stenosis** — inadequate mucosal width (<6 cm)
8. **Patient factors** — motivation, dexterity, psychological adaptation

### Problem-Solving: Candida albicans

Candida spp. thrive in warm moist environments with a pH near 6.5 (e.g. oesophagus). A voice prosthesis acts as a foreign body within the TEP, becoming covered by a biofilm of bacteria and yeasts, forming plaques. Candida albicans interferes with the prosthesis valve mechanism, causing leakage and aspiration or increasing airflow resistance such that the prosthesis becomes non-functional. Candida can also develop on the outside of the prosthesis burrowing into local tissue and causing an allergic-type reaction (granulation tissue).

**Management of Candida:**
- Daily brushing and flushing of prosthesis in situ, possibly including a liquid antifungal (e.g. nystatin)
- Exdwelling prostheses: regularly removed, washed and sterilized overnight in **3% hydrogen peroxide**
- If estimated prosthesis lifetime is too short → send prosthesis for **mycological analysis** and prescribe appropriate antifungal
- Prostheses impregnated with antifungals are available (at cost)
- Fungal resistant materials are being developed
- Dietary adjustment including addition of **probiotics** should be considered

### Problem-Solving: Granulation Tissue

Granulation is connective tissue occurring as an acute inflammatory response to a complex interaction between the prosthesis, Candida spp. and the patient's tissue. Removal of the voice prosthesis can cause bleeding and the open wound acts as a source for further infection.

**Management**:
- Small areas: cauterize using a **silver nitrate stick**
- Larger areas: require **surgical removal**
- Managing the Candida colonization is essential to prevent recurrence
- Prosthesis function may be disturbed and can lead to TEP closure

### Problem-Solving: Reflux

Reflux causes oesophagitis and heartburn, and encourages Candida spp. growth, granulation tissue formation and TEP dilation. Its influence has been **under-recognized**.

### Central Leakage (Through the Prosthesis)

**Definition**: Leakage of fluid/food through the valve mechanism of the prosthesis itself.

**Most common cause**: Candida interfering with prosthesis valving function — this is the **most common cause** of prosthesis central leakage. This results in aspiration and increases the risk of chest infections.

**Other causes**:
- Prosthesis fouling against the posterior oesophageal wall while swallowing
- Negative pressure created within the oesophagus leading to inappropriate valve opening

**Management**: Selection of alternative prostheses with more appropriate sizes.

### Peripheral Leakage (Around the Prosthesis)

**Definition**: Leakage around the prosthesis when the tissue around the TEP no longer beds down snugly onto the prosthesis barrel. Saliva or liquid penetrates around the prosthesis and is aspirated into the trachea causing coughing or chest infections.

**Causes**:
- An oversized prosthesis pistoning within the TEP, suctioning the bolus through
- A thinned or weakened party wall (below **8 mm**), associated with a wider diameter prosthesis in situ, often in patients with concurrent comorbidity (e.g. **diabetes**)

**Prevention**:
- Minimize trauma by avoiding dilation of the TEP and reducing the number of changes
- When changing the prosthesis: one option is to cut the in situ prosthesis in half, removing the anterior portion and allowing the posterior portion to pass out through the patient
- Hand-load the prosthesis into a smaller gel cap for placement

**Additional Treatment Options**:
- Injecting a filler to bulk the party wall
- Using successively smaller diameter catheters to shrink the TEP (e.g. from 20 FG to 16 FG)
- Using a snugly fitting **extended flange prosthesis** to cover the gap
- Purse string suture procedures (reported to have **poor success rates**)

## PART G: SWALLOW REHABILITATION AFTER LARYNGECTOMY

Removal of the hyoid bone and larynx during laryngectomy and separation of the trachea and oesophagus into discrete systems destroys the normal sequential muscular swallow sequence. **All laryngectomies have a modified swallow.**

### Differences Between Laryngeal and Alaryngeal Swallow

| Phase | Laryngeal Swallow | Alaryngeal Swallow |
|-------|------------------|-------------------|
| Oral | Lips seal; teeth, tongue, palate form bolus | Same as laryngeal |
| Pharyngeal | Soft palate elevates; tongue back approximates posterior pharyngeal wall; strong stripping action | Partial/reduced stripping action due to surgery and denervation |
| Laryngeal protection | Suprahyoids contract, elevating and tilting larynx forwards; epiglottis tilts; laryngeal sphincters close | No larynx — not applicable |
| Oesophageal entry | UES opens by suprahyoid contraction; negative pressure draws bolus in | Reconstructed muscles stretch and relax; reduced pressure from above; gravity assists |
| Oesophageal transit | Gravity + peristaltic wave | Same as laryngeal |

### Key Surgical Aspects for Good Swallow
- Repair of thyropharyngeus provides the vibratory segment
- Suturing suprahyoid muscles down to the thyropharyngeus strengthens the mucosa above the repair, avoiding a pseudoepiglottis and anterior pouch that may affect swallow
- Reattaches the middle constrictor and other suprahyoid muscles important in the swallow reflex
- Adequate pharyngeal mucosal width (≥6 cm unstretched)

### Post-operative Feeding
- Patient must be fed enterally (naso-gastrically or via catheter through TEP/prosthesis directed into the stomach)
- After appropriate healing period (significantly longer after chemoradiotherapy), oral feeding is reintroduced — water → pureed food → soft food → normal diet
- There is increasing support for **early feeding** commencing as early as the **second post-operative day** in patients who have undergone laryngectomy as the primary treatment, as there is **no good evidence that this increases the risk of pharyngocutaneous fistula formation**
- Work on voice production begins when adequate healing has taken place — if phonation begins too early, excess pressure during voicing can lead to suture line breakdown

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Surgical voice restoration should be offered to all patients undergoing laryngectomy
> 2. Management decisions about rehabilitation should be made within dedicated head and neck units by the MDT
> 3. Attention to surgical detail and long-term SLT input is required to optimize speech and swallowing
> 4. TEP is positioned in the midline, 10–15 mm below the cut edge of the posterior tracheal wall
> 5. Cricopharyngeal myotomy (4–5 cm, posterior midline, ALL fibres) should be performed at the time of surgery — European multicentre audit confirmed it improves voice outcomes
> 6. Minimum transverse, unstretched pharyngeal mucosal width of 6 cm is needed for adequate swallow and effortless TEP speech
> 7. HME should commence within a few days of surgery — delay leads to acclimatization to reduced respiratory pressure that is difficult to re-educate
> 8. Hypertonicity/spasm occurs in 10–12% — botulinum toxin has largely replaced open myotomy
> 9. Candida albicans is the most common cause of prosthesis malfunction and central leakage
> 10. Peripheral leakage occurs with party wall thickness below 8 mm, often in diabetics — prevention focuses on minimizing TEP trauma
> 11. Vascularized tissue reconstruction in salvage laryngectomy reduces PCF risk by one-third
> 12. Early feeding (day 2 post-op) does NOT increase PCF risk — supported by evidence


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **"One size does NOT fit all"** — extensive prosthesis ranges (Blom-Singer, Provox) with different diameters, lengths, opening pressures and designs
> - **Botulinum toxin injection technique**: Unilateral at 3 sites along the myotomy line; single treatment often sufficient for continued tonic voice
> - **Reflux is under-recognized** as a cause of Candida growth, granulation and TEP dilation — always consider and treat
> - **Exdwelling prosthesis care**: Sterilize overnight in 3% H2O2; if lifetime too short → mycological analysis → targeted antifungal
> - **Extended flange prosthesis**: Used for peripheral leakage when standard prosthesis does not seal adequately
> - **Stoma revision**: Consider if stoma too narrow for valve maintenance — divide sternomastoid tendons to flatten parastomal area for base plate adhesion
