## PART A: PHARYNGOCUTANEOUS FISTULA — AETIOLOGY & INCIDENCE

### Definition
A pharyngocutaneous fistula (PCF) is an abnormal communication between the reconstructed neopharynx and the skin of the neck following total laryngectomy. It is one of the most significant post-operative complications, with severe impact on duration of admission, costs, quality of life, and can cause severe complications such as bleeding, infection and death.

### Incidence
- **Primary laryngectomy with primary closure**: 31.2% (95% CI 25.8–36.9%)
- **Primary laryngectomy with flap reconstruction**: 22.2% (95% CI 17.9–26.8%)
- **Overall after salvage total laryngectomy (STL)**: Nearly one-third of patients
- **Salvage laryngopharyngectomy**: Up to **50%**
- **After partial pharyngectomy with [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]]**: 10.3%
- **Absolute risk reduction with flap**: 9% (NNT = 11 for flap; NNT = 6 in another analysis)

### Aetiology and Risk Factors

| Category | Risk Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| Prior treatment | Previous [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]]; chemoradiotherapy (especially >64 Gy); salvage surgery |
| Surgical factors | Primary closure (vs flap); inadequate mucosal width; tension on suture line |
| Patient factors | Poor nutritional status; diabetes; hypothyroidism; anaemia |
| Tumour factors | Advanced stage; hypopharyngeal extension; extensive resection requiring reconstruction |
| Post-operative | Early phonation (excess pressure on suture line); early oral feeding (controversial — no good evidence that it increases PCF risk) |

### Pathophysiology
PCF develops when there is breakdown of the pharyngeal closure, typically between days 7–14 post-operatively. Contributing factors include tissue ischaemia (especially in irradiated fields), infection, haematoma formation, excessive tension on the closure, and poor [[Wound Healing Phases Types Factors|wound healing]]. The fistula allows saliva and oral secretions to track from the neopharynx through the wound to the skin surface, causing wound breakdown, skin excoriation, infection and potentially life-threatening complications.

## PART B: PHARYNGOCUTANEOUS FISTULA — CLASSIFICATION

### Classification by Size

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Small | Pin-hole fistula; minimal leak; surrounding tissue healthy |
| Moderate | Established fistula tract; significant saliva leak; surrounding inflammation |
| Large | Extensive tissue breakdown; major salivary leak; wound dehiscence |

### Classification by Timing

| Type | Timing | Description |
|------|--------|-------------|
| Early | Within 2 weeks | Usually at the suture line; related to tension, ischaemia, or haematoma |
| Late | After 2 weeks | May be related to infection, tumour recurrence, or radiation necrosis |

### Classification by Aetiology

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Post-primary laryngectomy | Lower incidence; usually manageable conservatively |
| Post-salvage laryngectomy | Higher incidence (up to 50%); often requires surgical intervention |
| Post-pharyngolaryngectomy | Related to extent of pharyngeal resection and reconstruction method |

## PART C: PHARYNGOCUTANEOUS FISTULA — CLINICAL FEATURES & DIAGNOSIS

### Clinical Features

**Early signs (days 5–10)**:
- Erythema and swelling of the neck wound
- Fever and rising inflammatory markers
- Drain output — salivary character, increased volume, change from serosanguinous to milky
- Wound dehiscence — initial small area progressing to larger breakdown

**Established PCF**:
- Frank salivary leak through the neck wound or drain site
- Skin excoriation around the fistula opening
- Cervical cellulitis and soft tissue infection
- Difficulty with oral feeding — food/fluid visible in the wound
- Foul smell from infected wound

**Complications of PCF**:
- Prolonged hospitalization (weeks to months)
- **Carotid artery blowout** — the most feared and potentially fatal complication. PCF increases the incidence of carotid artery blowouts, particularly after salvage surgery with concurrent chemoradiotherapy
- Wound infection and sepsis
- Nutritional compromise
- Delayed rehabilitation (voice, swallow)
- Pharyngeal/oesophageal stenosis from scarring

### Diagnosis
- Clinical examination — visible leak, palpable wound breakdown
- Oral methylene blue test — swallow methylene blue and observe for staining at the wound site
- CT scan with oral contrast — delineates the fistula tract, identifies collections
- Contrast swallow study — defines the site, size and number of fistulae
- Endoscopy — direct visualization of the neopharyngeal defect

## PART D: PHARYNGOCUTANEOUS FISTULA — MANAGEMENT

### Conservative Management (Small/Moderate PCF)

Most small PCFs will close spontaneously with conservative management:

1. **Nil by mouth** — complete cessation of oral intake
2. **Enteral feeding** — NG tube or gastrostomy feeding to maintain nutrition
3. **Wound care** — regular wound dressing changes, packing of the fistula tract, negative pressure wound therapy (VAC)
4. **Antibiotics** — broad-spectrum antibiotics for associated infection
5. **Manage comorbidities** — optimize diabetes, nutrition, thyroid function
6. **Head-up positioning** — reduces hydrostatic pressure at the fistula site
7. **Anti-secretory agents** — proton pump inhibitors, hyoscine to reduce secretions

**For oropharyngeal tumour surgery**: If PCF occurs, repair using local tissues such as the digastric or sternomastoid muscle, followed by a 5-day period of nasogastric feeding, is usually sufficient to allow successful PCF healing.

### Surgical Management (Large PCF / Failed Conservative)

1. **Local tissue flaps**:
   - Sternocleidomastoid muscle flap
   - Digastric muscle flap
   - Levator scapulae flap

2. **Regional [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|pedicled flap]]s**:
   - **Pectoralis major myocutaneous flap (PMMC)** — the workhorse for PCF closure after failed conservative management; provides well-vascularized tissue from outside the radiation field
   - Deltopectoral flap

3. **Free flaps** (for extensive defects):
   - Radial forearm free flap
   - Anterolateral thigh flap
   - Jejunal free flap (for circumferential defects)

### Prevention of PCF

The evidence strongly supports the use of vascularized tissue from outside the radiation field in the laryngectomy defect:

- **Paleri et al. meta-analysis** (591 patients, 7 studies): Patients who had flap reconstruction/reinforcement **reduced their risk of PCF by a third** (pooled relative risk 0.63, 95% CI: 0.47–0.85)
- Flap closure: PCF 22.2% vs Primary closure: PCF 31.2%
- **NNT**: 11 patients need a flap to prevent one fistula (Paleri); NNT = 6 (Sayles)
- **Sayles et al.** independently arrived at similar conclusions — relative risk of fistula was 0.57 (95% CI 0.37–0.85, P < 0.001) for STL with flap-reinforced closure compared to primary closure alone
- These analyses suggest a **clear advantage** in using vascularized tissue from outside the radiation field

**Additional preventive measures**:
- Adequate pharyngeal mucosal width (≥6 cm unstretched)
- Meticulous surgical technique — tension-free closure
- Avoiding concomitant CRT with radiation doses >64 Gy (increases PCF risk considerably)
- Optimizing nutrition and comorbidities pre-operatively
- Staged [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] 2 weeks before TORS primary resection (for oropharyngeal cancers) — reduces PCF rate

## PART E: POST-LARYNGECTOMY STOMAL STENOSIS

### Definition
Stomal stenosis is narrowing of the tracheostome following total laryngectomy, which can compromise breathing, prevent voice prosthesis maintenance and prevent effective use of HME devices and base plates.

### Causes of Stomal Stenosis

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| Surgical factors | Excessive low tracheal transection; tension on skin-tracheal junction; insufficient skin excision for stoma creation |
| Post-operative | Infection; granulation tissue formation; wound breakdown; excessive scarring |
| Treatment-related | Post-operative radiotherapy; fibrosis from prior chemoradiotherapy |
| Tissue factors | Tracheal retraction; loss of cartilaginous support; keloid tendency |
| Local factors | Granulation tissue; crusting; poor stoma care; candida infection |

### Clinical Features
- Progressive difficulty breathing through the stoma
- Inability to fit or maintain laryngectomy tube, HME or voice prosthesis
- Audible stridor on exertion
- Difficulty with tracheal toileting — inability to suction or clean
- Stoma too narrow for valve maintenance (impacts voice rehabilitation)

### Prevention

The size, shape and contour of the stoma and surrounding skin are important:

1. **Adequate skin excision** — create a stoma of sufficient diameter at the time of surgery
2. **Avoid low tracheal transection** — prevents unnecessary tracheal retraction
3. **Tracheal anchoring** — margins of the trachea can be sutured to the medial margins of the sternomastoid muscle to secure it near the skin
4. **Divide sternomastoid tendons** — if prominent, they can be safely divided to ensure a flatter area around the stoma for better base plate adhesion
5. **Regular laryngectomy tube use** — maintains stoma patency in the early post-operative period
6. **Stoma care** — regular cleaning, humidification, prevention of crusting
7. **Early HME use** — maintains stoma patency and prevents adaptation

### Management

**Conservative**:
- Graded dilators/bougies
- Laryngectomy tube of appropriate size — gradually increasing
- Topical steroid application for granulation tissue
- Silver nitrate cautery for granulation tissue

**Surgical — Stoma Revision**:
- Indicated when conservative measures fail
- Also indicated when stoma is too narrow for valve maintenance at the time of secondary TEP
- Excision of scar tissue and re-fashioning of the stoma
- Local advancement or rotation flaps
- May need to divide the sternomastoid muscle tendons
- Can be combined with secondary TEP creation

## PART F: STOMAL RECURRENCE AFTER TOTAL LARYNGECTOMY

### Definition
Stomal recurrence refers to recurrence of tumour at or around the tracheostome following total laryngectomy. It is one of the most feared complications of laryngectomy, as it carries a very poor prognosis and is often unresectable.

### Incidence
- Reported incidence: 5–15% historically
- Incidence reduced with modern surgical techniques and adjuvant treatment

### Aetiology and Risk Factors

| Category | Risk Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| Tumour factors | Subglottic extension (most important); transglottic tumours; T4 tumours; paratracheal lymph node metastasis; anterior commissure involvement |
| Surgical factors | Inadequate inferior margin; emergency tracheostomy prior to laryngectomy (controversial — may seed tumour cells at stoma site); positive tracheal margins |
| Treatment factors | Delay between tracheostomy and definitive surgery; inadequate adjuvant therapy |

### Pathophysiology
Stomal recurrence may arise through several mechanisms:
- Direct submucosal spread of tumour along the trachea
- Paratracheal lymph node involvement with extracapsular extension
- Implantation of tumour cells at the time of surgery or prior tracheostomy
- Lymphatic spread to peristomal tissues

### Clinical Features
- Visible/palpable mass at or around the stoma
- Progressive stomal stenosis
- Bleeding from the stoma
- Pain and dyspnoea
- Difficulty with voice prosthesis function
- May present with carotid artery involvement

### Investigation
- CT/MRI of the neck and chest — to assess extent of recurrence, involvement of great vessels, mediastinal extension
- PET-CT — to rule out distant metastases
- Biopsy — tissue diagnosis essential before treatment planning

### Management

**Potentially Resectable Disease**:
- Wide excision of the stoma and surrounding tissues including part of the manubrium if involved
- May require mediastinal dissection
- Reconstruction with pedicled flap (PMMC)
- Adjuvant chemoradiotherapy

**Unresectable Disease** (majority of cases):
- Palliative radiotherapy/chemoradiotherapy
- Symptom control — pain management, airway management
- Best supportive care

### Prevention
- Adequate inferior tracheal margins at the time of laryngectomy
- Paratracheal node dissection when subglottic extension is present
- Avoid emergency tracheostomy through the tumour when possible
- Consider pre-operative tracheostomy placed well below the tumour
- Adjuvant radiotherapy to the stoma when risk factors present

## PART G: PAEDIATRIC TRACHEOSTOMY

### Overview
A paediatric tracheostomy differs significantly from the adult procedure in technique, indications and post-operative management.

### Key Differences from Adult Tracheostomy

| Feature | Adult | Paediatric |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| Tracheal incision | Horizontal window or flap | **Vertical slit** |
| Support sutures | Not routinely used | **Lateral support (stay) sutures** placed |
| Post-op tube security | Standard ties | **Stay sutures stuck to chest with tape** to enable replacement if displaced |
| Track formation | Rapid | Slower — stay sutures remain until track forms |
| Tube displacement | Less critical | **Potentially fatal** — immature tract, smaller airway |

### Technique
- A vertical slit is made in the trachea after placing **two lateral support sutures**
- Anaesthetic technique will depend upon why the patient is having the procedure — a stoma for a patient requiring chronic ventilation is very different from one for an acute airway problem
- The anaesthetist, who may have to sit at the head of the patient manually supporting the airway, must guide the surgeon
- Once the surgeon has identified the trachea and made a vertical slit, the endotracheal tube will be slowly withdrawn under direct vision until the tracheostomy tube can be inserted

### Post-operative Care
- **Stay sutures** remain in place and are stuck to the chest with tape while a track forms
- This will enable a replacement tube to be placed in the event of the first one being displaced or falling out
- The initial period is critical — accidental decannulation before tract maturation is a surgical emergency

### Indications
- Prolonged ventilation
- Upper airway obstruction (congenital or acquired)
- [[Subglottic Stenosis|Subglottic stenosis]]
- Craniofacial abnormalities
- Neurological conditions with airway compromise
- Vocal cord paralysis (bilateral)

### Complications

**Early**:
- Haemorrhage — minor bleeding from areas of granulation around the tube, normally controlled with cautery and ongoing medical treatment (steroid and antibiotic ointment)
- Tube displacement/obstruction
- Pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum
- Surgical emphysema

**Late**:
- **Peristomal granulation** — granulation at the skin edge of the tract
- **Suprastomal granulation** — on the anterior wall of the trachea above the tube
- **Granulation at tube tip** — inside the trachea at the tube tip
- Suprastomal collapse or narrowing of the upper trachea
- Tracheal stenosis (stomal/suprastomal)
- Tracheo-innominate artery fistula
- Tracheocutaneous fistula (persistent after decannulation)
- Swallowing difficulties
- Speech and language delay

### Granulation Tissue Management
Granulation may form at multiple sites — the skin edge of the tract (peristomal), inside the trachea on the anterior wall above the tube (suprastomal), and at the tube tip. Granulation tissue acts as an ongoing stimulus for further granulation formation. Management includes cautery with silver nitrate, steroid and antibiotic ointment application, and surgical removal for extensive granulation.

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. PCF occurs in nearly one-third of patients after salvage total laryngectomy; up to 50% after salvage laryngopharyngectomy
> 2. Vascularized tissue (flap) reconstruction reduces PCF risk by one-third (RR 0.63; NNT 6–11) — clear advantage over primary closure alone
> 3. Concomitant chemoradiotherapy with radiation doses >64 Gy considerably increases PCF risk
> 4. PCF increases the incidence of carotid artery blowout — the most feared complication
> 5. Early feeding (day 2 post-op) is supported and does NOT increase PCF risk
> 6. Stomal stenosis prevention begins at surgery — adequate skin excision, avoid low tracheal transection, suture trachea to SCM, divide prominent SCM tendons
> 7. Subglottic extension is the most important risk factor for stomal recurrence
> 8. Paediatric tracheostomy uses a vertical tracheal slit with lateral stay sutures — fundamentally different from adult technique
> 9. The stoma must be adequate for valve maintenance — stoma revision may be needed at the time of secondary TEP
> 10. Peristomal and suprastomal granulation are common complications of paediatric tracheostomy requiring ongoing surveillance


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **NNT for PCF prevention**: 11 patients need flap reinforcement to prevent one fistula (Paleri meta-analysis); 6 patients (Sayles et al.) — always consider flap in salvage cases
> - **PCF from oropharyngeal surgery**: Repair with local tissues (digastric/SCM muscle) + 5 days NG feeding is usually sufficient
> - **Stomal stenosis & voice rehab**: If the tracheostoma is too narrow for valve maintenance, stoma revision should be performed at the time of secondary TEP — do not attempt to force a prosthesis into a narrow stoma
> - **Stay sutures in paediatric tracheostomy**: Lifesaving — remain taped to the chest until the tract matures, allowing emergency tube replacement
> - **Stomal recurrence prevention**: Paratracheal node dissection should be performed when there is subglottic tumour extension
> - **Carotid protection**: In salvage cases with high PCF risk, always consider using vascularized tissue to buttress the pharyngeal repair and protect great vessels
