## Introduction

LASER (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation) is one of the most important surgical tools in modern otolaryngology. Since Jako and Strong pioneered CO2 laser use in the larynx in 1972, laser technology has expanded across virtually every ENT subspecialty. An understanding of laser physics, the classification and properties of different laser types, clinical applications, safety measures and the management of complications — particularly airway fire — is essential knowledge for every ENT surgeon.

---

## Part A — Principle of LASER

### Basic Physics

A laser consists of a **lasing medium** contained within a **resonant chamber** bounded by two mirrors (one fully reflective, one partially transmissive). The lasing medium is excited ("pumped") by an external energy source (usually electrical current), which promotes electrons from their ground state to an excited state — achieving **population inversion** (more molecules in the excited than ground state). Spontaneously emitted photons bounce between the mirrors, colliding with excited atoms and producing **stimulated emission** — each collision releasing a second photon of identical wavelength and direction. The partially transmissive mirror allows a fraction of this amplified light to escape as the laser beam.

### Properties of Laser Light

| Property | Definition |
|----------|-----------|
| Monochromatic | Single wavelength (determined by lasing medium) |
| Collimated | Travels in a single direction with minimal divergence |
| Temporally coherent | Photons oscillate in phase |
| Spatially coherent | Photons distributed equally across the wave front |

### Key Laser Parameters

| Parameter | Definition | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Power (watts) | Energy (joules) per second | Higher power = more energy to tissue per second |
| Spot size (mm) | Diameter of focused beam (0.2–2.0 mm) | Smaller spot = higher power density; defocusing spreads energy for haemostasis |
| Power density (W/cm²) | Power per unit area | Determines tissue effect; more useful than power alone |
| Fluence (J/cm²) | Power density × exposure time | Total "dose" of electromagnetic radiation |
| Output pattern | CW, SuperPulse, UltraPulse | Pulsed modes deliver high peak power in <1 ms bursts, reducing thermal spread and char |

### Laser–Tissue Interaction

When laser light hits tissue, it may be **reflected, scattered, absorbed or transmitted**. Absorbed energy produces the surgical effect through four mechanisms:

1. **Photothermal** (predominant in ENT) — heat causes denaturation → coagulation → vaporization → carbonization
2. **Photoablative** — molecular bonds directly disrupted (e.g., excimer laser)
3. **Photochemical** — light activates photosensitizer drug (basis of PDT)
4. **Photomechanical** — pulsed laser creates shock waves disrupting tissue/calculi (e.g., Holmium YAG lithotripsy)

Absorption requires a **chromophore** — a molecule that absorbs energy at the laser's specific wavelength. For CO2 laser, the chromophore is **water**; for KTP and argon, it is **haemoglobin**.

## Part B — Classification and Types of Lasers in ENT

### Classification by Lasing Medium

| Category | Laser | Wavelength | Chromophore | Delivery |
|----------|-------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Gas | CO2 | 10,600 nm (mid-IR) | Water | Articulated arm, hollow waveguide |
| Gas | He-Ne | 632.8 nm (red) | — | Co-axial aiming beam for CO2 |
| Gas | Argon | 488–514 nm (blue-green) | Haemoglobin, melanin | Fibre optic |
| Solid state | Nd:YAG | 1,064 nm (near-IR) | Tissue proteins | Fibre optic |
| Solid state | KTP | 532 nm (green) | Haemoglobin | Fibre optic |
| Solid state | Holmium:YAG | 2,100 nm | Water | Fibre optic |
| Solid state | Thulium:YAG | 2,013 nm | Water | Fibre optic |
| Liquid (Dye) | Pulsed-dye (PDL) | 585 nm (yellow) | Haemoglobin | Fibre optic |
| Semiconductor | Diode | 810–980 nm | Variable | Fibre optic |

### Classification by Other Criteria

**By wavelength region:** Ultraviolet (excimer) → Visible (KTP, argon, PDL) → Near-infrared (Nd:YAG, diode) → Mid-infrared (CO2, Holmium, Thulium)

**By tissue penetration depth:** Superficial (CO2 — several µm) → Intermediate (KTP, argon — 1–2 mm) → Deep (Nd:YAG — several mm)

**By delivery system:** Mirror/articulated arm (CO2) vs fibre-optic delivery (KTP, Nd:YAG, Holmium, argon, diode, PDL)

**By output mode:** Continuous wave (CW) vs pulsed (SuperPulse, UltraPulse, Q-switched)

## Part C — Applications of Lasers in Otolaryngology

### Applications by Subspecialty

**Laryngology (largest application):**
- Transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) for glottic, supraglottic and hypopharyngeal carcinoma (CO2)
- Phonosurgery — vocal fold polyps, nodules, cysts, Reinke's oedema (CO2)
- [[Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis Larynx|Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis]] (CO2, KTP)
- Angiolytic therapy for early glottic cancer (KTP, PDL)
- Posterior cordotomy for bilateral [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal fold paralysis]] (CO2)
- [[Subglottic Stenosis|Subglottic stenosis]] — radial incisions (CO2)

**Otology:**
- Stapedotomy — precise fenestration without mechanical trauma (CO2, KTP, argon)
- Ossicular surgery (KTP)

**Rhinology:**
- Turbinate reduction — vaporization/coagulation (CO2, Nd:YAG, KTP, argon, diode)
- Endonasal [[Lacrimal System DCR|DCR]] (Holmium:YAG, KTP, diode)
- Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (KTP, Nd:YAG)

**Oropharynx/Oral Cavity:**
- LAUP for snoring (CO2, Nd:YAG)
- Oral cavity leukoplakia/erythroplakia (CO2)
- Tongue base reduction in [[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|OSA]] (CO2 via TORS-compatible systems)

**Tracheobronchial:**
- Tracheal/bronchial tumour ablation (Nd:YAG via fibre)
- Tracheal/bronchial papillomatosis (KTP, Nd:YAG via fibre)

**Facial Plastics:**
- Vascular birthmarks, haemangiomas (PDL, KTP)
- Skin resurfacing (CO2, erbium:YAG)

### Emerging Roles

- **Thulium:YAG and Holmium:YAG** — newer solid-state lasers with water absorption similar to CO2 but deliverable via fibre optic; used for TLM and transoral robotic surgery (TORS), overcoming the line-of-sight limitation of the traditional CO2 micromanipulator
- **Hollow waveguide systems** (BeamPath™, FiberLase™) — allow CO2 laser delivery via flexible guides for use with TORS, flexible endoscopes and outpatient settings
- **Photoangiolytic therapy** (KTP/PDL) for early glottic carcinoma as an alternative to excisional TLM
- **Office-based laser procedures** — fibre-delivered KTP/PDL for vocal fold lesions under local anaesthesia via flexible laryngoscopy
- **Photodynamic therapy (PDT)** — photosensitizer + light for superficial mucosal malignancies (90% response rate reported)
- **AcuBlade™** — automatic scanning micromanipulator for precise linear/curvilinear incisions and circular ablation

## Part D — Laser Safety Measures in the Operation Theatre

### Regulatory Framework

Every hospital must have a **Laser Protection Adviser** (medical physicist) who produces **local laser rules** based on national guidance. All operating rooms used for laser surgery must comply with strict safety protocols.

### Safety Measures

| Category | Measures |
|----------|---------|
| Access control | Warning signs on all doors; doors locked during laser use; only authorized personnel permitted |
| Key control | Laser key held by a senior team member; laser in standby mode when not actively firing |
| Eye protection | All staff wear wavelength-specific protective goggles (CO2 → clear lenses; KTP/Nd:YAG → specific tinted lenses); patient's eyes taped shut and covered with saline-soaked gauze |
| Skin/tissue protection | Patient draped with wet towels/gauze surrounding operative site; non-target tissue shielded |
| Airway protection | Laser-resistant ET tubes used (metal, ceramic or Teflon); cuffs inflated with saline/methylene blue (not air); FiO2 kept <30% during laser firing; avoid N2O (supports combustion) |
| Equipment | Endoscopic instruments blackened/non-reflective to prevent specular reflection; no shiny instruments in laser field |
| Smoke evacuation | Smoke evacuator mandatory — removes potentially infectious plume (HPV viral particles documented in laser plume); staff wear high-filtration masks |
| Training | Mandatory training for all medical, nursing and anaesthetic staff; signed documentation of competency |
| Fire preparedness | Fire extinguisher, basin of water and saline in immediate proximity; fire drill protocol posted |

### Specific Eye Hazards by Laser Type

| Laser | Wavelength | Primary Ocular Risk |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| CO2 | 10,600 nm | Corneal burns (absorbed by corneal water) |
| Nd:YAG | 1,064 nm | Retinal damage (transmitted through cornea/lens, focused on retina) |
| KTP / Argon | 488–532 nm | Retinal damage |
| PDL | 585 nm | Retinal damage |

## Part E — Limitations and Complications of Surgical Lasers

### Limitations

1. **High capital and maintenance cost** — laser units are expensive; require dedicated infrastructure, trained staff and ongoing servicing
2. **Training requirements** — mandatory training and certification before use; steep learning curve
3. **Limited haemostasis** — CO2 laser haemostatic only for vessels ≤1 mm; larger vessels require clips or diathermy
4. **Line-of-sight delivery** — traditional CO2 micromanipulator requires direct line-of-sight; hollow waveguides partially overcome this but are not universally available
5. **Thermal collateral damage** — CW mode causes lateral thermal spread; Nd:YAG penetrates deeply and may cause delayed necrosis
6. **Fire and combustion risk** — unique to laser surgery; requires specialized airway management
7. **Tissue char and carbonization** — excessive char obscures the surgical field and prevents histopathological assessment of margins
8. **Laser plume** — contains potentially infectious viral particles (HPV documented), chemical carcinogens and fine particulate matter
9. **Vocal fold fibrosis** — late complication of repeated CO2 laser laryngeal surgery; interarytenoid fibrosis, glottic webbing and arytenoid fixation reported in 13–45% of patients with recurrent respiratory papillomatosis
10. **Depth assessment difficulty** — some laser systems offer "preset depth" but this is a software calculation based on average absorption — actual depth varies with tissue type, hydration and vascularity

### Complications

**Airway Fire:**
- The most feared laser complication
- Incidence: 0.4–1.5% in large series
- Occurs when laser ignites flammable materials (ET tube, drapes, gauze) in the oxygen-enriched airway environment
- Risk increases with: FiO2 >30%, N2O use, prolonged laser strike, unprotected tube, dry gauze

**Post-operative Haemorrhage:**
- Significant risk in larger TLM resections
- Sentinel bleeding episodes must be taken seriously — may herald major delayed haemorrhage
- Requires endoscopic examination under anaesthesia with clip applicators and diathermy

**Soft Tissue Complications (Laryngeal):**
- Vocal fold fibrosis, interarytenoid fibrosis, glottic webbing, arytenoid fixation — risk increases with frequency and number of laser procedures
- Minimized by appropriate laser settings, careful depth assessment and avoiding circumferential mucosal injury

**Aspiration Pneumonia** — risk with supraglottic and oropharyngeal resections

**Subcutaneous Emphysema** — if laser perforates the airway wall

**Inadvertent Tissue Injury** — reflected or scattered laser light may damage non-target tissue; specular reflection from shiny instruments is a particular hazard

## Part F — Management of Laser Airway Fire

### Immediate Management Protocol

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | **STOP the laser** — immediately cease firing |
| 2 | **Disconnect the oxygen** — remove the breathing circuit from the ET tube to eliminate oxygen supply |
| 3 | **Remove the ET tube** — pull out the burning tube immediately |
| 4 | **Flood the airway** — pour saline into the airway to extinguish any remaining flame or embers |
| 5 | **Re-establish the airway** — mask ventilate, then re-intubate with a new ET tube |
| 6 | **Bronchoscopy** — perform immediate rigid bronchoscopy to assess the extent of airway injury, remove any foreign bodies (ET tube fragments, charred material) and lavage the tracheobronchial tree |
| 7 | **Assess injury** — evaluate for thermal/chemical injury to larynx, trachea and bronchi |
| 8 | **Supportive care** — IV steroids (dexamethasone) to reduce oedema; humidified oxygen; antibiotics if mucosal injury extensive; ICU admission for observation |
| 9 | **Documentation** — complete incident documentation; file adverse event report |

### Prevention of Laser Fire

- Use **laser-resistant ET tubes** (metal spiral armoured with twin cuffs inflated with saline/methylene blue)
- Maintain **FiO2 <30%** during active laser firing
- Avoid **N2O** (supports combustion)
- Use **minimum laser power** and **shortest duration** of strike
- Protect ET tube cuff with **saline-soaked patties**
- Keep **saline and water** immediately available
- Use dedicated **laser anaesthetic protocols** with clear communication between surgeon and anaesthetist

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. LASER produces light that is monochromatic, collimated and coherent through the process of stimulated emission within a resonant chamber achieving population inversion
> 2. Lasers are classified by lasing medium (gas, solid state, liquid, semiconductor), wavelength, tissue penetration depth, delivery system and output mode (CW vs pulsed)
> 3. The CO2 laser (10,600 nm, chromophore = water) is the workhorse of ENT laser surgery; its very shallow tissue penetration (µm) provides precise cutting with minimal collateral thermal damage
> 4. Nd:YAG (1,064 nm) penetrates several millimetres — good for coagulation/debulking but greater risk of delayed necrosis; KTP (532 nm) targets haemoglobin — ideal for vascular lesions and angiolytic therapy
> 5. Power density (W/cm²) rather than power alone determines tissue effect; SuperPulse and UltraPulse modes deliver high peak power in <1 ms bursts, minimizing thermal spread and char
> 6. Emerging roles include Thulium/Holmium:YAG fibre-delivered systems for TORS, hollow waveguide CO2 delivery, office-based fibre-delivered KTP procedures and photoangiolytic therapy for early glottic cancer
> 7. Laser safety requires locked doors with warning signs, wavelength-specific eye protection for all personnel, laser-resistant airway devices, FiO2 <30%, smoke evacuation and mandatory training
> 8. CO2 laser causes corneal injuries; Nd:YAG, KTP and argon cause retinal damage — correct wavelength-specific goggles are mandatory
> 9. Laser airway fire management follows a strict protocol: stop laser → disconnect O2 → remove tube → flood with saline → re-intubate → bronchoscopy → assess and treat injury
> 10. Vocal fold fibrosis occurs in 13–45% of patients receiving repeated CO2 laser ablation for RRP; minimized by appropriate settings, avoiding circumferential injury and limiting number of procedures

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **The CO2 laser cannot be delivered via standard fibre optic** — it requires an articulated arm, micromanipulator or hollow waveguide; this line-of-sight limitation is overcome by newer fibre-compatible lasers (Thulium, Holmium) and hollow waveguide systems
> - **Defocus the CO2 laser for haemostasis** — increasing the spot size at the tissue surface spreads the energy over a wider area, producing coagulation of small vessels (≤1 mm) rather than cutting
> - **Laser plume is an infection hazard** — HPV viral particles have been documented in CO2 laser plume during papilloma ablation; high-filtration masks and effective smoke evacuation are mandatory, not optional
> - **Methylene blue in ET tube cuffs serves a dual purpose** — it is visible (alerts the surgeon to cuff puncture by the laser) and the dye absorbs laser energy, providing additional protection
> - **The pulsed-dye laser works differently from CO2** — rather than direct vaporization, the 585 nm PDL causes photoangiolysis (selective destruction of sublesional microcirculation), making it ideal for small vascular lesions but ineffective against bulky exophytic tumours
> - **Never fire the laser at the anaesthetic tube** — even "laser-resistant" tubes can combust under certain conditions; always use a Boyle-Davis gag or damp gauze to shield the tube shaft during oral laser surgery
