## Part A: Principles of Laser Action and Types of Lasers

*Q162: Types of lasers in ENT. Clinical applications and advantages (2011 Dec)*

### LASER — Definition and History

LASER is the acronym for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Surgical lasers are devices that amplify light and create coherent light beams ranging from the infrared to the ultraviolet parts of the spectrum.

**Historical milestones:**
- 1917: Albert Einstein described the theory of stimulated emission
- 1958: Schawlow and Townes described the working principles of lasers
- 1960: Theodore Maiman demonstrated the first laser action in solid ruby
- 1961: Ali Javan built the first helium-neon gas laser
- 1962: C Kumar N Patel introduced the carbon dioxide (CO2) gas laser
- 1972: Jako and Strong pioneered use of CO2 laser in otolaryngology (Boston, USA)

### Principles of Laser Action

A laser empowers atoms to store and emit light in a coherent form. The electrons in the atoms of a laser medium are first pumped or energized to an excited state by an external energy source. These electrons are then stimulated by external photons to emit their stored energy in the form of photons — this is 'stimulated emission'. The emitted photons have a frequency characteristic to their atoms and travel in step with the stimulating photons. These photons move back and forth between two parallel mirrors triggering further stimulated emission ('light amplification'). One mirror is partially silvered, allowing exit of the intense laser beam.

### Nature of Laser Light

A beam of laser light is:
- **Coherent**: The photons or waves travel in step, or in phase with one another
- **Collimated**: The laser light travels in one direction
- **Monochromatic**: One wavelength or colour (if in the visible spectrum)

### Types of Lasers

Depending on the laser medium, lasers are classified as:

| Type | Examples | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| Solid state | Ruby, Nd:YAG, KTP | KTP (frequency-doubled YAG); used in otology, vascular lesions |
| Gas | CO2, He-Ne, Argon | CO2 most widely used in ENT; He-Ne used as aiming beam |
| Semiconductor | Gallium arsenide, Diode | Used in CD players, some surgical applications |
| Liquid (Dye) | Pulsed-dye laser | Tuneable; suited for pigmented/vascular lesions |
| Free-electron | Research instruments | Tuneable across EM spectrum; experimental |

### Laser Parameters (CO2 Laser — TLM Context)

**Power**: Measured in watts (energy in joules emitted per second). Increasing power increases energy transferred to tissue per second.

**Focused beam spot size**: The beam is passed through a lens to focus on a spot typically 0.2-2.0 mm in diameter. By defocusing, the spot size increases and energy is spread over a wider area — this can be exploited for haemostasis of small vessels up to 1 mm in diameter.

**Power density**: Watts per cm2. More clinically relevant than power alone because the tissue effect depends on both power and spot size.

**Fluence**: Power density multiplied by exposure time (joules/cm2). This measures the total 'dose' of electromagnetic radiation delivered per unit area.

**Power output pattern**:
- **Continuous wave (CW)**: Constant intensity for >0.1 seconds; produces surrounding tissue damage from thermal spread
- **Pulsed modes**: Energy delivered as very rapid (<1 ms) pulses
  - **SuperPulse**: Higher peak energy, less total energy per pulse, more rapid pulse repetition
  - **UltraPulse**: Slightly wider pulses; incisions are marginally finer
  - Both produce desired cutting and coagulation with adequate haemostasis and limited char formation

### Laser-Tissue Interaction

When laser light hits tissue, a proportion is reflected, scattered, absorbed, or transmitted. Four types of tissue reactions:

1. **Photoablative**: Molecular bonds are divided (e.g., ruby laser splitting tattoo ink bonds)
2. **Photochemical**: Infrared, visible or ultraviolet light interacts with photosensitizers (basis for PDT)
3. **Photomechanical**: Pulsed laser disrupts tissue/stones by shock waves (e.g., Holmium YAG for calculi)
4. **Photothermal**: Conversion of absorbed laser light into heat — cutting, coagulation or vaporization depending on wavelength and delivery device. **This is the predominant mechanism in ENT laser use.**

**Heat effects in tissue (as temperature rises):**
Denaturation → Coagulation → Vaporization → Carbonization → Incandescence

**Cutting with a laser is narrow, controlled vaporization.** The lateral thermal effect produces haemostasis by coagulation. This varies with wavelength, rate of energy application, fluence and nature of tissue.

### CO2 Laser — Key Properties

- Wavelength: 10,600 nm (far infrared, invisible)
- Chromophore: Water (absorbed by all water-containing tissues regardless of tissue colour)
- Thermal penetration: Several micrometres (much shorter than other lasers)
- Compared to Nd:YAG, Argon or KTP lasers, CO2 causes much less thermal spread to adjacent tissues
- Only minimally scattered (shorter wavelengths scatter more)
- Energy is rapidly converted to heat, resulting in tissue vaporization and precise cutting
- Cannot be delivered via fibre optic; requires articulated arm or hollow waveguide system
- He-Ne aiming beam is superimposed for surgical targeting (CO2 beam is invisible)

### KTP Laser

- Wavelength: 532 nm (green light, visible)
- Frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser (potassium titanyl phosphate crystal)
- Chromophore: Haemoglobin (oxyhaemoglobin)
- Selectively absorbed by blood vessels — angiolytic properties
- Can be delivered via fibre optic
- Used for vascular lesions, angiolytic therapy for early glottic carcinoma
- Longer thermal penetration than CO2 (several millimetres)

### Nd:YAG Laser

- Wavelength: 1064 nm (near infrared)
- Deep tissue penetration (several millimetres)
- Good for coagulation of large tissue volumes
- Can be delivered via fibre optic (including through flexible endoscopes)
- Used for tracheobronchial tumour ablation (with sapphire-tipped fibre)

## Part B: Clinical Applications of Lasers in ENT

*Q132: Lasers in ENT — types and area of usage. Role of laser in laryngeal surgeries (2014 Dec)*

### Laser Applications by Region

| Region | Laser(s) Used | Application |
|---|---|---|
| [[Stapes Surgery|Stapedectomy]]/tympanomastoid | CO2, KTP, Argon | Stapedotomy, ossicular surgery |
| Endonasal [[Lacrimal System DCR|DCR]] | Holmium YAG, KTP, 810 nm diode | Dacrocystorhinostomy |
| Nasal/sinus surgery | Holmium YAG, KTP, Nd:YAG, Argon, 810 nm | Tumour surgery, telangiectasia, antrostomy, turbinate reduction |
| Nasal polyp reduction | CO2 laser ('Swiftlase' oscillating device) | Polyp debulking |
| Microlaryngeal surgery | CO2 laser | Benign lesions, phonosurgery |
| Laryngopharyngeal tumour | CO2 laser | Resection/ablation (TLM) |
| Vascular laryngeal lesions | KTP, pulsed-dye laser | Angiolytic therapy |
| OSA surgery | Laser (LAUP) | Laser-assisted uvulopalatoplasty |

### Laser Delivery Systems

1. **Articulated arm**: Hollow tubes and mirrors directing the beam; used for CO2 laser (absorbed in fibre optic material)
2. **Micromanipulator**: Connected to operating microscope; creates accurate, reproducible spot on target tissue; used in otology and laryngology
3. **Fibre optic fibre**: Most common technique; flexibility allows use with rigid and flexible endoscopes; laser energy can reach almost any tissue seen with an endoscope
4. **Shaped tip fibre**: Fibre with metal or synthetic sapphire tip; laser heats tip, heat conducts to tissue; used for Nd:YAG tracheobronchial tumour ablation
5. **Hollow waveguide systems**: Allow CO2 laser delivery via flexible guides (OmniGuide BeamPath, Lumenis FiberLase); can be used freehand, with TORS, or via flexible endoscopes
6. **Robotized scanners**: AcuBlade system — automatic scanning micromanipulator producing linear/curvilinear incisions or circular ablation areas

### Role of Laser in Laryngeal Surgery

#### Transoral Laser Microsurgery (TLM)

TLM is a major technique for management of laryngeal and pharyngeal carcinoma using the CO2 laser. It was first used by Strong and Jako in 1971 via a micromanipulator mounted on an operating microscope.

**Key principles of TLM:**
- Resection by tumour division (piecemeal removal) — distinguishes it from traditional en bloc surgical oncology
- Extensive evidence shows no compromise of local oncologic control with systematic piecemeal resection compared to en bloc approach
- The host-disease interface is carefully examined under the operating microscope
- For early glottic carcinoma: circumferential or en bloc resection where feasible, with second-look surgery for uncertain/positive margins
- Complete excision to negative histopathological margins is key to local disease control
- No need to reconstruct the surgical defect — tumour bed remains exposed and accessible for second or third resections
- No negative effect on local recurrence or survival if negative margin requires two or more attempts

**Operative technique:**
- Five Ts determine access: Tongue, Trismus, Teeth, Tumour, Tilt (neck extension)
- Operating microscope with suspension laryngoscopy
- Custom dental protection (thermoplastic guards)
- Endoscopic graspers, diathermy probes, clip applicators
- Normal tissue under tension springs apart with 'clean' appearance; tumour is firmer, less elastic, chars more

**Indications by subsite:**
- **Glottic carcinoma**: T1a (en bloc), T1b-T3 (piecemeal with initial tumour division to assess depth)
- **Supraglottic carcinoma**: Preserve at least one competent arytenoid complex
- **Oropharyngeal carcinoma**: Increasingly used especially for HPV16+ OPC as part of treatment de-intensification strategies; excellent access via Feyh-Kastenbauer retractor
- **Hypopharyngeal carcinoma**: Small T1/T2 tumours often surprisingly straightforward; large tumours contraindicated except in most experienced hands

**Advantages of TLM over open surgery:**
- Decreased need for [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]]
- Decreased need for gastrostomy/tube feeding
- Improved post-operative speech and swallowing function
- No reconstruction required
- Accessible tumour bed for re-excision if margins positive
- Suitable for de-intensification in HPV+ OPC

**Angiolytic therapy**: KTP laser or other angiolytic lasers using haemoglobin as chromophore can selectively destroy the microvasculature of early glottic cancers — early success reported as an alternative to CO2 excision.

#### Phonosurgery — Laser Applications

The laser is not merely a precise surgical knife — the surgeon must understand the effects of wavelength, spot size, wattage and mode (pulsed or continuous), soft tissue interaction and hazards. The three most widely utilized laser types in phonosurgery are CO2, KTP and diode.

In a prospective study (Benninger), both CO2 laser and microsurgery (cold instruments) were excellent tools in trained hands. Laser and cold instruments should be considered synergistic rather than in direct opposition. The authors prefer to limit laser use to:

- **Vascular lesions** or those that bleed on removal
- **Papillomatosis** — CO2 laser excision is treatment of choice; minimal trauma to surrounding tissue (may contain dormant virus); single papillomas grasped and laser excises the base; multiple papillomas managed with submucosal saline injection (hydrodissection) and en bloc mucosal excision
- **Granulomas** — CO2 laser excision combined with bilateral Botox injection to adductor muscles to reduce recurrence
- **Removal of cartilage**
- **Excising large areas of tissue**

**Specific applications in phonosurgery:**

| Pathology | Laser Use |
|---|---|
| Vocal fold nodules | CO2 laser excision at base (rare surgical cases) |
| Polyps | CO2 laser or cold instruments |
| Reinke's oedema | CO2 laser cordotomy incision, myxoedema aspiration |
| Intracordal cysts | CO2 laser dissection via microflap |
| Vocal fold varices | KTP or pulsed-dye laser (angiolytic) |
| Anterior webs | Laser or cold steel division |
| Granulomas | CO2 laser excision + bilateral Botox |
| Papilloma | CO2 laser excision (treatment of choice) |

### Photodynamic Therapy (PDT)

PDT is a subset of photochemotherapy requiring three elements: a photosensitizer drug (administered IV), light of appropriate wavelength, and oxygen. The photosensitizer accumulates within cells and reacts with light and oxygen to form 'singlet' oxygen, which damages cell membranes and produces cell death.

**Advantages**: Photosensitizer is retained in tumour tissue longer than normal tissue, improving therapeutic effect. Not affected by prior [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], chemotherapy or surgery.

**Limitations**: Limited depth of tissue penetration and extensive skin phototoxicity (patients cannot be exposed to daylight during treatment).

**Results in head and neck**: Karakullukcu et al. — 170 patients with early oral/oropharyngeal neoplasms: 90% response rate, 70% complete response (cure) rate. Biel — 25 patients with early laryngeal SCC: complete response despite prior radiation therapy failure in 17 patients.

## Part C: Laser Safety and Complications

### Laser Safety Principles

The following principles apply in all countries (local Medical Physics Department produces local laser rules based on national guidance):

1. A laser beam may hit or damage objects outside the target area and cause fire, tissue damage or eye/visual damage
2. The patient should be shielded by wet gauze or fireproof material from accidental strikes
3. The anaesthetic tube and airway should be protected from accidental strikes that could produce a fire
4. All personnel in the operating room should wear correct eye protection appropriate to the wavelength of the laser
5. A warning sign plus locked doors should prevent unprotected individuals from entering the operating room
6. The key to switch on the laser should be held by a senior member of the operating team to ensure only properly qualified individuals use the laser
7. The operating room and windows should be laser protected
8. Endoscopic equipment should be blackened to reduce accidental reflective strikes of the laser
9. Training for medical and nursing staff should be mandatory
10. Staff should read the laser safety manual and produce signed documentation

### Complications of TLM

**Airway fire:**
- Incidence in large series: 0.4-1.5%
- Time to ignition of fat in O2 concentrations >50% at power >5 W is less than 5 seconds
- TLM is performed with FiO2 <30%, especially with open ventilation systems (jet ventilation)
- Laser-compatible endotracheal and jet ventilation tubes used routinely
- Cuff of ET tube covered with saline-soaked patties
- Smoke exhaustion always used (combustible particles increase ignition risk)
- In event of airway fire: remove ET tube, re-secure airway, bronchoscopy

**Post-operative haemorrhage:**
- Significant risk, especially in larger resections
- Small bleeds should be considered sentinel bleeds
- Requires careful endoscopic examination under anaesthesia with large clip applicators, large-bore suction, monopolar and bipolar diathermy

**Aspiration pneumonia:**
- Risk especially in supraglottic and oropharyngeal resections

## Part D: Coblation Technology and Applications

*2024-117: Coblation technology. Applications of lasers in laryngeal surgery (2024 Oct)*

### Coblation Technology

Coblation (controlled ablation) is a form of radiofrequency ablation that uses bipolar radiofrequency energy to excite electrolytes in a conductive medium (usually saline), creating a precisely focused plasma field. The energized particles in the plasma have sufficient energy to break molecular bonds within tissue, causing tissue dissolution at relatively low temperatures (typically 40-70 degrees C), compared to conventional electrocautery (400+ degrees C). This results in minimal thermal damage to surrounding tissues.

### Principles of Coblation

- A bipolar radiofrequency wand is used in a saline environment
- Radiofrequency energy excites sodium ions in the saline to create a plasma field
- The plasma field is focused at the tip of the wand
- Tissue is dissolved at the molecular level at low temperatures
- Minimal lateral thermal damage compared to conventional electrocautery or laser
- The technology combines ablation and coagulation capabilities

### Applications of Coblation in ENT

**[[Tonsillectomy|Tonsillectomy]]:**
- Coblation versus conventional dissection tonsillectomy has been evaluated in a double-blind randomized controlled trial (Philpott et al.)
- Potential advantages include less post-operative pain and faster recovery
- The Coblation wand provides both tissue dissection and haemostasis
- Used for intracapsular (partial) and extracapsular (complete) tonsillectomy

**Adenoidectomy:**
- Coblation is suitable for adenoidectomy with less blood loss and more complete adenoid removal
- Cost limits its application to adenoidectomy as a sole procedure, while it is not a cost issue when tonsillectomy is performed using the same Coblation wand
- Allows direct-vision partial adenoidectomy to reduce risk of velopharyngeal insufficiency
- Using direct-vision suction coagulation or Coblation generally abolishes the need for swabs as haemostasis can be achieved during the procedure

**[[Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis Larynx|Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis]] (RRP):**
- Radiofrequency coblation has been used for treatment of advanced laryngotracheal recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (Carney et al.)
- Coblation resection of paediatric laryngeal papilloma has been reported (Rachmanidou et al.)
- Provides an alternative to CO2 laser with potential advantages of reduced thermal injury

**[[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|Obstructive sleep apnoea]] surgery:**
- Radiofrequency/coblation devices used for:
  - Soft palate channelling (volumetric tissue reduction)
  - Tongue base reduction
  - Turbinate reduction
- Three devices available: Somnus unit, Celon device (bipolar), Coblator unit
- Coblation assisted upper airway procedure (CAUP) described by Tvinnereim et al.: lateral palatal incisions, fan-shaped channels in soft palate, partial uvulectomy, channelling of pillars and tonsils
- Can be performed under local anaesthesia at multiple levels
- 33% improvement in OSA patients with multilevel therapy; long-term success rate of radiofrequency treatment to palate was 37%
- Less pain than UPPP or LAUP

**Lymphatic malformations:**
- Radiofrequency ablation (coblation) used for treatment of microcystic lymphatic malformations
- Advantage of avoiding thermal injury to surrounding structures

### Coblation vs Laser — Comparison

| Feature | CO2 Laser | Coblation |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 100-450 degrees C | 40-70 degrees C |
| Thermal damage | Minimal (CO2) but higher than coblation | Minimal — least of all technologies |
| Tissue effect | Vaporization/cutting | Molecular dissolution |
| Haemostasis | Small vessels (<1 mm) | Good — integrated coagulation |
| Precision | Excellent under microscope | Good but less microscopic precision |
| Delivery | Articulated arm/waveguide | Handheld wand in saline |
| Cost | High capital cost | Lower capital, higher consumable cost |
| Smoke plume | Significant (risk of HPV transmission debated) | Minimal |
| Main ENT use | Laryngeal surgery, TLM | Tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, OSA surgery, RRP |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Laser light is coherent, collimated and monochromatic. The CO2 laser, introduced by Jako and Strong in 1972, is the most widely used laser in otolaryngology.
>
> 2. The CO2 laser (wavelength 10,600 nm) uses water as chromophore, is absorbed by all water-containing tissues, has very short thermal penetration (micrometres), and causes minimal scatter and thermal spread compared to other lasers.
>
> 3. Fluence (joules/cm2) is the key parameter determining tissue effect — it combines power density and exposure time. SuperPulse and UltraPulse modes deliver high peak power in rapid short pulses to achieve cutting with limited char formation.
>
> 4. The four laser-tissue interactions are photoablative, photochemical, photomechanical and photothermal. The photothermal mechanism (cutting, coagulation, vaporization) predominates in ENT practice.
>
> 5. Region-specific laser applications: CO2 for microlaryngeal surgery and TLM; KTP/Argon for stapedotomy and vascular lesions; Holmium YAG for endonasal DCR; KTP/pulsed-dye for angiolytic therapy of early glottic carcinoma and vocal fold varices.
>
> 6. TLM uses CO2 laser for piecemeal tumour resection through an endoscope under operating microscope — no compromise in oncologic control compared to en bloc approach; advantages include reduced tracheostomy, gastrostomy, and improved swallowing function.
>
> 7. Laser safety essentials: patient shielding with wet gauze, airway protection, correct eye protection for wavelength, locked doors with warning signs, laser key held by senior team member, blackened endoscopic equipment, and mandatory staff training.
>
> 8. Airway fire during TLM occurs in 0.4-1.5% of cases; prevention requires FiO2 <30%, laser-compatible ET tubes, saline-soaked cuff patties, and smoke exhaustion.
>
> 9. Coblation (controlled ablation) uses bipolar radiofrequency energy to create a plasma field in saline, dissolving tissue at low temperatures (40-70 degrees C) with minimal thermal damage compared to both electrocautery and laser.
>
> 10. Coblation applications include tonsillectomy (less pain, faster recovery), adenoidectomy (less blood loss, more complete removal), RRP treatment, and multilevel OSA surgery (soft palate, tongue base, turbinates).
>
> 11. In phonosurgery, CO2 laser and cold instruments are synergistic tools. Laser is preferred for vascular lesions, papillomatosis, granulomas and large tissue excision; the KTP/pulsed-dye laser is preferred for angiolytic therapy of vocal fold varices.
>
> 12. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) uses a photosensitizer drug, light and oxygen to generate singlet oxygen that selectively destroys tumour cells; results show 90% response rate and 70% cure rate for early oral/oropharyngeal neoplasms; limitations include depth of penetration and skin phototoxicity.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The CO2 laser cannot be delivered via standard fibre optic cables (absorbed by fibre); it requires articulated arm delivery or newer hollow waveguide systems (OmniGuide BeamPath, Lumenis FiberLase).
>
> - During TLM, maintain tissue traction and counter-traction when working with the CO2 laser to visualize the cut surface and avoid excessive char — normal tissue springs apart cleanly while tumour is firmer and chars more.
>
> - In phonosurgery, laser should stay superficial to the vocal ligament for benign disease — there is no role for mucosal stripping. Preserve mucosa to prevent scarring and poor voice outcomes.
>
> - For papillomatosis, CO2 laser with submucosal saline hydrodissection and en bloc mucosal excision gives lower recurrence than surface ablation and better definition of tissue planes.
>
> - Granulomas recur frequently after surgery alone — primary treatment is bilateral Botox injection to adductor muscles combined with aggressive LPR treatment and voice therapy; laser excision reserved for airway-obstructing lesions, always with concomitant Botox.
>
> - When using coblation for adenoidectomy under direct vision, a partial adenoidectomy can be performed to leave a rim of tissue at the velopharyngeal junction, reducing the risk of VPI in at-risk children.
>
> - The KTP laser uses haemoglobin as chromophore and is the laser of choice for angiolytic therapy — selectively destroys microvasculature of early glottic carcinoma and treats vocal fold varices without excision.
