## Q(2022-72). TORS vs TOUSS — differences. TOUSS principle and indications (2022 Dec)

*Discuss the differences between TORS and TOUSS. Describe the principle and indications of TOUSS.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapters 1 (History), 13 (Oropharyngeal Tumours), 14 (Tumours of the Larynx)

---

### Introduction

Transoral approaches to head and neck surgery have undergone significant evolution. Transoral en bloc resection for tonsil tumours was initially described in 1951 by Pierre Charles Huet. Current transoral approaches include transoral laser surgery (TOLS/TLM), transoral robotic surgery (TORS) introduced in 2007, and transoral ultrasonic surgery (TOUSS). Both TORS and TOUSS aim to achieve function-sparing surgery for oropharyngeal and laryngeal cancers, reducing the extent of open surgery and the burden of post-treatment morbidity.

### TORS (Transoral Robotic Surgery)

TORS uses the da Vinci surgical robotic system to perform transoral resections. The system provides three-dimensional high-definition visualization, wristed instrumentation with 7 degrees of freedom, and tremor filtration.

**Key features:**
- Uses da Vinci robotic platform with 2-3 robotic arms
- 3D endoscopic visualization with magnification
- Wristed instruments allow access to difficult-to-reach areas
- Surgeon operates from a remote console
- Requires mouth retractor (e.g. Feyh-Kastenbauer or Crowe-Davis)

**Indications:**
- Early-stage oropharyngeal SCC (tonsil, base of tongue)
- Selected supraglottic and hypopharyngeal tumours
- [[Parapharyngeal Space|Parapharyngeal space]] tumours
- Unknown primary with tonsillar biopsy

**Limitations:**
- High capital cost of robotic system
- Bulky instruments may limit access in small oral cavity
- No tactile (haptic) feedback
- Requires specialized training
- Cautery-based cutting — thermal damage to tissues

### TOUSS (Transoral Ultrasonic Surgery)

TOUSS uses an ultrasonic cutting device (harmonic scalpel) via a transoral route for oropharyngeal and laryngeal tumour resection.

**Principle:** The harmonic scalpel vibrates at 55,500 Hz, causing protein denaturation and coagulation at lower temperatures (50-100 degrees C) than electrocautery (150-400 degrees C). This results in less thermal spread, reduced collateral tissue damage, and a cleaner surgical field with less smoke plume.

**Indications:**
- Early to intermediate-stage oropharyngeal SCC
- Base of tongue tumours
- Tonsillar tumours
- Supraglottic tumours
- Selected hypopharyngeal tumours

### Comparison: TORS vs TOUSS

| Feature | TORS | TOUSS |
|---------|------|-------|
| Instrument | da Vinci robotic system | Harmonic scalpel |
| Visualization | 3D endoscopic | 2D direct/endoscopic |
| Cost | Very high (capital + running) | Low (uses existing equipment) |
| Cutting mechanism | Electrocautery | Ultrasonic vibration |
| Thermal damage | Higher (150-400 degrees C) | Lower (50-100 degrees C) |
| Haptic feedback | No | Yes (direct handling) |
| Smoke plume | Significant | Minimal |
| Specimen margins | May have cautery artifact | Better margin assessment |
| Learning curve | Steep | Moderate |
| Access | Bulky instruments | Slimmer profile |
| Degrees of freedom | 7 (wristed) | Limited by handle |

**TOUSS Advantages:**
- Significantly lower cost — no capital investment in robotic system
- Superior tactile feedback — surgeon directly handles instruments
- Minimal thermal spread — better histological margin assessment
- Less smoke plume — improved visibility and reduced viral transmission risk (relevant in HPV+ tumours)
- Slimmer instrument profile — better access in smaller oral cavities
- Can be used alongside standard microlaryngoscopy instruments

## Q(2022-77). Biomaterials. Bioengineering methods for oral & maxillofacial reconstruction (2022 Dec)

*Discuss biomaterials used in ENT. Describe bioengineering methods for oral and maxillofacial reconstruction.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapters 12, 25, 67; Volume 2, Chapter 30

---

### Introduction

Biomaterials are natural or synthetic substances that interact with biological systems and are used to replace, augment, or support tissues. In head and neck surgery, biomaterials play an increasingly important role in reconstruction, from bone plates to tissue-engineered scaffolds. Bioengineering methods aim to create functional biological substitutes that can restore, maintain, or improve tissue function.

### Part A: Classification of Biomaterials

| Category | Examples | Applications in ENT |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| Metals | Titanium, stainless steel | Miniplates, screws, osseointegrated implants |
| Ceramics | Hydroxyapatite, bioactive glass | Bone grafts, middle ear prostheses |
| Polymers | Silicone, PTFE, PDS | Orbital floor repair, nasal implants |
| Composites | Calcium phosphate-polymer | Bone substitutes, scaffolds |
| Biologics | Collagen, hyaluronic acid | Vocal fold augmentation, wound healing |

**Properties required:**
- Biocompatibility — no adverse local or systemic response
- Appropriate mechanical strength
- Controlled degradation (if biodegradable)
- Ability to promote tissue integration
- Resistance to infection
- Ease of fabrication and sterilization

### Part B: Biomaterials in Specific ENT Applications

**1. Oral and Maxillofacial Reconstruction:**
- Titanium miniplates and reconstruction plates — mainstay of facial fracture fixation; semi-rigid internal fixation through extended subperiosteal exposure
- Titanium mesh — used for orbital floor reconstruction; available as prefabricated or custom-made using CAD-CAM technology
- Polydimethylsiloxane (PDS) sheets — for smaller orbital floor defects
- Hydroxyapatite granules — bone void filler
- Resorbable plates (polylactic acid/polyglycolic acid) — paediatric fracture fixation; avoid growth interference

**2. Airway Reconstruction:**
- PDS absorbable stents — used to treat malacia, particularly bronchomalacia, offering advantage over metallic stents
- Decellularized cadaveric trachea — scaffold for tissue-engineered tracheal replacement
- Silicone stents — for laryngotracheal stenosis

**3. Phonosurgery/Vocal Fold:**
- Collagen-based materials (e.g. Terudermis — atelocollagen sponge) — scaffolds for vocal fold regeneration
- Hyaluronic acid/alginate (HA/ALG) hydrogel scaffolds — studied for vocal fold tissue engineering
- Calcium hydroxyapatite (CaHA) — vocal fold injection augmentation
- Autologous fat — [[Injection Laryngoplasty|injection laryngoplasty]]

### Part C: Bioengineering Methods

**1. Tissue Engineering Triad:**
- **Cells:** Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), chondrocytes, osteoblasts
- **Scaffolds:** Provide 3D framework for cell attachment and growth
- **Growth factors:** TGF-beta, BMP (bone morphogenetic protein), VEGF

**2. Methods for Oral and Maxillofacial Reconstruction:**

**(a) CAD-CAM (Computer-Aided Design and Manufacture):**
- Pre-operative 3D CT planning of mandibular reconstruction
- Custom cutting guides for fibular free flap
- Patient-specific reconstruction plates
- Custom titanium orbital floor implants
- Virtual surgical planning for osteotomies

**(b) 3D Bioprinting:**
- Layer-by-layer deposition of biomaterials and cells
- Potential for custom bone and cartilage scaffolds
- Patient-specific anatomical models for surgical planning

**(c) Decellularization:**
- Removal of cellular components from donor tissue
- Preservation of extracellular matrix scaffold
- Applied to tracheal replacement — decellularized cadaveric trachea seeded with patient's stem cells

**(d) Growth Factor Application:**
- BMP-2 and BMP-7 — stimulate osteogenesis in bony defects
- TGF-beta3 — reduces collagen synthesis and scarring in vocal fold healing
- Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) — concentrate of growth factors for wound healing

**(e) Stem Cell-Based Approaches:**
- Mesenchymal stem cells from bone marrow or adipose tissue
- Differentiation into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, or fibroblasts
- Used for vocal fold regeneration and tracheal tissue engineering
- Fetal tissue engineering — explored for congenital airway defects

## Q(2022-79). Phases of HIV infection. Oral manifestations and management (2022 Dec)

*Discuss the phases of HIV infection. Describe oral manifestations of HIV and their management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 23: Human Immunodeficiency Virus (Kim & Holmes)

---

### Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) has had a profound impact on global healthcare since its discovery in 1983. Thirty-four million people are infected worldwide. Head and neck manifestations are prevalent, with up to 100% of HIV patients having some head and neck presentation during their illness. Oral cavity manifestations are extremely common and have been reported in up to 100% of AIDS patients. Oral manifestations are often the first presentation of HIV in otherwise asymptomatic patients.

### Part A: Virology of HIV

HIV is a retrovirus of the lentivirus genus. There are two strains: HIV-1 (global epidemic strain) and HIV-2 (largely limited to West Africa, less virulent). The HIV genetic material is a diploid, single-stranded RNA that uses reverse transcriptase to convert its RNA into double-stranded DNA.

**Key viral genes:**
- **pol** — encodes enzymes for viral replication (polymerase, integrase, protease)
- **gag** — encodes inner structural proteins
- **env** — encodes glycoproteins (gp41 and gp120) for viral envelope

**Mechanism of infection:**
- gp120 binds to CD4 receptor on host T helper lymphocytes
- Viral and host cell membranes fuse
- Reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA to DNA
- Integrase incorporates viral DNA into host genome
- Provirus replicates with host cell DNA
- Monocytes/macrophages also express CD4 and act as reservoirs

### Part B: Phases of HIV Infection

| Phase | Duration | CD4 Count | Viral Load | Clinical Features |
|-------|----------|-----------|------------|-------------------|
| Seroconversion | 2-6 weeks | Sharp decline | Very high | Flu-like illness, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, oropharyngeal ulceration, rash. Highly infectious |
| Asymptomatic | Several years | High, gradual decline | Controlled by immune system | No symptoms; ongoing viral replication and CD4 destruction |
| Early symptomatic | Months-years | Decreasing | Rising | Fatigue, generalized lymphadenopathy |
| Late symptomatic | Progressive | <200 cells/uL | High | Constitutional symptoms: fever, sweats, weight loss, diarrhoea |
| AIDS | Terminal | Very low | Very high | Opportunistic infections, AIDS-defining cancers (Kaposi's sarcoma, PCP). Universally fatal without treatment |

### Part C: Oral Manifestations of HIV

**1. Oral Candidiasis:**
- Most common oral manifestation
- Pseudomembranous form (thrush) — tender white plaque on erythematous mucosa
- Chronic hypertrophic form — heaped-up plaques, not easily removed
- Can be extremely painful, leading to malnutrition, dehydration, wasting
- May coexist with oesophageal candidiasis

*Management:*
- Mild disease: Nystatin mouthwash, clotrimazole or amphotericin lozenges
- Moderate/resistant: Systemic fluconazole
- Severe/refractory: IV amphotericin B or echinocandins
- Send culture for sensitivity if fluconazole-resistant

**2. Hairy Leukoplakia:**
- Unique to HIV-positive individuals
- Arises on lateral border of tongue as white, raised, corrugated or filiform lesion
- Can also occur on dorsal tongue, buccal mucosa, floor of mouth, soft palate
- Usually asymptomatic; requires no treatment

**3. Recurrent Aphthous Ulcers:**
- Larger than in immunocompetent patients
- Solitary or multiple, with erythematous halo, well-circumscribed
- Giant aphthous ulcers cause severe odynophagia
- *Management:* Topical anaesthetics/steroids (mild); topical tetracycline or systemic clindamycin (infected); intralesional triamcinolone acetonide (analgesic within 24-48 hours); oral thalidomide (heals 55% completely, 90% partially — limited to 2-4 weeks due to increased viral load; teratogenic)

**4. Kaposi's Sarcoma (KS):**
- Most common HIV-related oral malignancy
- Caused by co-infection with HIV and HHV-8
- Oral mucosa lesions classically raised, red, may ulcerate
- Majority arise on the palate; also gingiva and oropharynx
- Cutaneous lesions: purple to dark brown, variable size
- *Management:* HAART is mainstay (immune recovery causes regression); localized radiotherapy; surgical excision (recurrence common despite clear margins); topical retinoids; intralesional chemotherapy; laser coagulation; cryotherapy

**5. Oral HPV Lesions:**
- Verrucous, flat, or spiky lesions in oral cavity
- Appear similar to venereal warts
- Can involve entire oral cavity and oropharynx
- *Management:* Surgical excision; recurrence likely

**6. Gingivitis and Periodontitis:**
- More severe than in non-infected patients
- Bleeding from gums with minimal trauma
- Can progress to acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis (ANUG)
- *Management:* Antibiotics against anaerobes + topical chlorhexidine gluconate irrigation; early dental/oral surgery referral

**7. Xerostomia:**
- Secondary to chronic inflammation of major and minor salivary glands
- *Management:* Oral irrigations, saline rinses, salivary substitutes, sialogogues

**8. Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma:**
- Poor prognostic indicator; usually gingiva and alveolar ridges
- Red, exophytic lesions; may arise from Waldeyer's ring
- *Management:* Chemotherapy and radiation; recurrences common

### Part D: HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy)

| Drug Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| NRTIs | Terminate proviral DNA replication | Zidovudine, tenofovir |
| NNRTIs | Bind reverse transcriptase active site | Efavirenz, nevirapine |
| Protease inhibitors | Block viral maturation | Ritonavir (booster), lopinavir |
| Integrase inhibitors | Block viral DNA integration | Raltegravir, dolutegravir |
| Entry/fusion inhibitors | Block viral entry | Enfuvirtide, maraviroc |

- Standard regimen: 2 NRTIs (backbone) + NNRTI or PI
- Up to 95% of patients achieve undetectable viral load
- UK guidelines: Initiate when CD4 <350 cells/uL or with serious HIV-related infection/malignancy

### Part E: Indications for HIV Testing by Otolaryngologists

Testing should be carried out whenever HIV enters the differential diagnosis:
- Unexplained oral candidiasis
- Hairy leukoplakia of the tongue
- Atypical head and neck cancers
- Parotid cysts
- Cervical lymphadenopathy
- Chronic parotitis
- Pyrexia of unknown origin
- Non-paediatric adenoid hypertrophy

## Q(2023-84). Microdebrider in laryngeal surgery. Jet ventilation in airway surgery (2023 Apr)

*Discuss the role of microdebrider in laryngeal surgery. Describe jet ventilation in airway surgery.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapter 67 (Phonosurgery); Volume 2, Chapters 28 (Papilloma), 30 (Congenital Airway)

---

### Introduction

The microdebrider (powered instrumentation) and jet ventilation are two important tools in modern laryngeal and airway surgery. The microdebrider offers an alternative to laser and cold instruments for tissue removal, while jet ventilation provides an anaesthetic technique that optimizes surgical access by eliminating the endotracheal tube from the surgical field.

### Part A: Microdebrider in Laryngeal Surgery

The laryngeal microdebrider is a powered instrument consisting of a rotating blade within a hollow sheath connected to suction, allowing simultaneous tissue cutting and aspiration.

**Indications:**
- [[Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis Larynx|Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis]] (RRP) — considered the gold standard for papilloma removal
- Vocal fold polyps
- Reinke's oedema
- Glottic and subglottic tumour debulking
- Granulation tissue removal
- Supraglottic lesions

**Advantages:**
- Less post-operative pain compared to laser
- Quicker return to usable speaking voice
- No risk of airway fire (unlike CO2 laser)
- No laser plume — eliminates theoretical risk of viral transmission (important in HPV-related papillomatosis)
- Reduced thermal damage to surrounding tissues
- Preserved voice quality — favourable outcomes compared to CO2 laser in some studies

**Disadvantages and Limitations:**
- Loss of histological margins — specimen orientation is lost as tissue is aspirated into collection pot
- Must use suction collection pot to confirm histological diagnosis
- Requires awareness of microanatomy of vocal folds and principles of phonosurgery
- Accurate depth of resection may be more difficult compared to laser
- Cannot make as precise incisions as cold instruments

**Technical Considerations:**
- A suction collection pot must always be used to retrieve tissue for pathological examination
- The surgeon must be aware of the principles of phonosurgery — understanding the microanatomy of the vocal fold layers is essential
- The question of whether accurate depth of resection can be achieved with this tool must be considered for each case

### Part B: Jet Ventilation in Airway Surgery

Jet ventilation is an anaesthetic technique that delivers high-pressure gas directly into the airway without the need for an endotracheal tube, providing optimal laryngeal and tracheal surgical access.

**Types:**

| Type | Route | Placement | Features |
|------|-------|-----------|----------|
| Supraglottic | Above glottis | Jet catheter through laryngoscope | Glottis fully visible; open system |
| Subglottic | Below glottis | Percutaneous or via suspension laryngoscope | Better oxygenation; more stable |
| High-frequency (HFJV) | Various | Specialized ventilator | 100-300 breaths/min; minimal vocal fold movement |
| Combined/tubeless | Both above and below | Dual catheters | Best access; most complex |

**Advantages:**
- Provides best exposure of the larynx — no endotracheal tube obscuring surgical field
- Can be performed for prolonged periods
- Allows uninterrupted surgical access
- Minimal vocal fold movement with HFJV
- Compatible with laser surgery (with appropriate precautions)

**Disadvantages and Risks:**
- **Barotrauma** — the most significant risk; can cause pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema
- No airway protection from aspiration
- Should NOT be used when bleeding is expected — no barrier to aspiration of blood
- Requires experienced anaesthesiologist
- No capnography monitoring possible in open systems
- Risk of gastric distension

**Indications:**
- Transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) of laryngeal tumours
- Microlaryngoscopy for phonosurgery
- Papilloma surgery
- Foreign body retrieval in airway
- Diagnostic endoscopy requiring optimal visualization
- Subglottic surgery
- Skull base procedures requiring stable ventilation

**Contraindications:**
- Expected significant bleeding
- Severe [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] (risk of barotrauma)
- Inadequate laryngeal exposure
- Inexperienced anaesthetic team

**Alternative Anaesthetic Techniques:**
1. Standard oral endotracheal intubation — smallest possible tube; laser-safe tube if laser used
2. Apnoeic technique — intermittent ventilation by mask or tube; used when posterior glottis access needed
3. Jet ventilation — best exposure of larynx

## Q(2023-89). Bilateral lymph node enlargement in neck. Suspected lymphoma management (2023 Apr)

*Discuss the causes of bilateral lymph node enlargement in the neck. Outline the management of suspected lymphoma.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapter 13 (Oropharyngeal Tumours); Volume 1, Chapter 23 (HIV)

---

### Introduction

Bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy is a relatively common presentation in ENT practice. While reactive lymphadenopathy is the most frequent cause, the possibility of lymphoma or other serious pathology must be considered, particularly with persistent, painless, or progressively enlarging nodes.

### Part A: Causes of Bilateral Cervical Lymphadenopathy

**1. Infective/Reactive:**
- Viral upper respiratory tract infections
- Infectious mononucleosis (Epstein-Barr virus)
- HIV infection — persistent generalized lymphadenopathy is a common feature of early symptomatic HIV; nodes usually small, symmetrical, non-tender; biopsy reveals follicular hyperplasia
- Cytomegalovirus
- Tuberculosis — cervical TB lymphadenopathy
- Toxoplasmosis
- Syphilis
- Acute bacterial infections (tonsillitis, pharyngitis)

**2. Neoplastic:**
- Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL) — more common in oropharynx; arises in lymphoid tissue of palatine tonsil or base of tongue; affects younger patients (45-55 years)
- Hodgkin's lymphoma — painless, rubbery nodes; often cervical/supraclavicular
- Metastatic carcinoma — bilateral in NPC, thyroid carcinoma, base of tongue SCC
- Leukaemia

**3. Inflammatory/Autoimmune:**
- Sarcoidosis
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- [[Sjogren Syndrome|Sjogren's syndrome]]
- Castleman disease — rare lymphoproliferative disorder; in HIV, usually multicentric with constitutional symptoms; associated with HHV-8

**4. Drug-related:**
- Phenytoin-induced lymphadenopathy
- Serum sickness

**5. Storage Diseases:**
- Gaucher disease
- Niemann-Pick disease

### Part B: Assessment and Investigation

**History:**
- Duration, rate of growth, associated symptoms
- Systemic B symptoms (fever, night sweats, weight loss >10% in 6 months) — suggests lymphoma
- Alcohol-related pain in nodes (Hodgkin's disease)
- Risk factors: immunosuppression, HIV, EBV, previous radiation, family history

**Examination:**
- Size, consistency (rubbery in lymphoma), tenderness, fixation
- Distribution — Hodgkin's tends to be contiguous spread; NHL may be non-contiguous
- Waldeyer's ring examination — NHL commonly arises from palatine tonsil or base of tongue
- General examination — hepatosplenomegaly, axillary/inguinal nodes
- Full head and neck examination including nasopharynx (NPC)

**Investigations:**

| Investigation | Purpose |
|--------------|---------|
| FBC, ESR, LDH | Baseline; LDH elevated in lymphoma |
| Blood film | Atypical lymphocytes; leukaemic cells |
| Monospot/EBV serology | Infectious mononucleosis |
| HIV test | HIV-associated lymphadenopathy |
| Chest X-ray | Mediastinal lymphadenopathy |
| CT neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis | Staging |
| PET-CT | Gold standard for lymphoma staging |
| FNAC/Core biopsy | Initial tissue diagnosis |
| Excision biopsy | Essential for lymphoma subtyping |

### Part C: Management of Suspected Lymphoma

**1. Tissue Diagnosis:**
- FNAC alone is insufficient for lymphoma diagnosis — cannot provide architectural information
- Core needle biopsy may provide preliminary diagnosis
- **Excision biopsy is the gold standard** — provides complete architectural assessment and allows immunohistochemistry and flow cytometry for accurate subtyping
- Lymphomas arising in the oropharynx are almost exclusively non-Hodgkin lymphomas

**2. Staging:**
- Ann Arbor staging system modified by Cotswolds (Lugano classification)
- Stage I: Single lymph node region
- Stage II: Two or more regions, same side of diaphragm
- Stage III: Both sides of diaphragm
- Stage IV: Diffuse extranodal involvement
- Suffix A (no symptoms) or B (B symptoms present)

**3. Treatment:**
- Lymphomas are diagnosed by biopsy but treated by haemato-oncologists
- Hodgkin's lymphoma: Chemotherapy (ABVD regimen) +/- radiotherapy; excellent cure rates
- NHL: Depends on subtype and grade:
  - Low-grade: Watch and wait, or rituximab-based immunochemotherapy
  - High-grade (e.g. DLBCL): R-CHOP chemotherapy +/- radiotherapy
- Localized extranodal NHL (e.g. tonsillar lymphoma): Combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy

**4. Role of ENT Surgeon:**
- Accurate tissue diagnosis via excision biopsy
- Examination under anaesthesia with biopsies of Waldeyer's ring
- Identification and assessment of head and neck complications
- Management of treatment-related complications (mucositis, xerostomia, airway compromise)

## Q(2024-102). Odontogenic tumours classification. Ameloblastoma mandible management (2024 May)

*Classify odontogenic tumours. Describe the management of ameloblastoma of the mandible.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapter 25: Cysts and Tumours of the Bony Facial Skeleton

---

### Introduction

Odontogenic tumours are a spectrum of lesions unique to the jaws, derived from tooth-forming apparatus. They range from hamartoma-like dental malformations (odontomas) through to benign neoplasms, locally invasive growths, and malignant neoplasms with metastatic potential. The ameloblastoma, although the most common odontogenic neoplasm, only accounts for around 1% of oral biopsies. Diagnosis depends on clinical and radiological features as well as precise histology.

### Part A: Classification of Odontogenic Tumours (Modified WHO Classification)

**Benign Odontogenic Tumours:**

| Category | Tumour Type |
|----------|-------------|
| From odontogenic epithelium only | Ameloblastoma |
| | Squamous odontogenic tumour |
| | Calcifying epithelial odontogenic tumour (CEOT/Pindborg) |
| | Keratocystic odontogenic tumour (KCOT) |
| Including epithelium + ectomesenchyme | Ameloblastic fibroma |
| | Ameloblastic fibro-dentinoma and fibro-odontoma |
| | Complex and compound odontoma |
| | Calcifying cystic odontogenic tumour |
| | Dentinogenic ghost cell tumour |
| From odontogenic ectomesenchyme only | Odontogenic fibroma |
| | Odontogenic myxoma |
| | Cementoblastoma |

**Malignant Odontogenic Tumours:**

| Category | Type |
|----------|------|
| Odontogenic carcinomas | Metastasizing (malignant) ameloblastoma |
| | Ameloblastic carcinoma (primary/secondary) |
| | Primary intraosseous carcinoma |
| | Clear cell odontogenic carcinoma |
| | Ghost cell odontogenic carcinoma |
| Odontogenic sarcomas | Ameloblastic fibrosarcoma |
| | Ameloblastic fibrodentino-/odontosarcoma |

Odontomas account for around one-third of all odontogenic tumours. Of the true neoplasms (excluding KCOT), the ameloblastoma is as common as all other types combined.

### Part B: Classification of Ameloblastoma

| Subtype | Key Features |
|---------|-------------|
| Solid/Multicystic (SMA) | Most common; locally invasive; high recurrence if not adequately removed |
| Unicystic (UA) | 5-15% of all ameloblastomas; luminal, intraluminal, or intramural types |
| Peripheral (PA) | 2-10%; extra-osseous; arises from glands of Serres or basal oral mucosa |
| Desmoplastic (DA) | 4-13%; maxillary lesions equally common; ill-defined radiographic borders |

### Part C: Ameloblastoma of the Mandible — Clinical Features

**Epidemiology:**
- Most common odontogenic neoplasm
- All ages affected (mean 37 years); M:F ratio 1.2:1
- Mandibular: maxillary ratio approximately 5:1
- Most common site: mandibular molar-ramus region

**Clinical Presentation:**
- Few clinical signs in early stages (centrally located within jaw)
- Later: increasing facial deformity, loosening of teeth
- Eggshell crackling on palpation (thinned cortex)
- Bone perforation and spontaneous jaw fracture (advanced)

**Radiology:**
- Multilocular destruction of bone — 'soap-bubble' or 'honeycomb' appearance
- Scalloped margin incorporating tooth roots (typically with root resorption)
- Some occur in dentigerous relationship with unerupted tooth
- Differential diagnosis: keratocystic odontogenic tumour, odontogenic myxoma, giant cell lesion, haemangioma

**Histology:**
- Odontogenic epithelium within fibrous stroma
- Two main patterns: follicular and plexiform (many lesions comprise both)
- Follicular: outer columnar ameloblast-like cells with reverse polarity + inner stellate reticulum-like cells
- Tumour infiltrates within cancellous bone leading to poorly defined margins

### Part D: Management of Mandibular Ameloblastoma

**1. Solid/Multicystic Ameloblastoma (SMA):**
- **En bloc resection is the preferred treatment** — cancellous infiltration may extend 5 mm or more beyond radiological limits
- Resection margin should extend at least 1-1.5 cm beyond radiological margins
- **Conservative approach** (enucleation, curettage, and burring of bone cavity followed by close review) may be considered ONLY for small mandibular lesions
- **All maxillary lesions should be treated radically** — consequences of recurrence involving the skull base are severe with mortality up to 60%
- Reconstruction with free vascularized fibula flap is standard after segmental resection

**2. Unicystic Ameloblastoma (UA):**
- Types I (luminal) and II (intraluminal): Usually treated conservatively by enucleation and curettage
- Type III (intramural): Shows greater tendency to recur — managed as SMA with resection
- Most common site: posterior mandible (maxilla:mandible ratio 1:5)
- Teens and young adults mainly affected

**3. Peripheral Ameloblastoma (PA):**
- Does not invade bone
- Conservative supraperiosteal surgical excision with adequate disease-free margins
- Recurrence is rare after complete excision

**4. Desmoplastic Ameloblastoma (DA):**
- Radical resection favoured
- Recurrence rates resemble those for SMA
- Maxillary lesions equally common as mandibular (unlike other subtypes)

**Follow-up:**
- Long-term clinical and radiological follow-up is essential — recurrence can occur many years after treatment
- Annual panoramic radiographs (OPG) recommended

## Q(2023-82). Tracheo-oesophageal fistula types. Etiology and management (2023 Apr)

*Classify the types of tracheo-oesophageal fistula. Discuss etiology and management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 30: Congenital Disorders of the Larynx, Trachea and Bronchi

---

### Introduction

Tracheo-oesophageal fistula (TOF) is a fairly common congenital malformation of the neonatal air and food passages, which usually occurs in association with oesophageal atresia (OA). It can also be acquired in adults due to malignancy, trauma, or prolonged intubation.

### Part A: Classification of Congenital TOF

| Type | Description | Frequency |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| Type C (Vogt IIIb) | OA with distal TOF | 87% (most common) |
| Type A (Vogt II) | OA without TOF (pure OA) | 6% |
| Type E (Vogt IV) | TOF without OA (H-type fistula) | 4% |
| Type B (Vogt IIIa) | OA with proximal TOF | 2% |
| Type D (Vogt IIIc) | OA with both proximal and distal TOF | 1% |

### Part B: Etiology

**Congenital TOF:**
- Embryological failure of separation of the trachea from the oesophagus during the 4th-5th week of gestation
- The trachea and oesophagus develop from the foregut through formation of the tracheo-oesophageal septum
- Incomplete septation results in persistence of communication (fistula)
- Associated congenital malformations in approximately 50% of infants:
  - VACTERL association (Vertebral, Anorectal, Cardiac, TOF, Esophageal, Renal, Limb anomalies)
  - CHARGE syndrome
  - 10-20% have associated tracheomalacia
  - Other: cardiovascular anomalies, pulmonary artery sling, subglottic stenosis

**Acquired TOF:**
- Malignant — oesophageal or tracheal/bronchial carcinoma invading adjacent structure
- Post-intubation — prolonged mechanical ventilation with cuff pressure necrosis
- Post-surgical — following laryngectomy, oesophageal surgery
- Post-radiation — radiation necrosis
- Trauma — blunt or penetrating neck/chest injury
- Corrosive ingestion

### Part C: Clinical Presentation

**Congenital TOF with OA (Types A-D):**
- Usually diagnosed within hours of birth
- Excessive salivation, drooling, choking
- Inability to swallow feeds — immediate regurgitation
- Respiratory distress — aspiration pneumonia
- Inability to pass nasogastric tube beyond 10 cm
- Abdominal distension (air passes via distal fistula to stomach)

**H-type fistula (Type E):**
- No oesophageal atresia — least symptomatic
- May present late (weeks to months)
- Symptoms of recurrent minor aspiration
- Recurrent chest infections
- Choking episodes during feeds
- Failure to thrive

### Part D: Diagnosis

- **Nasogastric tube test:** Resistance at ~10 cm in OA; confirms on X-ray
- **Plain X-ray:** Air-fluid level in upper oesophageal pouch; gasless abdomen in pure OA; air in stomach in distal fistula types
- **Barium swallow:** May demonstrate H-type fistula (thin contrast may not pass through tiny fistula)
- **Microlaryngoscopy and bronchoscopy (MLB):** Essential for H-type fistula; tracheal opening characterized by V-shaped mucosal fold around it in posterior tracheal wall
- **CT with contrast:** Defines anatomy in complex cases

### Part E: Management

**Pre-operative:**
- Head-up positioning (30-40 degrees) to reduce aspiration
- Continuous suction of upper oesophageal pouch (Replogle tube)
- IV fluids, nil by mouth
- Antibiotics for aspiration pneumonia
- Echocardiography to identify associated cardiac anomalies
- Assessment of VACTERL components

**Surgical Management:**
- **TOF with OA (Types B, C, D):** Primary repair — right thoracotomy (extrapleural approach) with division of fistula, closure of tracheal defect, and primary oesophageal anastomosis
- **Pure OA (Type A):** May require staged repair with delayed anastomosis or interposition grafting if long gap
- **H-type fistula (Type E):** Cervical approach for high fistulae; ligation and division of fistula; MLB to identify tracheal opening pre-operatively

**Post-operative Complications:**
- Anastomotic leak (most common early complication)
- Anastomotic stricture — may require serial dilatation
- Recurrent fistula (5-10%)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux (common, often requiring fundoplication)
- Tracheomalacia (10-20%) — may require aortopexy
- Dysphagia from oesophageal dysmotility
- Respiratory complications — recurrent chest infections

## Q48. Mid-face traumatic injuries classification. Assessment in intubated ICU patient (2019 Jun)

*Classify mid-face traumatic injuries. Describe assessment in an intubated ICU patient.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 107: Nasal and Facial Fractures (Kim & Holmes)

---

### Introduction

Injuries to the face are common and account for 10% of all accident and emergency department attendances. The facial skeleton provides support for muscles of facial expression, holds the eyes for binocular vision, and provides protection for the globes. Modern management focuses on restoration of both vertical and transverse facial dimensions using extended craniofacial bone exposure and semi-rigid internal fixation, together with meticulous soft tissue repair.

### Part A: Classification of Mid-face Fractures

**1. Le Fort Classification (Classical):**

| Level | Fracture Pattern |
|-------|-----------------|
| Le Fort I (Guerin) | Horizontal fracture above floor of nasal cavity, through nasal septum, maxillary sinuses, inferior parts of medial and lateral pterygoid plates. Separates alveolar process from upper face |
| Le Fort II (Pyramidal) | From floor of maxillary sinuses, superiorly to infraorbital margin, through zygomaticomaxillary suture; within orbit across lacrimal bone to nasion. Infraorbital nerve often damaged |
| Le Fort III (Craniofacial dysjunction) | Disconnection of facial skeleton from cranial base. Through medial orbit wall to superior orbital fissure, across greater wing of sphenoid and zygomatic bone to zygomaticofrontal suture. Zygomatic arch also broken |

While the Le Fort classification is accurate for low-energy injuries, in high-energy injuries there are very few instances of pure Le Fort fractures and most configurations involve multiple Le Fort levels, together with zygomatic and nasal/nasoethmoid components.

**2. By Region:**

| Region | Type |
|--------|------|
| Central | Maxillary (Le Fort I-III), nasal, nasoethmoid |
| Lateral | Zygomatic body, zygomatic arch |
| Orbital | Orbital floor (blow-out), orbital wall |
| Combined | Naso-orbito-ethmoid (NOE) complex |
| Upper third | [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|Frontal sinus]] fractures |

**3. Zygomatic Complex Fractures:**
- Five articulations disrupted: frontozygomatic, infraorbital rim, zygomaticomaxillary buttress, zygomatic arch, zygomaticosphenoid
- Originally termed 'tripod' fractures

**4. NOE Fractures (Markowitz Classification):**
- Type I: Single large central fragment bearing canthal ligaments
- Type II: Fragmentation of central fragment, canthal ligaments attached to bone
- Type III: Comminution with no bone attached to canthal ligaments

### Part B: Assessment of Mid-face Injuries in Intubated ICU Patient

**1. Initial Survey (ATLS Protocol):**
The primary survey follows: Airway (secured by intubation), Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure. In an intubated patient, the airway is already secured.

**2. History (from records/witnesses):**
- Mechanism and timing of injury
- Associated injuries (cervical spine, head injury, GCS at scene)
- Exclude cervical spine injury — always suspect and exclude
- Check for [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]]

**3. Systematic Examination:**

**(a) Inspection:**
- Circumorbital ecchymosis ('panda facies') — suggests Le Fort II/III
- Facial oedema and asymmetry
- Surgical emphysema
- Lengthening of the face (maxillary impaction)
- Skin and mucosal lacerations — document proximity to key anatomical landmarks

**(b) Palpation:**
- Orbital margins — step deformity
- Zygomatic projection — reduced prominence
- Nasal skeleton — deviation, crepitus
- Infraorbital nerve — anaesthesia (suggests Le Fort II/infraorbital rim fracture)
- Mandibular outline — step deformity, instability
- Maxillary instability — grasp anterior alveolar ridge and test mobility at Le Fort I, II, III levels
- Frontozygomatic suture — tenderness

**(c) Dental Occlusion:**
- Assess dental alignment if possible
- Anterior open bite — suggests bilateral condylar fractures or Le Fort I-III
- Missing teeth — account for (chest X-ray to exclude aspiration)

**(d) Ophthalmic Assessment (Critical):**
- Visual acuity — essential in ALL middle-third injuries
- Pupil reactions — direct and consensual light reflex
- Eye movements — restricted upward gaze suggests orbital floor fracture
- Enophthalmos/hypoglobus — orbital floor blow-out
- Subconjunctival haemorrhage — almost invariably present in zygomatic body fractures
- **Retrobulbar haemorrhage** — emergency; decreased acuity, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, tense globe, dilated pupil, loss of direct light reflex. Management: dexamethasone 4 mg/kg bolus; acetazolamide 500 mg IV; mannitol 20% 200 mL; lateral canthotomy if needed

**(e) Nasal Assessment:**
- CSF rhinorrhoea — test for beta-2 transferrin
- Septal haematoma — fluctuant swelling, requires urgent drainage

**4. Imaging:**
- CT scan with axial, coronal, and sagittal reformatting — best standard of care
- Include facial skeleton in any CT ordered for head injury
- 3D CT reconstruction helpful for complex panfacial fractures
- Chest X-ray — aspirated teeth, associated thoracic injury

**5. Key Points for ICU Assessment:**
- Bruising at junction of hard and soft palate — suggests maxillary fracture
- Inadvertent nasogastric tube insertion through cribriform plate is a risk — should be borne in mind when passing any tube through the nose in mid-face injuries
- Haemorrhage: Use epistat devices rather than anterior/posterior packing
- Timing of surgery: Trend towards earlier fixation to avoid 'double insult'; swelling reduced by head-up position, ice packs, and single dose of dexamethasone

## Q155. Nasal fractures — classification and outline (2013 Jun)

*Classify nasal fractures. Outline their management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 107: Nasal and Facial Fractures

---

### Introduction

Isolated fractures of the nasal pyramid account for about 40% of all facial fractures. Relatively little force is required to fracture nasal bones (25-75 lb/in2). Young men are twice as likely as women to sustain fractured nose, with peak incidence in the 15-30 year age group.

### Part A: Classification of Nasal Fractures

**1. By Nature of Injury:**
- Lateral impact (>66% of fractures) — less force required
- Frontal impact (13%) — greater force needed; nasal cartilages act as shock absorbers

**2. By Extent of Deformity (5-point grading):**
- Grade 0: Bones perfectly straight
- Grade 1: Deviation less than half the width of nasal bridge
- Grade 2: Deviation half to one full width of nasal bridge
- Grade 3: Deviation greater than one full width
- Grade 4: Bones almost touching the cheek

**3. By Pattern of Fracture (3 Classes):**

| Class | Force | Structures Involved | Deformity |
|-------|-------|---------------------|-----------|
| Class 1 (Chevallet) | Low-moderate | Nasal bones +/- septum | Minimal; depressed fragment, impaction |
| Class 2 (Jarjavay) | Moderate | Nasal bones + frontal process of maxilla + septum (C-shaped fracture) | Significant; true deviation or flattening |
| Class 3 (NOE) | High velocity | All of above + ethmoid labyrinth collapse, medial canthal ligament disruption | Severe; 'pig-like' saddled nose, telecanthus |

**Class 1:** Simplest form — depressed nasal bone; maintained by upper lateral cartilage attachment. Septum generally not involved. Does not cause gross lateral displacement. In children, may be greenstick.

**Class 2:** In addition to nasal bones, the frontal process of maxilla and septum are involved. A complex C-shaped fracture (Jarjavay fracture) extends through quadrangular cartilage, perpendicular plate of ethmoid, and vomer. Rule of thumb: if nasal dorsum deviated >Grade 2, septal fracture must be present.

**Class 3:** Most severe — naso-orbito-ethmoid fractures. External buttresses give way; ethmoid labyrinth collapses; perpendicular plate rotates; quadrilateral cartilage falls back. Classic 'pig-like' appearance with foreshortened saddled nose, anteriorly facing nostrils, telecanthus. Raveh sub-classification: Type I (anterior skull base intact) and Type II (disruption of posterior frontal sinus wall, multiple fractures of orbital and ethmoid roof).

### Part B: Clinical Presentation

**History:**
- How and when injury occurred (medicolegal; timing affects treatment window)
- Nasal obstruction — complete obstruction may indicate septal haematoma
- Change in shape of nose; previous injuries or nasal surgery
- Diplopia, visual disturbance, epiphora — suggest orbital trauma
- Loose teeth, altered bite, trismus — need dental opinion
- Watery rhinorrhoea, anosmia — possible skull base damage

**Examination:**
- External deformity — may be obscured by swelling; re-examine at 4-5 days
- Palpation for step deformity, surgical emphysema
- Document soft tissue lacerations
- Nasal cavity inspection — septal haematoma or deviation
- Full ENT/head and neck examination

### Part C: Investigations

- Nasal X-rays are NOT required to make the diagnosis or aid reduction
- Not cost-effective; only utility is proof of injury for litigation
- CT scan if evidence of more serious facial injury
- Beta-2 transferrin testing if suspected CSF leak

### Part D: Management

**1. Indications for Surgery:**
- Significant cosmetic deformity
- Nasal obstruction caused by septal haematoma (URGENT — within 24 hours)
- Up to 80% of patients do NOT require active treatment

**2. Timing:**
- Optimal clinical assessment at 4-5 days (oedema subsides, deformity apparent)
- Reduction by 7 days (bony abnormality palpable and movable)
- Further delay makes effective reduction less likely; may require osteotomies
- In children, healing is faster — earlier intervention needed

**3. Anaesthesia:**
- Local: Lignocaine injection (nasomaxillary groove, infraorbital nerve, infratrochlear nerve) + topical intranasal (cocaine, lignocaine, adrenaline)
- General: For children, anxious patients, delayed fractures
- No significant difference in outcomes between local and general anaesthesia

**4. Methods of Reduction:**

**(a) Closed Reduction:**
- General principle: mobilize by slightly increasing deformity, then reducing
- Firm digital pressure; instruments (Freer, Hills, Howarth elevators; Ashe, Walsham forceps) if needed
- Insert instrument with index finger along it to gauge depth and avoid orbital damage
- All Class 1 and most Class 2 fractures can be reduced with closed techniques

**(b) Open Reduction (indications):**
- Bilateral fractures with septal dislocation
- Infraction of nasal dorsum
- Fractures of cartilaginous pyramid with dislocation of upper laterals
- K-wire fixation for unstable depressed fragments

**5. Septal Management:**
- Septal fracture present in 46.9% of nasal fractures (often missed)
- 'Where goes the septum, so does the nose'
- Reduction with Ashe's forceps, or septoplasty technique (Killian/hemitransfixion incision, mucosal flap elevation, fragment replacement/removal)

**6. Post-operative:**
- External splint/plaster for ~7 days
- No contact sports for 6 weeks
- Review at 7 days for splint removal

### Part E: Complications

- Poor cosmetic result — due to delay, poor technique, unrecognized septal fracture, pre-existing deformity
- Nasal obstruction — valve collapse, septal deviation, widened septum, tip ptosis
- Epistaxis — usually brief; anterior ethmoidal artery laceration if nasoethmoid involvement
- Septal haematoma — URGENT; drain by aspiration or excision of mucosal window; quilting sutures; if untreated → abscess → cartilage necrosis → saddle deformity → rarely cavernous sinus thrombosis
- [[Septal Perforation Repair|Septal perforation]]
- Septorhinoplasty may be needed 6+ months later for residual deformity

## Q163. Facio-maxillary fractures — classification, assessment, management (2011 Dec)

*Classify facio-maxillary fractures. Describe assessment and management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 107: Nasal and Facial Fractures

---

### Introduction

Maxillofacial trauma is extremely common; 10% of all accident and emergency attendances are related to facial injuries. Common causes include road traffic accidents, physical violence, falls, attempted suicide, and sporting injuries. Successful management involves restoration of pre-morbid facial proportions, dental occlusion, and accurate soft tissue repair. Modern management utilizes semi-rigid internal fixation through extended subperiosteal exposure.

### Part A: Classification of Facio-maxillary Fractures

**1. By Anatomical Region:**

| Region | Fracture Types |
|--------|---------------|
| Lower third (Mandible) | Body, angle, symphysis/parasymphysis, ramus, condylar neck (intra/extra-capsular) |
| Middle third (Maxilla) | Le Fort I, II, III; zygomatic body; zygomatic arch; orbital floor; NOE complex |
| Upper third | Frontal sinus (anterior/posterior table) |
| Nasal | Classes 1-3 |
| Panfacial | Multiple regions |

**2. Le Fort Classification (see Q48 above)**

**3. Mandibular Fracture Sites:**
- Lines of weakness: angles (weakened by wisdom teeth), parasymphysis (long canine root), condylar neck (slender anatomy)
- Common to fracture in more than one place
- Displacement depends on pull of attached muscles

**4. Frontal Sinus Fractures:**
- Anterior table (with/without displacement)
- Posterior table (requires neurosurgical opinion)
- With/without nasofrontal duct involvement

### Part B: Assessment (ATLS Protocol)

**Primary Survey:**
1. **Airway** — Compromise from oral/nasal bleeding, posterior tongue displacement (mandibular fractures), postero-inferior maxillary displacement. May need cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy
2. **Breathing** — Ensure adequate ventilation
3. **Circulation** — Control haemorrhage; epistat devices for nasal bleeding
4. **Disability** — GCS monitoring; neurosurgical referral if consciousness deteriorates
5. **Exposure** — Identify all injuries; CSF rhinorrhoea in high maxillary fractures

**Secondary Survey:**
- Soft tissue examination: lacerations, proximity to anatomical landmarks (eyebrows, vermillion border, nasal aperture, conjunctival margins)
- Facial nerve function documentation
- Parotid duct integrity assessment
- Visual acuity and ocular movements
- Hard tissue palpation: orbital margins, zygomatic projection, nasal skeleton, mandibular outline
- Dental examination: fractured/missing teeth, dental occlusion
- Maxillary or mandibular instability assessment

**Signs and Symptoms by Region:**

*Mandible (body/angle/symphysis):*
- Step deformity (external/intraoral), deranged occlusion
- Pain, paradoxical movement, crepitus
- Floor of mouth/buccal sulcus haematoma, blood-stained saliva
- Lower lip anaesthesia

*Condylar neck:*
- TMJ tenderness, trismus
- Deviation to injured side on opening
- Lateral open bite; anterior open bite (bilateral)

*Maxillary/mid-face:*
- Circumorbital ecchymosis, facial oedema, surgical emphysema
- Lengthening of face, anterior open bite
- Infraorbital anaesthesia
- Palatal bruising

**Imaging:**
- CT with multiplanar reformatting — standard of care
- 3D CT reconstruction for complex/panfacial fractures
- Chest X-ray for aspirated teeth/fragments

### Part C: Management

**1. Emergency Management:**
- Secure airway — reduce displaced fractures immediately if compromising airway; bridal wires to stabilize dentoalveolar segments
- Haemorrhage control — epistat devices rather than packs
- Do not pass nasogastric tube through nose in mid-face fractures (risk of cribriform plate penetration)
- CSF rhinorrhoea → prophylactic antibiotics
- Tetanus prophylaxis in non-immune patients
- Close facial wounds early (within hours); meticulous debridement

**2. Mandibular Fracture Management:**

**(a) Internal Fixation (Modern Standard):**
- Intra-oral approach preferred; mucogingival incision preserving periosteum
- Monocortical 2 mm miniplates for simple fractures (load-sharing)
- Anterior mandible: two plates; posterior: one plate; 6 mm screws
- Extra-oral incision for lower border or condylar approach (2 finger-breadths below mandible to protect facial nerve)
- Comminuted/tissue loss: load-bearing with rigid fixation, bicortical screws

**(b) Condylar Fractures:**
- Most controversial area in maxillofacial trauma
- Open reduction with internal fixation increasingly preferred
- IMF may be used for undisplaced fractures or unilateral condylar fractures
- Bilateral displaced condylar neck and high intracapsular fractures → open reduction
- Endoscope-assisted repair — technically difficult but avoids large scars

**(c) IMF (Intermaxillary Fixation):**
- No longer standard of care for most fractures
- Limited role: undisplaced fractures, unilateral condylar fractures
- Methods: eyelet wires, Leonard button, arch bars, intermaxillary bone pins

**3. Maxillary Fracture Management:**
- Mobilize maxilla with digital pressure and traction on arch bars
- Rowe maxillary disimpaction forceps for impacted maxilla
- 1.3 or 1.5 mm low-profile miniplates along buttresses
- Access via gingivobuccal incision with adequate mucosa cuff
- Restore dental occlusion and facial proportions

**4. Zygomatic Fractures:**
- Minimally displaced: conservative management with 10-day review
- Displaced: reduction by Gillies temporal approach, Poswillo hook, Keen intraoral approach, or open reduction with plating
- Plating at frontozygomatic suture, infraorbital margin, zygomatic buttress, and/or arch

**5. Orbital Floor Fractures:**
- Significant injury requires exploration and repair
- PDS sheets for smaller defects; titanium alloplasts (prefabricated or CAD-CAM) for larger defects
- Trap-door: urgent repair to prevent inferior oblique/orbital fat entrapment
- Post-operative: 12-hour observation for retrobulbar haemorrhage

**6. NOE Fractures:**
- Type I: Stabilize with miniplates via coronal flap
- Types II/III: Miniplates + transnasal canthopexy
- Lacrimal integrity assessed and stented if damaged

**7. Soft Tissue Repair:**
- Accurate identification of key landmarks (vermillion border, eyebrow, alar margin, eyelid)
- Deep sutures (4/0 vicryl) to eliminate dead space
- Skin sutures (5/0-6/0 monofilament), remove after 4-7 days
- Facial nerve repair within 48 hours (epineural repair)
- Avoid sunlight on wounds for 1 year

**8. Paediatric Considerations:**
- Serious maxillofacial injuries uncommon in children
- Same principles as adults
- Use short screws to avoid tooth germs; remove plates at 3-6 months
- Condylar fractures treated conservatively (immense remodelling capacity)

#> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. TORS uses the da Vinci robotic system providing 3D visualization and wristed instruments, while TOUSS uses the harmonic scalpel with ultrasonic cutting at lower temperatures, offering lower cost, better tactile feedback, and reduced thermal damage
> 2. Biomaterials in head and neck surgery include metals (titanium plates), ceramics (hydroxyapatite), polymers (PDS, silicone), and biologics (collagen, hyaluronic acid); tissue engineering combines cells, scaffolds, and growth factors
> 3. HIV infection has three main phases: seroconversion, asymptomatic, and AIDS; oral manifestations include candidiasis, hairy leukoplakia, aphthous ulcers, Kaposi's sarcoma, HPV lesions, gingivitis/periodontitis, and NHL
> 4. The laryngeal microdebrider is considered gold standard for papilloma removal, offering less pain and no laser plume risk, but margins and specimen orientation are lost
> 5. Jet ventilation provides best laryngeal exposure by eliminating endotracheal tube; main risk is barotrauma; contraindicated when bleeding is expected
> 6. Excision biopsy is the gold standard for lymphoma diagnosis — FNAC alone is insufficient as it cannot provide architectural information needed for subtyping
> 7. Ameloblastoma is the most common odontogenic neoplasm; en bloc resection is preferred for solid/multicystic type as cancellous infiltration extends 5+ mm beyond radiological limits; all maxillary ameloblastomas require radical resection
> 8. Congenital TOF most commonly occurs as oesophageal atresia with distal fistula (87%); H-type fistula (4%) presents with recurrent aspiration without swallowing difficulty
> 9. Le Fort classification describes three levels of maxillary fracture; most high-energy injuries involve multiple Le Fort levels and are rarely pure patterns
> 10. Nasal fractures classified into 3 classes by increasing severity (Chevallet, Jarjavay, NOE); optimal assessment at 4-5 days; reduction by 7 days; 'where goes the septum, so does the nose'

#> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Up to 80% of patients referred with nasal fractures do NOT require active treatment — many do not have fractures or have undisplaced fractures
> - Nasal X-rays are not required for diagnosis of nasal fractures and are not cost-effective — diagnosis is clinical; CT only if more serious facial injury suspected
> - In mid-face injuries, NEVER pass nasogastric tube through the nose due to risk of inadvertent intubation through the cribriform plate into anterior cranial fossa
> - Retrobulbar haemorrhage is a treatable emergency — must be sought in every orbital injury; management includes IV dexamethasone, acetazolamide, mannitol, and lateral canthotomy if needed
> - Non-paediatric adenoid hypertrophy should prompt HIV testing — this is a recognized clinical indicator
> - Kaposi's sarcoma regresses with HAART as immune system recovers — antiretroviral therapy is the mainstay treatment, with local treatment only for advanced cases
