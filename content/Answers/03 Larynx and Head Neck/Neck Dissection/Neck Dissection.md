# Neck Dissection — Classification, Indications, Technique and Complications

**Question:** *Neck dissection — principle, classification, indications and complications. Types of neck dissections. Modified radical neck dissection — indications, surgical steps and complications. Selective neck dissection — types and indications. Complications of neck dissection. Chyle leak management.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 18 (Metastatic Neck Disease); Chapter 35 (Surgical Anatomy of the Neck)

---

## Introduction

Metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy is the most important prognostic factor in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Standardized neck dissection terminology was first produced by the American Academy of Otolaryngology and Head and Neck Surgery in 1991, updated by the Committee for Neck Dissection Classification of the American Head and Neck Society in 2002 and is widely used. There is an increasing trend to divide neck dissections into two broad types: comprehensive (removal of levels I–V) and selective (less than five levels). The guiding principle of neck dissection is the systematic removal of lymph node-bearing tissue from defined levels of the neck, tailored to the site and stage of the primary tumour and the status of cervical lymph nodes.

---

## PART A: Neck Node Levels

The Memorial Sloan-Kettering Hospital published, in 1981, a system of levels or regions within the neck which contain groups of lymph nodes representing the first echelon sites for metastases from head and neck primary sites. Currently six neck levels are recognized, with level VII referring to the paratracheal nodes below the suprasternal notch.

| Level | Location | Boundaries |
|---|---|---|
| Ia | Submental triangle | Between anterior bellies of digastric |
| Ib | Submandibular triangle | Body of mandible to digastric |
| IIa | Upper jugular | Skull base to hyoid; medial to SAN |
| IIb | Upper jugular | Skull base to hyoid; lateral to SAN |
| III | Mid jugular | Hyoid to cricoid arch |
| IV | Lower jugular | Cricoid arch to clavicle |
| Va | Posterior triangle | SCM–trapezius above cricoid |
| Vb | Supraclavicular | SCM–trapezius below cricoid |
| VI | Anterior compartment | Hyoid to sternal notch, between CCA |
| VII | Superior mediastinum | Sternal notch to innominate artery |

### Region-Specific Lymphatic Drainage

Knowledge of lymphatic drainage patterns is essential for planning selective neck dissection. The echelon lymph node groups for each primary site are:

- **Nasopharynx:** Retropharyngeal nodes → levels II, III (bilateral drainage common)
- **Oral cavity:** Levels I, II, III (tongue may cause 'skip metastases' to level IV)
- **Oropharynx:** Levels II, III, IV (retropharyngeal nodes also involved)
- **Supraglottic larynx:** Levels II, III (via superior laryngeal pedicle); bilateral drainage common
- **Glottis:** Sparse lymphatic drainage; metastases uncommon from small carcinomas
- **Subglottic/infraglottic:** Levels III, IV, VI (via cricothyroid membrane)
- **Hypopharynx:** Levels III, IV, VI, VII; contralateral spread common near midline
- **Thyroid:** Levels IV, VI, VII

Previous treatment (surgery or [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]]) alters lymphatic drainage patterns and causes unpredictable changes in spread, including contralateral and retrograde metastasis. The echelon level concept does not apply to recurrent disease.

## PART B: Classification of Neck Dissection

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| RND | Removal of levels I–V with SAN, IJV and SCM |
| MRND | Removal of levels I–V; preserves ≥1 of SAN, IJV, SCM |
| SND | Preserves ≥1 lymph node level |
| ERND | RND plus additional structures (e.g. level VII, hypoglossal nerve) |

### Modified Radical Neck Dissection — Subtypes

| MRND Type | Structures Preserved |
|---|---|
| Type I | Spinal accessory nerve preserved |
| Type II | SAN and IJV preserved |
| Type III | SAN, IJV and SCM preserved |

The previous division of SND into named subtypes (supraomohyoid, lateral, posterolateral, anterior) has been superseded by recommendations that the levels or sublevels removed be precisely stated (e.g. SND levels I–III, SND levels II–IV).

### Selective Neck Dissection — Types and Indications

| SND Type | Levels Cleared | Indication |
|---|---|---|
| I–III | Ia, Ib, IIa, IIb, III | Oral cavity SCC |
| I–IV | Ia, Ib, IIa, IIb, III, IV | Oral tongue SCC (skip metastases) |
| II–IV | IIa, IIb, III, IV | Larynx, hypopharynx, oropharynx |
| II–V | IIa, IIb, III, IV, Va, Vb | Cutaneous malignancy (posterolateral) |
| VI–VII | VI, VII | Thyroid carcinoma (central compartment) |

## PART C: Indications for Neck Dissection

### Elective Neck Dissection (END)

END is performed on the clinically and radiologically negative neck (cN0). The general consensus is to treat the neck electively when the risk of occult spread exceeds 15–20%. This figure is derived from a decision analysis model showing that at a threshold occult metastatic rate of 20%, the morbidity of treatment is offset by its benefit.

**Key evidence:** The D'Cruz et al trial (2015, NEJM) randomized 500 patients with early oral cavity SCC (T1–T2 N0) to END versus observation with therapeutic neck dissection. The primary outcome of 3-year overall survival was 80% for the END group and 68% for the observation group (p = 0.01). The rate of occult nodal disease in the END group was 30%. Based on this evidence, NICE guidelines recommend offering END or sentinel node biopsy for T1–T2 N0 oral cavity SCC.

**Indications for elective neck treatment:**
- Greater than 15–20% chance of subclinical neck disease
- Vigilant follow-up not possible
- Clinical evaluation of the neck is difficult
- Access to the neck required for reconstruction

### Therapeutic Neck Dissection

- **N1 disease:** Single-modality treatment gives good control rates; MRND is as good as RND in regional control; SND may be sufficient for pathologically confirmed N1 disease
- **N2a/N2b disease:** Comprehensive neck dissection required; MRND sparing the SAN gives equivalent control to RND; post-operative adjuvant radiotherapy or chemoradiotherapy indicated
- **N2c (bilateral) disease:** Conservation neck dissection may be possible on the less involved side; post-operative radiotherapy usually administered
- **N3 disease (>6 cm):** Long-term control rates are poor; combined modality therapy leads to better control; many patients best managed with palliation

### Post-Chemoradiation Neck

The PET-NECK trial (Mehanna et al, 2016) demonstrated that [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]]-guided surveillance at 12 weeks after chemoradiation showed equivalent 2-year overall survival (85%) to planned neck dissection (82%), with fewer neck dissections (54 vs 221). Complete responders on PET-CT can be safely observed. Partial responders or those with positive PET-CT uptake should be offered neck dissection.

## PART D: Surgical Steps of Modified Radical Neck Dissection

### Pre-operative Preparation

- Cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI) to define extent of disease
- Assessment for ENE, carotid invasion, contralateral disease
- Consent for potential sacrifice of SAN, IJV, SCM if intraoperatively indicated
- Preparation for [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] if combined with primary site surgery

### Operative Steps

1. **Position:** Patient supine, neck extended, head turned to opposite side
2. **Incision:** Modified Schobinger or McFee (parallel horizontal) incision; should incorporate any previous biopsy scars
3. **Subplatysmal flaps:** Raised superiorly to mandible and inferiorly to clavicle
4. **Level Ib dissection:** Submandibular gland identified; marginal mandibular nerve identified and protected by raising it with the superior flap or identifying it at the facial vessels; submandibular gland mobilized
5. **Level Ia dissection:** Submental contents cleared between anterior bellies of digastric
6. **Identification of SAN:** The spinal accessory nerve is identified as it enters SCM (Erb's point) and traced through its course in level IIb; the nerve is carefully preserved and freed from surrounding tissue
7. **Level II–III dissection:** IJV exposed; tissue dissected from anterior to posterior, staying lateral to the carotid sheath; SAN preserved; fibro-fatty tissue cleared from level II to level III
8. **Level IV dissection:** Continued inferiorly to the clavicle; care taken at the junction of the IJV and subclavian vein (thoracic duct territory)
9. **Level V dissection:** Tissue cleared from posterior border of SCM to anterior border of trapezius; SAN identified and preserved as it crosses the posterior triangle
10. **Haemostasis and closure:** Drain placement; wound closure in layers

### Key Surgical Landmarks

- **Marginal mandibular nerve:** May run as low as the greater cornu of the hyoid; incisions should be placed below this level
- **Spinal accessory nerve:** Runs in the roof of the posterior triangle; all important structures lie caudal to the nerve
- **Thoracic duct:** Encountered at the root of the neck, either as a main trunk on the left or as a network of smaller channels; care must be taken to avoid chylous leak during dissection of level IV

## PART E: Complications of Neck Dissection

### Intra-operative Complications

| Complication | Comment |
|---|---|
| Haemorrhage | IJV, carotid, subclavian vessel injury |
| Air embolism | Entry of air via open IJV |
| Pneumothorax | Injury to cupola of lung at root of neck |
| Nerve injury | SAN (shoulder syndrome), marginal mandibular, phrenic, vagus, hypoglossal, sympathetic chain (Horner's) |
| Thoracic duct injury | Chyle leak (see Part F) |

### Post-operative Complications

- **Wound infection and flap necrosis** — especially in previously irradiated necks
- **Haematoma and seroma**
- **Chyle leak** (incidence: 2–8% for neck dissection, 0.5–1.4% for thyroidectomy)
- **Shoulder dysfunction** — greatest after RND (SAN sacrifice); also occurs after MRND due to devascularization and traction injury to the SAN; least problems with SND
- **Cerebral oedema** — if bilateral IJV ligation
- **Facial and cerebral venous congestion** — bilateral neck dissection
- **Carotid blow-out** — rare but devastating; associated with wound breakdown in irradiated necks
- **Hypothyroidism** — if combined with radiotherapy to the neck

### Shoulder Dysfunction (Accessory Nerve)

Shoulder problems are greater in patients undergoing RND compared to MRND. Despite preserving the accessory nerve in MRND, dysfunction occurs due to the nerve being devascularized and stretched. The least problems are seen in patients undergoing SND. Even SND clearing levels II–IV can affect the shoulder by stretching the accessory nerve during dissection of the submuscular recess (level IIb). The small cervical plexus branch that joins the accessory nerve lateral to the posterior border of SCM should be preserved if possible.

## PART F: Chyle Leak — Aetiology and Management

### Anatomy of the Thoracic Duct

The lacteals of the villi coalesce to form the cisterna chyli, which traverses the diaphragm to run superiorly as the thoracic duct in the posterior thoracic cavity behind the oesophagus. As the thoracic duct enters the neck, it crosses between the internal jugular vein and the anterior scalene muscle. It either terminates at the junction of the left IJV and left subclavian vein or drains directly into the IJV. For this reason, the majority of chyle leaks are on the left, although approximately 5% occur on the right from the right lymphatic duct.

### Chyle Physiology

Chyle is a by-product of the absorption and digestion of fat. Fat digestion begins in the duodenum with pancreatic lipase, which hydrolyses triglycerides into monoglycerides and free fatty acids. These interact with bile salts to form micelles, which transport the fat across the brush border of the villi. Fat absorbed into epithelial cells combines with cholesterol, phospholipids and lipoproteins to form chylomicrons, which enter the lacteals as milky-looking lymphatic fluid (chyle). About 2–4 litres of chyle flows through the thoracic duct daily; flow rates vary with dietary fat consumed.

### Classification and Diagnosis

| Feature | Low Output | High Output |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | < 500 ml/24 hours | > 500 ml/24 hours |
| Management | Conservative | May require intervention |

**Diagnosis:** A triglyceride level > 110 mg/dl in the drain fluid is diagnostic. A level < 50 mg/dl usually rules out chyle leak. Between 50–110 mg/dl, further analysis for chylomicrons is required.

### Management

**1. Nutritional management (mainstay):**
- Based on reducing chyle formation to allow the fistula to heal
- Long-chain triglycerides (LCTs) enter the bloodstream via chyle; medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) are absorbed directly into the portal system
- Fat-free or high-MCT diet is recommended — MCT is directly absorbed into the portal system, resulting in less chyle production
- Many patients with low-output leaks respond to this dietary intervention alone, making early dietetic input essential

**2. Parenteral nutrition:**
- If dietary manipulation is unsuccessful
- Should not be used as first-line management except in extreme cases (very high volume leaks > 1000 ml)

**3. Pharmacological:**
- Octreotide — reduces lymphatic flow
- Orlistat — reduces fat absorption

**4. Surgical intervention:**
- Required for high-output leaks that fail to respond to conservative management
- Re-exploration of the neck with identification and ligation of the thoracic duct
- Tissue sealants and muscle flap reinforcement may be used

## PART G: Extranodal Extension (ENE)

ENE occurs when cancer penetrates and infiltrates beyond the lymph node capsule. It is associated with approximately 50% reduction in survival compared to patients whose tumour was confined to the nodes. The 8th edition TNM staging recognizes clinical ENE (N3b) for p16 negative SCC, diagnosed on clinical examination (skin involvement, soft tissue invasion, nerve dysfunction).

**Clinical significance:**
- ENE decreases survival by approximately half
- Patients with occult nodes but ENE do statistically worse
- ENE increases the risk of local recurrence and distant metastases
- Level 1 evidence supports concomitant cisplatin with post-operative radiotherapy in the presence of ENE

**Indications for post-operative adjuvant treatment:**
- Extracapsular spread (strongest indication for chemoradiation)
- Two or more positive nodes
- N2–N3 disease stage
- Residual disease

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy is the single most important prognostic factor in HNSCC — it indicates an aggressive tumour where the tumour–host balance has swung in favour of the tumour
> 2. The neck is divided into levels I–VII — echelon nodes for oral cavity are I–III, for larynx/hypopharynx are II–IV, and for thyroid are IV, VI, VII
> 3. There are four types of neck dissection — RND (levels I–V + SAN, IJV, SCM), MRND (levels I–V preserving ≥1 structure), SND (preserving ≥1 level), and ERND (RND + additional structures)
> 4. MRND is as good as RND in regional control — there is no evidence that RND achieves better survival than MRND for palpable neck disease
> 5. END should be performed when the risk of occult nodal disease exceeds 15–20% — the D'Cruz trial (2015) demonstrated significant survival benefit for END in T1–T2 N0 oral cavity SCC
> 6. SND is adequate for the management of the N0 neck — levels I–III for oral cavity, levels II–IV for larynx/hypopharynx/oropharynx
> 7. ENE is the strongest adverse prognostic feature — it halves survival and is an indication for adjuvant chemoradiation (level 1 evidence from EORTC and RTOG trials)
> 8. The PET-NECK trial (2016) demonstrated that PET-CT surveillance at 12 weeks post-chemoradiation is equivalent to planned neck dissection — complete responders can be safely observed
> 9. Chyle leak occurs in 2–8% of neck dissections — the majority are on the left side due to the termination of the thoracic duct at the left IJV–subclavian junction
> 10. Management of chyle leak is primarily nutritional — fat-free or high-MCT diet reduces chyle production; low-output leaks usually respond to dietary manipulation alone


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Previous treatment (surgery or radiotherapy) alters lymphatic drainage patterns — the echelon level concept does not apply to recurrent disease, and comprehensive neck dissection is recommended in the salvage setting
> - The tongue is known to cause 'skip metastases' to level IV — therefore SND for oral tongue cancer should include level IV (I–IV) rather than just I–III
> - Cystic neck metastases are usually of oropharyngeal origin and are strongly associated with HPV — any cystic level II mass in an adult should be investigated as a potential metastasis rather than assumed to be a branchial cyst
> - The marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve can run as low as the greater cornu of the hyoid — skin incisions should be placed below this level to avoid injury
> - SND clearing levels II–IV can still cause shoulder dysfunction by stretching the accessory nerve during dissection of the submuscular recess (level IIb) — the cervical plexus branch joining the SAN should be preserved
