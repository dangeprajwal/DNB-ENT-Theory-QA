# Unknown Primary with Malignant Cervical Lymphadenopathy

> [!question] Questions
> Neck node levels. Unknown primary with malignant cervical lymphadenopathy — evaluation and management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 17 (Neck Metastases from an Unknown Primary); Chapter 18 (Metastatic Neck Disease)

---

## Introduction

The term carcinoma of unknown primary (CUP) represents a heterogeneous disease entity characterized by the presence of clinically overt metastatic disease in the absence of a clinically or radiologically obvious primary tumour. The incidence of CUP in HNSCC has reduced significantly over the last two decades — from 10–30% in the 1970s prior to universal cross-sectional imaging, to around 4–5% currently, due to standardized diagnostic protocols and improved imaging. It is increasingly recognized that high-risk HPV-related HNSCC tends to present with regional disease and a clinically unrecognized primary focus, and most patients with CUP presentation will have a primary site in the oropharynx.

---

## PART A: Neck Node Levels

The Memorial Sloan-Kettering system divides the neck into six levels (plus level VII) based on lymph node groups representing first echelon drainage sites.

| Level | Location | Key Boundaries |
|---|---|---|
| Ia | Submental triangle | Between anterior bellies of digastric |
| Ib | Submandibular triangle | Mandible to posterior belly of digastric |
| IIa | Upper jugular (ant.) | Skull base to hyoid, medial to SAN |
| IIb | Upper jugular (post.) | Skull base to hyoid, lateral to SAN |
| III | Mid jugular | Hyoid to inferior border of cricoid |
| IV | Lower jugular | Cricoid to clavicle |
| Va | Posterior triangle | SCM–trapezius, above cricoid |
| Vb | Supraclavicular | SCM–trapezius, below cricoid |
| VI | Anterior compartment | Hyoid to sternal notch |
| VII | Superior mediastinum | Sternal notch to innominate artery |

### Predicting the Primary Site from Node Level

The distribution of cervical metastases can predict the location of the primary tumour. In a landmark study of 1155 patients (Lindberg, 1972), distinct patterns of spread to the neck were identified based on the primary site. Key associations include:

- **Level I nodes:** Oral cavity, lip, anterior floor of mouth
- **Level II nodes (most common site for CUP):** Oropharynx, oral cavity, nasopharynx, supraglottic larynx
- **Level III–IV nodes:** Larynx, hypopharynx, oropharynx
- **Level V nodes:** Cutaneous malignancy, nasopharynx (never laryngeal cancer)
- **Level VI–VII nodes:** Thyroid, subglottic larynx, cervical oesophagus
- **Retropharyngeal nodes:** Nasopharynx, oropharynx, paranasal sinuses

Involvement of nodal levels I to IV is almost exclusively associated with occult upper aerodigestive tract primary SCC, with level II being the most common site. Level I nodes are almost never seen with nasopharyngeal primaries.

## PART B: Hypotheses and Incidence of CUP

### Hypotheses

Two hypotheses currently predominate:

1. **Occult mucosal primary:** A microscopic primary tumour lies undetected in the mucosal folds of Waldeyer's ring, too small to detect by conventional diagnostic methods, ultimately treated either by design or coincidentally by inclusion in radiation fields
2. **Immune-mediated regression:** The primary tumour is removed by the patient's innate or adaptive immune system, but not before early metastasis to cervical lymph nodes has occurred

### Incidence

The incidence of CUP has progressively declined with improved diagnostic technology. Current rates are approximately 4–5% of all HNSCC cases. The reduction is attributable to:
- Universal availability of cross-sectional imaging (CT and MRI)
- FDG [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]] scanning
- Standardized diagnostic protocols including bilateral [[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]] and tongue base mucosectomy
- Molecular testing (HPV, EBV) guiding site determination

## PART C: Evaluation and Diagnostic Workup

### Clinical Assessment

A thorough history is essential. Excessive alcohol and smoking may suggest a primary outside the nasopharynx, while multiple sexual partners and orogenital contact may suggest an oropharyngeal primary. Clinical examination should include fibre-optic nasolaryngoscopy with special attention to the nasopharynx, tongue base, infrahyoid epiglottis and pyriform sinus.

### Cytopathology

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) confirms the presence of metastatic carcinoma. The aspirate can be subjected to testing for HPV and EBV status — demonstration of HPV safely allows the primary site to be determined as oropharynx, and EBV indicates nasopharyngeal origin. The possibility of anaplastic carcinoma or lymphoma usually makes core biopsy or open biopsy mandatory.

### Imaging

| Modality | Role in CUP |
|---|---|
| CT/MRI | Cross-sectional imaging to identify primary site; defines extent of nodal disease |
| PET-CT | Key modality — primary detection rate 37%; sensitivity and specificity both 84% |
| NBI endoscopy | High diagnostic accuracy for identifying mucosal primary; sensitivity 74%, specificity 94% |

PET-CT should be performed before panendoscopy and biopsies — the post-biopsy inflammatory response may increase FDG uptake, causing false positive results.

### Surgical Diagnostic Assessment

**Panendoscopy and directed biopsies:**
- Guided biopsies of nasopharynx (using rigid telescopes), tongue base, and any suspicious mucosal lesions
- Blind biopsies of the nasopharynx and tongue base have proven unsatisfactory

**Bilateral tonsillectomy:**
- The contralateral tonsil harbours the primary in up to 10% of cases
- HPV-related tumours can be multifocal at presentation, providing further basis for bilateral tonsillectomy
- Previous tonsillectomy remnants should also be excised

**Tongue base mucosectomy (TBM):**
- Using transoral robotic surgery (TORS) — samples the entire tongue base mucosa
- Identifies a primary in over 50% of patients who are PET-negative and have no primary in the tonsil
- Around 10% of identified primaries are contralateral foci

### Recommended Algorithm (NICE Guidelines)

1. FNAC with ultrasound guidance → confirm SCC, assess HPV/EBV status
2. Conventional imaging (CT/MRI) → search for primary site
3. FDG PET-CT → first investigation to detect primary site (before biopsies)
4. NBI endoscopy → if PET-CT fails to identify primary
5. Panendoscopy + bilateral tonsillectomy + tongue base mucosectomy → if no primary site identified on imaging

## PART D: Clinical Management

### Treatment by Stage

| Stage | Surgery | [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]] | Chemotherapy |
|---|---|---|---|
| T0N1 (no ENE) | SND or MRND | No (unless for mucosa) | No |
| T0N1 (ENE+) | SND or MRND | Yes to neck | Consider |
| T0N2 | SND or MRND | Yes — ipsilateral (consider contralateral) | Consider |
| T0N3 | MRND or RND | Yes — ipsilateral (consider contralateral) | Consider |

### Surgical Management of the Neck

Definitive [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] should be performed in all patients with CUP. Selective neck dissection is a valid option for N2a and N2b disease, as the risk of metastases in levels I and V is rare unless N3 disease is present. Primary surgical management of the neck is preferred to radiotherapy in patients with operable disease, as it allows adequate pathological staging and tailoring of treatment.

If FNAC and core biopsy are non-diagnostic but the index of clinical suspicion remains high, open biopsy should be performed as an oncological procedure — namely, as a selective neck dissection.

### Radiation Therapy

The extent of radiation fields remains controversial:

- **Ipsilateral radiation:** To the neck and ipsilateral likely primary sites — lower morbidity
- **Total mucosal irradiation (bilateral):** Improved local control rates and disease-free survival, but at the expense of increased pain, xerostomia and long-term dysphagia with increased feeding tube dependence
- **IMRT:** Standard of care; allows parotid-sparing techniques, significantly reducing morbidity; for total mucosal irradiation, a dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions is sufficient for control of occult primary disease

### Management of the Violated Neck

Previous open biopsy does not signify a poorer prognosis provided adequate and timely treatment is given. Any incision should be placed to facilitate removal of the scar via a subsequent standard neck dissection incision.

## PART E: Treatment Outcomes and Prognosis

- **N1 disease:** 5-year survival of 70–100%
- **N2–N3 disease:** 5-year survival of 30–60%
- **Overall 5-year survival for all stages:** 52–75%

Most series show improved overall and disease-free survival with combined modality treatment. The pattern of failure largely depends on the initial treatment protocol — recurrence usually presents in the neck or as distant metastases (most commonly lung). The emergence of a primary tumour often occurs within the first 24 months, usually in the oral cavity, oropharynx or nasopharynx.

### Branchial Cyst Carcinoma

The evidence for the existence of branchiogenic carcinoma is tenuous. The majority are in fact cystic metastases from HPV-related oropharyngeal carcinoma. All patients over the age of 35 with lateral cystic neck masses must be presumed to have cancer until proven otherwise and should be entered into a CUP investigation protocol.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. CUP accounts for only 4–5% of patients with head and neck malignancy — the incidence has reduced significantly due to improved diagnostic protocols and imaging
> 2. Level II is the most common site for CUP nodal metastases — involvement of levels I–IV is almost exclusively associated with upper aerodigestive tract SCC
> 3. HPV status on FNAC/core biopsy safely localizes the primary to the oropharynx; EBV indicates nasopharyngeal origin — molecular analysis is a key tool in CUP evaluation
> 4. PET-CT is a key modality and should precede biopsies — it has a primary detection rate of 37% with sensitivity and specificity both 84%; post-biopsy inflammation causes false positives
> 5. Bilateral tonsillectomy should be performed as the primary resides in the contralateral tonsil in up to 10% — HPV-related tumours can be multifocal
> 6. Tongue base mucosectomy using TORS identifies a primary in over 50% of PET-negative patients without a tonsillar primary — around 10% are contralateral foci
> 7. Definitive neck dissection should be performed in all patients with CUP — SND is valid for N2a and N2b disease
> 8. Total mucosal irradiation improves local control but not always overall survival — associated mucositis and xerostomia rates are high; IMRT reduces morbidity
> 9. All patients over 35 with lateral cystic neck masses must be presumed to have cancer until proven otherwise — most represent cystic metastases from HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma
> 10. Nodal stage is the most important risk factor for local recurrence — 5-year survival ranges from 70–100% for N1 to 30–60% for N3


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The NICE algorithm for CUP workup is: FNAC → CT/MRI → PET-CT (before biopsies) → NBI endoscopy → panendoscopy with bilateral tonsillectomy and tongue base mucosectomy
> - Blind biopsies of the nasopharynx and tongue base are unsatisfactory — nasopharyngeal biopsies should be guided using rigid telescopes and tongue base should be sampled using transoral robotic mucosectomy
> - Open biopsy does not worsen prognosis provided it is followed by adequate treatment within 6 weeks — any incision should be placed to facilitate incorporation into a subsequent neck dissection incision
> - The previous concept of 'branchiogenic carcinoma' arising in a branchial cleft cyst should be viewed with scepticism — these almost always represent cystic metastases from HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer
> - Narrow band imaging (NBI) endoscopy is a useful adjunct that identifies neoplastic tissue through highlighting neoangiogenesis — it can be performed in the clinic or during general anaesthetic assessment
