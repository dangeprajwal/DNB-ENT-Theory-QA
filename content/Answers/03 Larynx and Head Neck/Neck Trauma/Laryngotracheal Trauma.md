# Laryngotracheal Trauma — Classification and Management

> [!question] Questions
> Laryngotracheal trauma — classification and management algorithm. Grade 4 laryngotracheal trauma — management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 71 (Contemporary Management of Laryngotracheal Trauma)

---

## Introduction

The laryngotracheal region provides the essential functions of airway protection, swallowing and phonation. Injury to structures in this region is diverse and rare — the reported incidence varies from 1 in 5000 to 1 in 137 000 emergency department presentations. More than 2000 deaths are attributed annually to neck trauma in the UK. Despite females being anatomically more susceptible (longer, more slender necks), approximately 80% of traumatic laryngotracheal injuries occur in males, particularly in the second and third decades, owing to greater participation in violent activities. The aims of treatment are to maintain a safe airway and restore upper aerodigestive tract function in terms of both a safe swallow and voice.

---

## PART A: Anatomy and Mechanisms of Injury

### Anatomical Protection

The larynx is a midline neck structure protected anteriorly by the mandible and strap muscles, inferiorly by the sternum, posteriorly by the cervical spine, and laterally by the sternocleidomastoid muscles. The rarity of laryngeal injuries is attributed to this anatomical configuration. The subglottic region, being the only complete cartilaginous ring, is the most sensitive and vulnerable even during trivial trauma.

### Mechanisms of Injury

| Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|
| Blunt — crush | MVA with hyperextended neck; anterior crushing forces |
| Blunt — clothesline | High velocity impact with stationary object; massive energy over small area; can cause cricotracheal separation |
| Blunt — strangulation | Hanging or soft ligature; initially minor but can progress to oedema and airway obstruction |
| Penetrating — GSW | Low velocity (handgun): moderate damage; high velocity (rifle): wide-field damage beyond visible necrosis |
| Penetrating — stab | Predictable course; damage limited to entry/exit path |
| Inhalational | Toxic gas/fire exposure; reflex glottic closure limits injury to supraglottis; delayed oedema possible |
| Iatrogenic | Intubation, cricothyroidotomy, [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]], laryngoscopy; prolonged intubation causes [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] |

### Risk Factors for Post-Intubation Injury

- High cuff pressure
- Prolonged duration of intubation
- Large diameter endotracheal tubes
- Patient comorbidities (diabetes, COPD)

## PART B: Classification of Laryngotracheal Trauma

### Trone/Fuhrman Classification (Groups 1–5)

| Group | Description | Management |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Minor endolaryngeal haematoma; no detectable fracture | Non-operative |
| 2 | Oedema, haematoma, minor mucosal disruption without exposed cartilage; non-displaced fractures on CT | Non-operative |
| 3 | Massive oedema, mucosal tears, exposed cartilage, vocal fold immobility | Operative (open) |
| 4 | As Group 3 plus >2 fracture lines or massive mucosal trauma | Operative (open) |
| 5 | Complete laryngotracheal separation (added by Fuhrman) | Emergent operative |

Group 5 (complete cricotracheal separation) was added by Fuhrman et al. to the original Trone classification which included only Groups 1–4.

### Bogdasarian and Olson Classification — Posterior Glottic Stenosis

This classification addresses chronic sequelae of laryngeal trauma affecting the posterior glottis:

- **Type I:** Isolated interarytenoid band
- **Type II:** Posterior glottic mucosal tunnel, but no arytenoid cartilage ankylosis
- **Type III:** Ankylosis and immobility of one arytenoid joint
- **Type IV:** Ankylosis and immobility of both arytenoid joints

## PART C: Evaluation of Laryngotracheal Trauma

### Clinical Presentation

The most common presenting symptoms are (in order): dysphonia, dyspnoea, dysphagia, neck pain and haemoptysis. Hallmark signs of airway obstruction include stridor, tachypnoea, tachycardia, sweating and use of accessory muscles.

Additional signs in penetrating injury: visible neck wounds, bleeding, expanding haematoma, bruising, hypovolaemic shock. Blunt injuries with no external signs may remain undetected for 24–48 hours.

**Key principle:** Patients initially exhibiting only subtle symptoms can progress to complete airway obstruction from worsening oedema.

### Airway Management

Securing the airway is the absolute priority. Options include:

1. **Tracheostomy under local anaesthetic** — recommended as gold standard by most authors; allows examination of the injury site; avoids aggravation of existing injury
2. **Endotracheal intubation** — adequate evidence supports safety in experienced hands; some controversy as it may hinder examination and potentially worsen the injury
3. **Cricothyroidotomy** — reserved for emergencies when intubation fails; use vertical incision to avoid anterior jugular veins

**Critical:** Blind endotracheal intubation must be avoided — it can cause complete laryngotracheal separation in a partially transected airway held together only by peri-tracheal soft tissue. Muscle relaxants are contraindicated as paralysis of spontaneous respiration and positive pressure ventilation can aggravate air leak and cause respiratory arrest.

### Imaging

CT with 3D reconstruction and MRI are gold standard radiological modalities. CT findings to assess:
- Cartilage fractures
- Haematoma and oedema
- Cricoarytenoid joint dislocation or subluxation
- Extra-tracheal subcutaneous air
- Airway stenosis

CT angiography is essential in penetrating trauma, especially in Zones I and III, for trajectory of foreign bodies and vascular integrity.

## PART E: Non-Operative Management (Groups 1 and 2)

Groups 1 and 2 injuries (minor haematoma, undisplaced fractures, minor mucosal disruption without exposed cartilage) are managed non-operatively:

- Admission to ICU for airway monitoring for at least 24 hours
- Constant humidification
- Head of bed elevation
- Nil by mouth and voice rest
- Nasogastric feeding
- Antireflux therapy (to prevent mucosal irritation and stenosis)
- Corticosteroids — effectiveness unclear; must be given within hours of injury if used
- Antibiotics — controversial; IV penicillin or cephalosporin may reduce granulation tissue

Endotracheal intubation may be necessary if concern about ability to maintain airway. Panendoscopy (microlaryngoscopy, oesophagoscopy, bronchoscopy) should be performed to exclude other upper aerodigestive tract injuries.

## PART F: Operative Management (Groups 3, 4 and 5)

### Indications for Surgery

- Groups 3 and 4: displaced or comminuted laryngeal fractures, massive mucosal disruption, exposed cartilage, vocal fold immobility
- Group 5: complete cricotracheal separation — requires emergent exploration
- A subset of Group 2 with minimally or non-displaced fractures may also need open repair
- Concomitant neurovascular, thoracic or abdominal injuries

### Timing

Consensus recommendation is exploration within 24 hours of injury. Some authors advocate delay until oedema subsides, but early intervention is preferred to avoid long-term voice and airway dysfunction.

### Endoscopic Approach

- Haematoma aspiration from vocal folds
- Suturing of damaged folds
- Stent placement to support framework and prevent adhesions
- Chronic sequelae: laser division of webs, cordotomy, cordectomy, arytenoidectomy
- Silastic keel placement (1–2 weeks) combined with mucosal suturing for post-traumatic webs
- Injection [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|thyroplasty]] with hyaluronic acid for vocal fold injury

### Open Approach

**Surgical technique:**
1. Position supine with shoulder roll for cervical extension
2. Horizontal collar incision at cricothyroid membrane level (or through traumatic wound)
3. Superior and inferior subplatysmal flaps raised
4. Division of strap muscles and thyroid isthmus
5. Laryngofissure (midline thyrotomy) to expose endolarynx — avoids RLN injury
6. Debridement of contaminated tissue and removal of foreign material
7. Mucosal laceration repair; denuded cartilage covered with mucosal flaps or skin grafts
8. Vocal fold repositioning and suturing; arytenopexy if needed
9. Fracture reduction and stabilization — perichondrial suturing, wire fixation or metal/biodegradable miniplates
10. Covering tracheostomy 2–3 rings below the repair

### Management of Group 4 Injuries

Group 4 injuries involve massive mucosal trauma with more than two fracture lines. These require open exploration with:
- All laryngeal fractures identified, reduced and stabilized using miniplates or wires
- Extensive mucosal repair — avulsion injuries at anterior commissure repaired by suturing vocal folds to outer thyroid perichondrium
- Cartilage loss: local muscle flaps (strap muscles, SCM) or free rib cartilage grafts
- Stents placed when there is disruption of anterior commissure anatomy, extensive mucosal trauma or comminuted fractures
- Neural repair rarely performed acutely (unless iatrogenic) — cable graft options include ansa cervicalis, great auricular nerve or sural nerve

### Cricotracheal Resection for Chronic Stenosis

For chronic subglottic stenosis following trauma:
- Simple circumferential resection with primary anastomosis provides excellent outcomes in selected patients
- Stenosis at distance > 1.5 cm from inferior aspect of vocal folds is considered safe for resection
- Maximum resection length: 4–6 cm with laryngeal and pulmonary release procedures
- Patients must be optimized pre-operatively (comorbidities, immunosuppression)

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Laryngotracheal trauma is rare (1 in 5000 to 1 in 137 000 ED presentations) — the larynx is protected by the mandible, sternum, spine and SCM muscles
> 2. The Trone/Fuhrman classification divides injuries into 5 groups — Group 5 (complete laryngotracheal separation) was added by Fuhrman to the original 4-group Trone system
> 3. Groups 1–2 are managed non-operatively with ICU admission, humidification, voice rest, head elevation, NG feeding and antireflux therapy — airway monitoring for at least 24 hours is mandatory
> 4. Groups 3–4 require open surgical exploration within 24 hours — Group 5 (cricotracheal separation) requires emergent tracheostomy and immediate repair
> 5. Blind endotracheal intubation must be avoided — it can cause complete laryngotracheal separation in a partially transected airway; tracheostomy under local anaesthetic is the gold standard
> 6. Muscle relaxants are contraindicated in laryngotracheal trauma — paralysis of spontaneous respiration and positive pressure ventilation can aggravate air leak and cause respiratory arrest
> 7. CT with 3D reconstruction is the gold standard imaging — findings include cartilage fractures, haematoma, cricoarytenoid dislocation, subcutaneous air and airway stenosis
> 8. Laryngeal fractures should be stabilized with miniplates or wires — biodegradable plates (poly-L-lactic-acid-polyglycolic-acid) resorb over 1.5–3 years
> 9. Stents are indicated for disruption of the anterior commissure, extensive mucosal trauma and comminuted fractures — they maintain framework structure and prevent adhesion formation
> 10. For chronic subglottic stenosis, cricotracheal resection with primary anastomosis is performed at a safe distance of > 1.5 cm from the vocal folds — maximum resection length is 4–6 cm


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Patients initially exhibiting only subtle symptoms and signs can progress to complete airway obstruction from worsening oedema — urgent review and close airway monitoring for at least 24 hours is essential, even for apparently minor injuries
> - The subglottic region is the most vulnerable area of the larynx — it is the only complete cartilaginous ring and is most sensitive even to trivial trauma
> - Bogdasarian and Olson's classification of posterior glottic stenosis (Types I–IV) addresses the chronic sequelae of laryngeal trauma — Type I is an isolated interarytenoid band, while Type IV involves bilateral arytenoid ankylosis
> - Antireflux therapy is recommended in all cases of laryngotracheal trauma — gastric acid irritation of the injured laryngeal mucosa promotes granulation tissue formation and stenosis
> - In paediatric laryngeal trauma, the preferred airway management is inhalational anaesthesia with spontaneous ventilation, followed by rigid bronchoscopy and tracheostomy — cricothyroidotomy is avoided in children
