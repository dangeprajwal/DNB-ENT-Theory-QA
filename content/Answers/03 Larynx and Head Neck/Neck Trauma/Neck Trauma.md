# Neck Trauma — Zones, Assessment and Management

> [!question] Questions
> Zones of the neck in trauma. Assessment and management of neck trauma. Penetrating trauma in the laryngeal region — assessment and management.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 38 (Neck Trauma)

---

## Introduction

The management of penetrating neck trauma has undergone a distinctive paradigm shift over the past few decades — from mandatory exploration towards selective, conservative management based on clinical evaluation and specialized investigations. Introduction of diagnostic tools such as flexible endoscopy, oesophagography, high-resolution CT, duplex Doppler and endovascular techniques has improved non-operative evaluation of aerodigestive and vascular injuries. The neck contains three principal anatomic components: vascular (carotid, vertebral and subclavian arteries; jugular and subclavian veins), digestive (pharynx and oesophagus) and respiratory (larynx and trachea). Knowledge of the anatomical zones and a systematic approach to evaluation is essential for safe management.

---

## PART A: Zones of the Neck

Roon and Christensen's classification divides the neck into three zones based on anatomical landmarks. All three zones contain major vascular and aerodigestive structures. However, injuries to Zones I and III are both diagnostically and surgically more challenging than Zone II injuries.

| Zone | Boundaries | Key Structures |
|---|---|---|
| I | Sternal notch/clavicle to cricoid | Subclavian vessels, brachiocephalic, thoracic duct, oesophagus, trachea, lung apex |
| II | Cricoid cartilage to angle of mandible | Carotid, IJV, larynx, hypopharynx, oesophagus |
| III | Angle of mandible to skull base | Distal ICA, vertebral artery, pharynx |

### Surgical Significance of Zones

- **Zone I:** Vascular control is difficult; may require sternotomy or thoracotomy for exposure; pre-operative angiography generally recommended
- **Zone II:** Most accessible surgically; injuries readily exposed through a standard neck incision; chance of detecting asymptomatic arterial injury by angiography is < 1%
- **Zone III:** Surgical exposure and vascular control are challenging; distal ICA is difficult to access; pre-operative angiography recommended; endovascular techniques are increasingly used
- **Transcervical injuries** (crossing the midline) are associated with vascular or aerodigestive injuries in 73–100% of patients and are not well accounted for by either classification system

## PART B: Assessment — ATLS Principles

### Primary Survey

Resuscitation should follow Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles. The primary survey focuses on factors causing early deaths — hypoxia, hypovolaemia, tension pneumothorax and head injury.

**A — Airway and Cervical Spine:**
- Examine for stridor and use of accessory muscles
- Stabilize neck with cervical collar until spine injury excluded
- Chin lift and jaw thrust to overcome obstruction
- GCS ≤ 8 usually requires intubation
- Endotracheal tube may be inserted through a penetrating cervical wound
- Cricothyroidotomy if intubation fails; convert to formal [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] within 24 hours

**B — Breathing:**
- Exclude tension pneumothorax and haemothorax
- Tension pneumothorax: hypoxia, hyper-resonance, decreased air entry, contralateral tracheal shift — decompress with needle thoracocentesis followed by chest drain

**C — Circulation:**
- Two high-flow 14-gauge cannulae in antecubital fossae (not on side of vascular injury)
- Crystalloid followed by O-negative blood if no improvement
- Active bleeding controlled with compressive dressing, digital pressure or Foley catheter balloon tamponade
- Patient must remain recumbent or wound covered to prevent air embolism

**D — Disability:** GCS, pupil reactivity, limb power

**E — Exposure:** Remove all clothing; keep warm

### Secondary Survey

A head-to-foot examination is conducted. Key points include:
- Direction of injury tract from wound locations
- Cranial nerve examination and Horner syndrome
- Cervical haematoma, subcutaneous emphysema, JVP
- Blood in nasogastric tube
- Peripheral pulse assessment, bruits
- Hemiplegia or Brown-Séquard syndrome

**Key principle:** Wounds should never be probed as this may cause massive haemorrhage from an arterial injury.

### Imaging and Investigations

| Investigation | Role |
|---|---|
| Chest X-ray | Haemo/pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, widened mediastinum (>8 cm on supine film) |
| C-spine X-ray | Spinal injury, prevertebral air |
| CT angiography | 90–100% sensitivity and specificity for arterial injury; largely replacing conventional angiography |
| Contrast swallow | Sensitivity 48–100% for oesophageal injury |
| Oesophagoscopy | Rigid: 89% sensitivity, 95% specificity; flexible: improving accuracy |

## PART C: Mandatory Versus Selective Exploration

### Historical Background

Prior to World War II, non-operative management had 15–18% mortality. Bailey (1944) proposed early exploration, reducing mortality to 7%. Fogelman and Stewart (1956) established mandatory exploration whenever the platysma was breached — mortality 6% (explored) versus 35% (not explored). Since 1963 (Stone), controversy has persisted, with the majority of trauma centres now advocating selective conservative management.

### Selective Exploration Algorithm

**Indications for emergency surgery:**
- Severe active bleeding
- Hypovolaemic shock not responding to resuscitation
- Rapidly expanding haematoma
- Large blowing wound
- Major haemoptysis

**Remaining patients** undergo clinical assessment with selective investigations based on clinical suspicion:

| Clinical Suspicion | Investigation |
|---|---|
| Vascular injury (large haematoma, pulse deficit, bruit, stroke) | Angiogram or CT angiography |
| Oesophageal injury (dysphagia, haematemesis, saliva leak, prevertebral air) | Oesophagogram, oesophagoscopy |
| Airway injury (persistent air leak, pneumomediastinum, emphysema, dysphonia) | Bronchoscopy |

### Evidence for Selective Management

Six prospective studies have validated selective conservative management with mortality rates of 0–1.8%. Demetriades et al. (335 patients) reported 100% sensitivity and 85% specificity for clinically significant injuries using clinical examination combined with selective investigations, with 80% of patients managed non-operatively. Even 80% of transcervical gunshots may be safely managed non-operatively by active observation.

## PART D: Management of Specific Injuries

### Pharyngeal Injury

- Suspect in Zone II penetrating injuries with dysphagia, odynophagia, voice change, haemoptysis or haematemesis
- Upper hypopharyngeal injuries (supra-arytenoid): may be managed non-operatively — the hypopharynx is capacious with low intraluminal pressure and is enveloped by two constrictor muscles
- Lower hypopharyngeal injuries (infra-arytenoid): should be explored, repaired and drained — higher intraluminal pressure, less capacious, surrounded only by the inferior constrictor

### Oesophageal Injury

Early recognition is critical — delayed diagnosis beyond 12 hours increases mortality from 9% to 40%. The majority of injuries can be repaired primarily. Key management principles:
- Intravenous antibiotics and enteral or parenteral nutrition
- Adequate drainage (suction or dependent) — 13% develop fistulae even with sound repair
- Barium swallow on day 5–7 (50% of post-operative fistulae are asymptomatic)
- Lateral cervical oesophagostomy if tissue blown away, mediastinitis, or injury extends into chest
- Local strap or SCM muscle flaps to buttress repair
- Small cervical oesophageal perforations may be managed conservatively with repeat oesophagography at day 5

### Tracheal Injury

- Priority is securing the airway — intubation through wound, tracheotomy if laryngeal trauma
- Minor injuries not otherwise requiring exploration: manage expectantly
- Tracheal repair with interrupted sutures
- Muscle flap interposition if associated oesophageal or vascular injury
- Tracheotomy may protect the repair in selected cases

### Vascular Injury

**Carotid artery (most common major vessel injured, 22% of vascular injuries):**

The management algorithm is based on haemodynamic status and neurological deficit:
- **Unstable patient:** Reperfuse
- **Stable patient:** Angiogram → if carotid injury:
  - Reperfuse if coma < 4–6 hours, no ischaemic infarct, distal flow present
  - Ligate if coma > 4–6 hours, ischaemic infarct or brain oedema, complete distal occlusion, or inaccessible high injury

**Vertebral artery:**
- Increasingly recognized with improved angiography
- Mortality 5–17% for isolated injury; increases to 50% with associated carotid injury
- Majority managed by angiographic embolization
- If discovered at surgery: ligate before entry into foramen transversarium (C6); bone wax for intra-foraminal bleeding

**Venous injury:**
- External jugular: ligate
- Internal jugular: lateral venorrhaphy or ligation
- If both IJVs injured, at least one must be repaired (prevent facial swelling, raised ICP, blindness)

**Endovascular techniques:** Increasingly used for false aneurysms and arteriovenous fistulae — covered stents or trapping with balloons/coils after confirming cross-flow through Circle of Willis.

## PART E: Exploration — General Principles

- IVI lines, suction, blood in theatre
- General anaesthesia with crash induction and cricoid pressure
- Airway: orotracheal/nasotracheal intubation or cricothyroidotomy/tracheotomy
- Position: supine, neck extended, head turned to opposite side (if no C-spine injury)
- Exposure: chest and face for Zones I and III; contralateral groin and lower leg for saphenous vein harvesting
- Incision: horizontal skin crease for localized injury; anterior border of SCM for wider exploration
- Zone I additional exposure: divide omohyoid; bilateral exploration via apron flap
- Zone III additional exposure: anterior dislocation of the mandible

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The neck is divided into three zones (Roon and Christensen) — Zone I (sternal notch to cricoid), Zone II (cricoid to angle of mandible), Zone III (angle of mandible to skull base)
> 2. Zone II injuries are the most accessible surgically — Zones I and III are diagnostically and surgically more challenging and generally require pre-operative angiography
> 3. There has been a paradigm shift from mandatory to selective exploration — properly executed, selective management is as safe as mandatory exploration with mortality rates of 0–1.8% in prospective studies
> 4. Indications for emergency surgery are severe active bleeding, shock unresponsive to resuscitation, rapidly expanding haematoma, large blowing wound, and major haemoptysis
> 5. CT angiography has 90–100% sensitivity and specificity for arterial injury and is largely replacing conventional angiography — colour-flow duplex Doppler combined with clinical examination has 91% sensitivity and 99% specificity
> 6. Early diagnosis of oesophageal injury is critical — delayed repair beyond 12 hours increases mortality from 9% to 40%; a combination of oesophagography and oesophagoscopy achieves 100% sensitivity
> 7. Carotid revascularization is preferred over ligation — except in patients with coma > 4–6 hours, ischaemic infarct on imaging, brain oedema, or complete distal occlusion
> 8. Vertebral artery injuries are best managed by angiographic embolization — mortality is 5–17% for isolated injury but increases to 50% if associated carotid injury is present
> 9. If both internal jugular veins are injured, at least one must be repaired to prevent raised ICP, facial swelling and blindness
> 10. Wounds should never be probed — digital pressure or Foley catheter balloon tamponade can control active cervical haemorrhage


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Transcervical gunshot wounds (crossing the midline) are associated with visceral/vascular injury in 73–100% of patients — yet 80% may be safely managed non-operatively with active observation and selective investigations
> - Upper hypopharyngeal (supra-arytenoid) injuries can be managed conservatively due to the capacious lumen and low intraluminal pressure — lower (infra-arytenoid) injuries require exploration and repair like oesophageal injuries
> - A Foley catheter balloon inflated within a bleeding neck wound is a highly effective haemostatic technique for life-threatening cervical haemorrhage, particularly from subclavian and cervical vessels
> - Small cervical oesophageal perforations may be managed conservatively with repeat oesophagography at day 5 — but overall, 50% of post-operative oesophageal fistulae are asymptomatic and detected only on contrast study
> - Cricothyroidotomy performed in the emergency setting should be converted to a formal tracheostomy within 24 hours to prevent [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]]
