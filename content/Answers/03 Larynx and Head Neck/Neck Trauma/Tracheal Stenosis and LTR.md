# Laryngotracheal Stenosis — Aetiology, Grading and Surgical Management

> [!question] Questions
> Tracheal stenosis — etiology and management. Laryngotracheal reconstruction and partial cricotracheal resection.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 76 (Laryngotracheal Stenosis in Adults); Chapter 71 (Contemporary Management of Laryngotracheal Trauma)

---

## Introduction

Laryngotracheal stenosis (LTS) is an abnormal narrowing of the laryngotracheal complex that causes breathlessness, retention of pulmonary secretions and can interfere with phonation and swallowing safety. With the discovery of antibiotics and the ability to ventilate patients for prolonged periods, a period of ventilation on the intensive care unit has become the most common cause of LTS, replacing infections. Approximately 50% of adult airway stenosis is post-intubation. Although significant airway injury has been reported in 47% of patients bronchoscoped at extubation, long-term airway compromise develops in only 1–4%, suggesting that the initial fibro-inflammatory injury resolves in the majority and only leads to persistent narrowing in a subset through healing by fibrosis and wound contracture.

## PART A: Aetiology

### Causes of Laryngotracheal Stenosis (600 Consecutive Referrals — National Centre for Airway Reconstruction)

| Cause | Percentage |
|---|---|
| Acquired LTS (post-intubation) | 50.3% (34.3% subglottic, 16% tracheal) |
| Bilateral vocal cord mobility impairment | 16.67% |
| Granulomatosis with polyangitis (GPA) | 10.0% |
| Idiopathic [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] (ISS) | 8.67% |
| Supraglottic stenosis | 5.70% (2.7% sarcoid, 3.0% other) |
| Previous papillomatosis treatment | 2.33% |
| Glottic web | 2.33% |
| Tracheomalacia | 1.33% (0.67% relapsing polychondritis) |
| Vascular lesion | 1.17% |
| Amyloidosis | 0.83% |
| Congenital subglottic stenosis | 0.67% |

### Risk Factors for Post-Intubation LTS

- Duration of ventilation
- Sizing of ventilation tubes (large diameter)
- Excessive cuff pressures
- Irritation from orogastric secretions
- Infections
- Immune disturbance
- Individual healing biology

## PART B: Pathophysiology

### Poiseuille's Law

In laminar airflow, airway resistance is governed by Poiseuille's law:

**R = 8ηl / πr⁴**

Where R = resistance, η = viscosity, l = length, r = radius.

The resistance to airflow by a stenotic segment is proportional to its length but **inversely proportional to the fourth power of its radius**. This means that even a small reduction in airway radius causes a dramatic increase in resistance. Turbulence further adds to resistance and is also inversely proportional to airway diameter.

### Flow Volume Loops

Flow volume loops are characteristic for different types of obstruction:

- **Variable extrathoracic stenosis:** Limits inspiration (reduced lumen during inspiration as intraluminal pressure falls below atmospheric)
- **Variable intrathoracic stenosis:** Limits expiration
- **Fixed obstruction** (intra- or extrathoracic): Limits peak airflow equally on inspiration and expiration — produces characteristic flattened loop

### Pathophysiology of Stenosis Development

The sequence is: mucosal ulceration → fibrinoid necrosis → fibrosis → cicatrix → stenosis. Chronic airway obstruction may be misdiagnosed as asthma or COPD. Patients compensate by reducing physical activity and the same degree of stenosis occurring acutely may cause significant disability or death.

## PART C: Diagnosis and Grading

### Clinical Assessment

- History: previous prolonged ventilation, duration of dyspnoea, coexisting conditions (vasculitis, sarcoidosis)
- Symptoms: exertional dyspnoea (main symptom), voice changes, chronic cough, swallowing impairment, laryngopharyngeal reflux
- Examination: severity of stridor, chest recession, BMI, neck scars
- Fibre-optic nasal endoscopy: vocal cord mobility, pooling of secretions, degree of stenosis

### Investigations

| Investigation | Role |
|---|---|
| CT scan | High-definition imaging; may under- or overestimate stenosis depending on slice level and secretions |
| Spirometry and flow volume loops | Essential to determine severity and monitor response to treatment |
| Cardiopulmonary exercise testing | Ideally performed for comprehensive assessment |
| FEES / videofluoroscopy | If any evidence of disordered swallowing — poor outcomes reported in patients with even minor aspiration following reconstruction |

### Myer–Cotton Grading System

| Grade | Obstruction |
|---|---|
| I | No obstruction to 50% |
| II | 51% to 70% |
| III | 71% to 99% |
| IV | No detectable lumen |

### Multidisciplinary Team

Patients must be managed by a team including ENT and thoracic surgeon, pulmonologist, anaesthetist, radiologist, pathologist, speech and swallowing therapist, and dietician. Bariatric services should be available as obesity increases resting metabolic rate, decreases respiratory performance, and worsens surgical outcomes.

## PART D: Endoscopic Management

### Technique for Post-Intubation LTS

Using suspension laryngoscopy with supraglottic high-frequency jet ventilation:

1. **Steroid injection:** Up to 3 ml of methylprednisolone acetate (40 mg/ml) injected into the stenosis
2. **Radial laser cuts:** 3–4 radial incisions made with CO₂ laser (8–10 watts) through operating microscope
3. **Balloon dilatation:** Pulmonary balloon dilator system used to dilate to size of adjacent normal airway

This procedure is typically repeated every 3–4 weeks until the airway has stabilized. By the third procedure, it is clear whether the technique will work or whether open surgery is needed.

### Success Rate

The endoscopic approach is successful in approximately **two-thirds** of post-intubation LTS cases. Failure occurs with:
- Significant cartilage framework damage
- Stenosis length > 3 cm
- Obesity
- Swallowing impairment with persistent airway inflammation

### Post-[[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]] Stenosis

A specific variant causing a **'lambda-shaped' deformity** due to over-resection or fracturing of anterior tracheal rings during tracheostomy insertion. At decannulation, scarring pulls in lateral ring remnants. Management:
- CO₂ laser (8–10 W superpulsed) to vaporize encroaching cartilage
- Preserve mucosa over trachealis and apex of lambda to avoid circumferential injury
- Balloon dilatation
- Rarely requires more than 3 endoscopic interventions; if refractory, open tracheal resection

## PART E: Open Surgical Approaches

### Tracheal Resection and Anastomosis

Indicated when endoscopic management fails due to loss of cartilaginous support or refractory stenosis.

**Key principles:**
- Maximum resection length: **4–6 cm** even with laryngeal and pulmonary release procedures
- Stenosis must be at a safe distance of **> 1.5 cm from the inferior aspect of the vocal folds** for resection with primary anastomosis
- Pearson et al.: up to half the trachea can be resected with primary anastomosis
- Macchiarini et al.: recommend ≤ 4 cm to avoid anastomotic tension and failure
- Pre-operative optimization: treat comorbidities (diabetes, immunosuppression)
- Complete resection of stenotic tissue is essential

### Cricotracheal Resection

Used when stenosis involves the subglottic region and proximal trachea. The technique involves:
- Resection of the stenosed subglottic/proximal tracheal segment
- Primary anastomosis of trachea to the remaining cricoid
- Superior results to rib graft augmentation in adults (high incidence of ischaemic necrosis of rib grafts in adults, and quality/quantity of harvestable rib cartilage diminishes with age)

### Laryngotracheal Reconstruction (LTR)

More commonly used in paediatric LTS using rib grafts to augment the airway. In adults:
- Single and double stage reconstruction described
- Anterior and posterior grafting may be used
- Some surgeons still use primary cartilage grafts, but there is poor appreciation of the high incidence of ischaemic necrosis of rib grafts in adult patients
- Tracheal/cricotracheal resection generally preferred over LTR in adults

## PART F: Special Conditions

### Idiopathic Subglottic Stenosis (ISS)

- Rare, slowly progressive fibro-inflammatory process of unknown aetiology
- Predominantly affects **post-pubertal women** (Caucasians)
- Involves subglottis and first/second tracheal rings; may extend to glottis
- Diagnosis of exclusion: no intubation/tracheostomy in last 2 years, no neck trauma/surgery/[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], negative ANCA and ACE at repeated intervals

**Management:**
- Majority managed with endoscopic airway surgery (steroid injection + radial CO₂ laser cuts + balloon dilatation) once or twice per year
- Aggressive laser ablation is not advised — risks more severe recurrence
- Cricotracheal resection: good results reported but not universally reproducible; concerns about 'male-type' voice in predominantly female patients; disease may extend to glottis making safe resection difficult
- Alternative approach: laryngofissure with posterior cricoid split, placement of costal cartilage spacer, and covered silastic stent for 2 weeks followed by endoscopic removal and decannulation

### Granulomatosis with Polyangitis (GPA)

- Formerly Wegener's granulomatosis; immune disorder of small/medium vessels
- Positive cANCA aids diagnosis (but 20% of untreated active GPA lack cANCA)
- Approximately 25% have laryngeal/tracheal/bronchial involvement
- Management: intralesional corticosteroid injections, radial laser cuts and balloon dilatation
- Long-term stents and tracheostomies should be avoided — lead to difficult airway complications

### Sarcoidosis

- Non-caseating granulomatous disorder; laryngeal involvement in 1–5%
- Predominantly affects ages 20–40 years; F:M ratio 2:1; predilection for Black African Americans
- Supraglottic larynx most commonly involved
- Treatment: CO₂ laser 'pepper pot' pattern (multiple narrow pits separated by 2–3 mm) — avoiding circumferential injury; average 2 treatments needed

### Mitomycin-C

- Derived from Streptomyces caespitosus; inhibits fibroblast proliferation by cross-linking DNA
- Applied topically at 1 mg/ml for 3 minutes after airway surgery
- Despite widespread use, no randomized controlled trials proving efficacy
- Authors have stopped using MMC due to lack of clear evidence and uncertainty about long-term toxicity

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Post-intubation injury is the most common cause of adult LTS (50.3%) — risk factors include prolonged ventilation, large tubes, high cuff pressures and patient comorbidities
> 2. Poiseuille's law states that resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius — even a small reduction in airway diameter causes a dramatic increase in resistance
> 3. The Myer–Cotton grading system classifies subglottic stenosis into 4 grades — Grade I (≤50%), Grade II (51–70%), Grade III (71–99%), Grade IV (no detectable lumen)
> 4. Endoscopic management (steroid injection + radial CO₂ laser cuts + balloon dilatation) is successful in approximately two-thirds of post-intubation LTS — failure occurs with cartilage damage, stenosis >3 cm, obesity and swallowing impairment
> 5. Maximum tracheal resection length is 4–6 cm with release procedures — stenosis must be >1.5 cm from the vocal folds for safe resection with primary anastomosis
> 6. Cricotracheal resection is preferred over rib graft augmentation (LTR) in adults — rib grafts have a high incidence of ischaemic necrosis in adults and cartilage quality diminishes with age
> 7. ISS predominantly affects post-pubertal Caucasian women — diagnosis is by exclusion requiring negative ANCA, negative ACE, no intubation history and tissue biopsy to rule out other inflammatory conditions
> 8. GPA (formerly Wegener's) causes laryngotracheal stenosis in 25% of cases — positive cANCA aids diagnosis but 20% of untreated active GPA lack cANCA; managed with intralesional steroids and radial laser cuts
> 9. Post-tracheostomy stenosis produces a characteristic 'lambda-shaped' deformity — managed endoscopically with CO₂ laser while preserving the trachealis mucosa to avoid circumferential injury
> 10. Flow volume loops are essential for diagnosis and monitoring — a fixed obstruction produces a characteristic flattened loop with equal limitation of inspiratory and expiratory peak flows


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Chronic airway obstruction from LTS is frequently misdiagnosed as asthma or COPD — patients compensate by reducing physical activity, and the same degree of stenosis occurring acutely may cause significant disability or death
> - Patients with even minor aspiration on pre-operative assessment have poor outcomes following airway reconstruction — FEES or videofluoroscopy should be performed in all cases with any evidence of disordered swallowing
> - The endoscopic approach for LTS uses suspension laryngoscopy with supraglottic jet ventilation — this provides binocular vision, two hands free for surgery, and allows use of CO₂ laser, balloon dilators, microdebriders and stents
> - In ISS, aggressive laser ablation of the stenosis is specifically contraindicated — it risks more severe stenosis recurrence; the radial cut and dilatation technique is preferred
> - Antireflux therapy is prescribed for all LTS patients — laryngopharyngeal reflux promotes ongoing mucosal inflammation and restenosis
