# Croup, Epiglottitis, Diphtheria and the Difficult Paediatric Airway

> [!question] Questions
> Causes of croup. Epiglottitis — features and management. Stridor in children — diphtheria management. Evaluation and management of difficult airway in children.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapters 28 (Stridor) and 29 (Acute Laryngeal Infections)

---

## Introduction

Acute laryngeal infections are the commonest cause of acquired stridor in children. Croup (acute viral laryngotracheobronchitis) accounts for 90% of acute airway obstruction in children. Epiglottitis, though much less frequent since the introduction of Hib vaccination, remains a life-threatening emergency. Bacterial tracheitis is a rare but dangerous condition that must be differentiated from croup when the child fails to respond to standard treatment. Diphtheria, while rare in immunised populations, remains important in developing countries and in unvaccinated children. Management requires a multidisciplinary team approach with protocols for securing the airway using endotracheal intubation (now preferred over [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] in most cases).

---

## PART A: Croup (Acute Viral Laryngotracheobronchitis)

### Aetiology

Croup presents as hoarseness, a distinctive barking cough, and inspiratory or biphasic stridor, usually preceded by upper respiratory tract symptoms, malaise and pyrexia. It is caused by mucosal oedema of the larynx and trachea following viral infection. Critical obstruction occurs at the subglottis — just 1 mm of oedema in an 18-month-old reduces subglottic cross-sectional area by approximately 50%.

**Causative organisms:** Parainfluenza virus type I (classic cause), parainfluenza type II, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rhinovirus, metapneumovirus, coronavirus, bocavirus.

**Epidemiology:** Children 6 months to 3 years, peak at 2 years. Boys more commonly affected. Annual incidence 1.5-6% in children <6 years. Seasonal — autumn peak in odd years (biennial parainfluenza type I epidemics).

**Course:** Self-limiting — 50% improve within 24 hours, most recover within 4 days. Mortality rare (~1 in 30,000 cases). Children do not typically drool or appear toxic (contrast with epiglottitis).

### Westley Croup Score

| Parameter | Score 0 | Score 1 | Score 2 | Score 3 | Score 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inspiratory stridor | None | With stethoscope | Without stethoscope | — | — |
| Retraction | None | Mild | Moderate | Severe | — |
| Air entry | Normal | Decreased | Severely decreased | — | — |
| Cyanosis | None | With agitation | At rest | — | — |
| Consciousness | Normal | — | — | — | Altered |

Maximum score 17. Mild = 2-3, Moderate = 4-7, Severe = ≥8.

### Management

**Mild croup:** Supportive care, reassurance, observation. Do not separate child from carers. No evidence for humidification but may be soothing.

**Corticosteroids (mainstay of treatment):** Cochrane review (41 studies) — glucocorticoids produce significant clinical improvement within 6 hours. Benefit at 12 hours, no longer significant at 24 hours. Treatment should be extended to ALL children with croup including mild cases (not limited to moderate/severe). **Oral dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg** is preferred (safety and efficacy). Nebulised budesonide 2 mg if vomiting.

**Nebulised epinephrine:** 1 mL of 1:1000 in 3 mL 0.9% saline. Alpha-agonist effect — vasoconstriction and bronchodilation. Maximum effect within 30-60 minutes, no lasting benefit beyond 2 hours. Does not alter natural history but may eliminate need for artificial airway. Can be given concurrently with glucocorticoids.

**Heliox:** 70:30 helium:oxygen mixture — less dense than air, promotes laminar flow, reduces work of breathing. Some evidence for short-term benefit in moderate-severe croup (Cochrane review).

**Important:** Recurrent croup should raise suspicion of underlying congenital mild [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] — endoscopy is mandatory.

## PART B: Bacterial Tracheitis

A rare but life-threatening cause of upper airway obstruction. Characterised by profuse mucopurulent secretions with sloughing of respiratory epithelium. Secretions are adherent and may occlude the airway.

**Clinical features:** Toxic child with high fevers, worsening stridor unresponsive to steroids and nebulised epinephrine. Older children affected (mean age 4-8 years) compared with croup. Increased susceptibility in Down syndrome and immunodeficiency.

**Diagnosis:** Confirmed at airway endoscopy — pseudomembrane in subglottis and trachea, thick mucopus and debris extending into bronchi.

**Management:** Direct LTB under GA, removal of tracheal secretions and pulmonary toilet. Secure airway by endotracheal intubation (may be needed for days). Repeated endoscopic procedures almost invariably required. Broad-spectrum parenteral antibiotics (Staphylococcus aureus most common; also H. influenzae, Moraxella, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Pseudomonas).

**Complications:** Airway stenosis, ARDS, respiratory failure, toxic shock syndrome, anoxic encephalopathy, death.

## PART C: Acute Epiglottitis (Supraglottitis)

### Clinical Presentation

Classical presentation of a **toxic child** with short history (hours) of sore throat, inspiratory stridor, muffled voice, drooling (odynophagia/dysphagia). Progressive respiratory distress. Child is febrile, tachypnoeic, sitting upright with neck extended, using accessory muscles. Age 2-8 years, increased prevalence in winter.

### Key Management Principles

- **Pharyngeal examination with tongue depressor is CONTRAINDICATED** — may precipitate acute airway obstruction
- Fibre-optic or small rigid endoscope can assist diagnosis if presentation is atypical
- Endoscopic evaluation confirms gross erythema and oedema of supraglottic structures
- **Investigations before securing the airway are contraindicated** (lateral soft tissue X-ray shows "thumb sign" — thickened oedematous epiglottis — but must not delay airway management)

### Airway Management

- Endotracheal intubation under controlled conditions (OR or PICU) by personnel skilled in airway intervention
- ENT surgeon should be available with tracheostomy set ready
- Nasotracheal intubation preferred for prolonged intubation (better tolerated, more secure, easier nursing)
- Usually extubated within 48 hours
- Mortality remains ~3%

### Treatment

Intravenous antibiotics — third-generation cephalosporins for 5-7 days (ampicillin resistance >50% in H. influenzae). Chloramphenicol/clindamycin as alternatives for allergy.

### Post-Hib Vaccination Era

Hib vaccination has reduced epiglottitis by ~90%. Non-type b H. influenzae, meningococci, group A streptococci, pneumococci and Staphylococcus aureus now more commonly causative. Clinical presentation unchanged — reduced clinical experience must not lead to increased mortality.

## PART D: Diphtheria

### Aetiology and Clinical Features

Caused by toxogenic strains of **Corynebacterium diphtheriae** and C. ulcerans. Rare in immunised populations but remains significant in developing countries. Symptoms onset 2-5 days after exposure.

**Pharyngeal diphtheria:** Pharyngitis with sore throat and malaise. Characteristic grey pseudomembrane over tonsils — necrotic tissue, bacteria and fibrinous exudate. Early removal causes bleeding. Bull-neck appearance (cellulitis, lymphadenopathy).

**Laryngeal diphtheria:** Inspiratory stridor, paroxysmal barking cough, dysphagia. Death may follow from acute airway obstruction or exotoxin effects.

**Exotoxin effects:**
- Toxic myocarditis (second week — may be fatal)
- Peripheral neuritis — palatal paralysis most common (nasal regurgitation, hypernasal speech)

### Management

- Early diagnosis is critical — treatment should not await microbiological confirmation
- **High-dose benzylpenicillin** (IV)
- **Antitoxin** (10,000-100,000 units depending on severity) — delays increase mortality as antitoxin cannot neutralise already tissue-bound toxin
- Airway management: removal of laryngeal membrane, oxygen, humidification, intubation or tracheostomy if necessary
- Systemic steroids may reduce need for airway intervention
- Bed rest until danger of myocarditis has passed

## PART E: Management of the Difficult Paediatric Airway

### General Principles

- Assessment, history taking and resuscitation proceed simultaneously in the acute situation
- Do NOT upset a child with a compromised but functional airway — crying and distress may convert partial to complete obstruction
- Keep child in position of comfort with carers, calm environment
- Interventions (venepuncture, radiographs, nasendoscopy) may jeopardise a critical airway

### Airway Interventions

**Endotracheal intubation preferred over tracheostomy** — advances in anaesthesia and paediatric intensive care have made intubation the standard approach. Paediatric tracheostomy carries significant morbidity (even short-term) and potential mortality.

**Difficult intubation aids:**
- Small ventilating bronchoscope (may be easier to pass than ETT)
- Hopkins rod telescope with ETT mounted — telescope guides tube into position
- Laryngeal mask airway (LMA) — useful for fibre-optic tracheobronchoscopy, particularly in mandibular hypoplasia

**ENT surgeon role:** Must be informed if a child with airway obstruction of unknown aetiology is to be intubated. Emergency tracheostomy set with correct-size tube should be immediately available (though very rarely needed with experienced paediatric anaesthetists).

### Emergency Tracheostomy in Children

Now very rarely required. Key points:
- Stay strictly in the midline
- Position child carefully without neck or head deviation
- A finger on each side of the larynx to maintain orientation
- Vertical skin incision through to trachea
- If paediatric tracheostomy tube not available, a paediatric ETT can be used (ensuring it stays above the carina)

### CHAOS and EXIT

**Congenital High Airway Obstruction Syndrome (CHAOS):** Recognised antenatally on ultrasound. Managed by **ex utero intrapartum treatment (EXIT)** — delivery of foetal head and upper torso through hysterotomy incision while maintaining placental blood/gas exchange. Airway is secured (intubation, bronchoscopy, tracheostomy) while on placental support.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Croup accounts for 90% of acute airway obstruction in children — parainfluenza type I is the classic cause; it is self-limiting with 50% improving within 24 hours and mortality of ~1 in 30,000
> 2. Glucocorticoids are the mainstay of croup treatment — Cochrane review supports use in ALL cases (including mild); oral dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg is preferred for safety and efficacy
> 3. Nebulised epinephrine (1 mL 1:1000 in 3 mL saline) provides transient benefit within 30-60 minutes lasting up to 2 hours — it does not alter natural history but may eliminate need for artificial airway
> 4. Pharyngeal examination with tongue depressor is absolutely contraindicated in suspected epiglottitis — it may precipitate acute complete airway obstruction
> 5. Epiglottitis presents as a toxic child with rapid onset stridor, muffled voice, drooling and sitting upright — endotracheal intubation under controlled conditions is the airway management of choice
> 6. Hib vaccination has reduced epiglottitis by ~90% but clinical presentation is unchanged — reduced experience must not lead to increased mortality; non-type b organisms now more commonly causative
> 7. Bacterial tracheitis should be suspected when a child with apparent croup fails to respond to steroids and epinephrine — it requires LTB, airway toilet, intubation and broad-spectrum antibiotics
> 8. Diphtheria treatment requires early high-dose benzylpenicillin AND antitoxin — the decision to administer antitoxin should be based on clinical suspicion as delays increase mortality
> 9. Nasotracheal intubation is preferred over tracheostomy for acute laryngeal infections — it is better tolerated, more secure and allows easier nursing; extubation is usually within 48 hours for epiglottitis
> 10. An ENT surgeon should be informed whenever a child with airway obstruction of unknown aetiology is to be intubated — emergency tracheostomy equipment must be immediately available


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A child with croup does not drool or appear toxic — drooling and toxicity point toward epiglottitis or [[Retropharyngeal Abscess|retropharyngeal abscess]]; this clinical distinction guides the urgency and nature of airway intervention
> - The Westley Croup Score correlates with tracheal lumen diameter on radiography — however, it is less commonly used in daily practice due to interobserver variance; clinical judgement remains paramount
> - Bacterial tracheitis carries risk of sudden complete obstruction from dislodged pseudomembranes even after intubation — vigilant expert nursing care and repeated endoscopic procedures are essential
> - The introduction of Hib vaccination has not eliminated epiglottitis — it has changed the bacteriology (non-type b organisms now predominate), but the clinical presentation and lethal potential remain identical
> - In the EXIT procedure for CHAOS, the principle is "operating on placental support" — this allows unhurried securing of the neonatal airway while materno-foetal gas exchange is maintained through intact placental circulation
