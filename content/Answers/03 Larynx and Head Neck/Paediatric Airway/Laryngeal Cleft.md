# Laryngeal Cleft — Definition, Classification, Diagnosis and Management

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Laryngeal cleft — definition, features, diagnosis and management. Type 1 laryngeal cleft. Disc battery ingestion management.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 30 (Congenital Disorders of the Larynx, Trachea and Bronchi) and Chapter 34 (Foreign Bodies)

---

## Introduction

Posterior laryngeal clefts result from failure of the posterior cricoid lamina to fuse during embryogenesis. In more extensive laryngotracheo-oesophageal clefts, there is also incomplete development of the tracheo-oesophageal septum. Approximately 25% of patients with a laryngeal cleft also have a tracheo-oesophageal fistula (TOF), but the incidence of laryngeal cleft in patients with TOF is low. The majority of laryngeal cleft patients have associated congenital abnormalities. The Benjamin-Inglis classification relates well to symptom severity and treatment approach. Type I clefts may require no treatment or endoscopic repair, while type IV clefts extending into the thoracic trachea carry a mortality of up to 66%.

---

## PART A: Classification (Benjamin-Inglis)

| Type | Extent | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Type I | Down to level of vocal cords | Interarytenoid cleft only |
| Type II | Below vocal cords into cricoid | Extends into cricoid cartilage |
| Type III | Into cervical trachea | Beyond cricoid into trachea |
| Type IV | Into thoracic trachea | May extend to carina |

---

## PART B: Associations and Clinical Features

### Associated Conditions

- Tracheo-oesophageal fistula (most common association — 25%)
- Tracheobronchomalacia
- Congenital heart disease, dextrocardia, situs inversus
- Severe gastro-oesophageal reflux
- **Opitz-Frias syndrome (G syndrome):** hypertelorism, cleft lip/palate, laryngeal cleft, hypospadias
- **Pallister-Hall syndrome:** hypothalamic hamartoblastoma, hypopituitarism, imperforate anus, postaxial polydactyly, bifid epiglottis, laryngeal cleft

### Clinical Presentation by Type

**Type I:** Cyanotic attacks on feeding, recurrent chest infections. Stridor (similar to [[Stridor and Laryngomalacia|laryngomalacia]]) may be present due to prolapse of cleft edges into the airway.

**Type II and III:** Dramatic aspiration with recurrent pneumonia, sometimes with stridor and an abnormal cry.

**Type IV:** Severe aspiration, cyanosis and incipient cardiorespiratory failure.

Symptoms become more severe the longer the cleft. The differential diagnosis includes gastro-oesophageal reflux, neuromuscular incoordination of deglutition, vocal cord paralysis, raised intracranial pressure and TOF.

---

## PART C: Diagnosis

### Endoscopy (Gold Standard)

MLB is essential — no other diagnostic method can replace it. **Suspension microlaryngoscopy** allows the use of two probes to part the arytenoids. Without this manoeuvre, the diagnosis may be missed as redundant mucosa tends to prolapse into the defect and obscure it. The interarytenoid groove is probed and its lower limit compared with the posterior commissure.

### Other Investigations

- Plain chest X-ray: changes secondary to recurrent aspiration pneumonitis
- Lateral neck X-ray: vaguely increased laryngeal soft tissue; nasogastric tube may protrude anteriorly into the airway
- Videofluoroscopic contrast swallow: may not differentiate laryngeal incompetence from neuromuscular incoordination — a high index of suspicion is required

## PART D: Management

### Type I Laryngeal Cleft

- **Short Type I with no aspiration:** No treatment required
- **Minimal aspiration:** Managed by thickening feeds
- **Significant aspiration:** Endoscopic repair in two layers using nasogastric feeding tube until suture line heals. The endoscopic approach involves approximation of the cleft edges with sutures placed through the mucosa on each side

### Type II Laryngeal Cleft

- Very short Type II: May be repaired endoscopically (with difficulty) using nasogastric tube
- Long Type II: Requires anterior approach through extended laryngofissure with low [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] to cover the procedure; gastrostomy required (nasogastric tube tends to erode through suture line) combined with Nissen fundoplication

### Type III Laryngeal Cleft

Requires open anterior approach through extended laryngofissure with tracheostomy. Repair is in three layers — the two mucosal layers are reinforced by an interposition graft of tibial periosteum or temporalis fascia. Gastrostomy with Nissen fundoplication is required. These children take many months to learn to swallow after successful repair, requiring long-term gastrostomy feeding.

### Type IV Laryngeal Cleft

The most difficult surgical challenge. Tracheostomy is unhelpful due to the length of the cleft (tube convexity would erode the suture line). Repair must use a single-stage technique with endotracheal extubation at 7-10 days.

- Short Type IV: Anterior cervical approach, pulling trachea into neck
- Long Type IV: Lateral cervical approach with thoracotomy, or anterior cervicothoracic approach via median sternotomy with ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass
- Post-operative tracheomalacia may prevent extubation, necessitating tracheostomy once the cleft has healed

### Outcomes

Overall mortality approximately 14%. Type IV laryngotracheo-oesophageal clefts: mortality 66%. Full-length clefts to the carina: mortality up to 100%. A large proportion of mortality is from causes unrelated to the cleft itself. Management should be in a major paediatric centre with full multidisciplinary team.

## PART E: Disc Battery Ingestion Management

Disc (button) battery ingestion is a paediatric emergency. Batteries lodged in the oesophagus cause tissue injury through three mechanisms: electrical current generation causing electrolysis and hydroxide production (the most significant mechanism), pressure necrosis and leakage of alkaline contents.

### Key Principles

- **Oesophageal impaction is an emergency** — removal within 2 hours is recommended to minimise injury
- Batteries in the oesophagus must be removed urgently by rigid oesophagoscopy under general anaesthesia
- Batteries that have passed beyond the oesophagus into the stomach can usually be observed with serial radiographs — most pass spontaneously
- Lithium coin cells (3V, 20 mm diameter) are the most dangerous due to higher voltage and larger size
- The negative pole (flat side) causes the most damage — tissue injury occurs at the anode
- Injuries include mucosal burns, oesophageal perforation, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, aorto-oesophageal fistula (potentially fatal), mediastinitis and vocal cord paralysis
- Honey or sucralfate may be given pre-removal to limit ongoing injury if endoscopic removal is delayed

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Posterior laryngeal clefts are classified by the Benjamin-Inglis system — Type I (to vocal cords), Type II (into cricoid), Type III (into cervical trachea), Type IV (into thoracic trachea)
> 2. Approximately 25% of patients with laryngeal cleft also have a TOF — associated syndromes include Opitz-Frias (hypertelorism, cleft lip/palate, hypospadias) and Pallister-Hall (hypothalamic hamartoblastoma, bifid epiglottis)
> 3. MLB with bimanual probing of the interarytenoid groove is essential for diagnosis — without parting the arytenoids with two probes, redundant mucosa may obscure the defect and the diagnosis will be missed
> 4. Type I clefts with no aspiration need no treatment; significant aspiration requires endoscopic two-layer repair — Type III and IV clefts require open surgery with three-layer repair (two mucosal layers + interposition graft)
> 5. Type IV cleft repair requires single-stage technique via cervicothoracic approach with ECMO/cardiopulmonary bypass — tracheostomy is unhelpful as tube convexity erodes the suture line
> 6. Overall mortality is ~14%, rising to 66% for Type IV and up to 100% for full-length clefts — management should be in a major paediatric centre with full multidisciplinary team
> 7. Disc battery impaction in the oesophagus is a surgical emergency — removal within 2 hours is recommended; lithium coin cells (20 mm, 3V) cause the most severe injury through electrolysis
> 8. Tissue injury from disc batteries occurs primarily through electrical current generating hydroxide ions at the negative pole — complications include oesophageal perforation, TEF, and potentially fatal aorto-oesophageal fistula


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always probe the interarytenoid groove with two probes at MLB in any child presenting with unexplained aspiration and stridor — a laryngeal cleft is easily missed on routine endoscopy without this specific manoeuvre
> - A laryngeal cleft should be suspected in any neonate with aspiration who also has a TOF — the two conditions coexist in 25% of cleft patients
> - Long Type II and Type III clefts require gastrostomy rather than nasogastric feeding post-repair — the nasogastric tube tends to erode through the delicate suture line
> - An oesophageal button battery can cause full-thickness perforation within 2 hours and aorto-oesophageal fistula within 8-12 hours — this is a true emergency that takes priority over all other non-life-threatening surgical cases
