# Stridor in Children — Causes, Evaluation and Laryngomalacia Management

> [!question] Questions
> Stridor in newborn — causes, evaluation and role of MLB. Stridor in children — causes and diphtheria management. Laryngomalacia — features, surgical management. Clinical anatomy of bronchus on bronchoscopy.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapters 28 (Stridor), 29 (Acute Laryngeal Infections) and 30 (Congenital Disorders of the Larynx, Trachea and Bronchi)

---

## Introduction

Stridor is a high-pitched noise generated by turbulent airflow through a narrowed airway at the level of the larynx or trachea. It is a symptom, not a diagnosis. The paediatric airway is particularly vulnerable to obstruction because of its smaller calibre — Poiseuille's law dictates that airway resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius. Just 1 mm of mucosal swelling in a 4 mm neonatal airway results in a 75% reduction in airflow and a 16-fold increase in resistance. Laryngomalacia is the most common congenital cause of stridor and is self-limiting in approximately 90% of cases. However, a systematic approach to evaluation is essential, as a second co-existing pathology may be present in up to 20% of cases. Laryngotracheobronchoscopy (LTB/MLB) remains the gold standard for assessment.

---

## PART A: Definitions and Airway Dynamics

### Types of Noisy Breathing

| Type | Pitch | Site of Obstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Stridor | High-pitched, musical | Larynx and trachea |
| Stertor | Low-pitched, snoring | Naso-/oropharynx |
| Wheeze | High-pitched, musical | Small intrathoracic airways |

### Phase of Stridor and Localisation

| Phase | Level of Obstruction |
|---|---|
| Inspiratory | Extrathoracic (supraglottis/glottis) |
| Biphasic | Subglottis/trachea |
| Expiratory | Intrathoracic airway |

The extrathoracic airway tends to collapse on inspiration (negative intraluminal pressure) and expand on expiration. The intrathoracic airway shows the opposite behaviour. The subglottis and trachea, supported by cartilage, produce biphasic stridor when narrowed.

### Associated Clinical Features by Level

| Feature | Typical Diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Hoarseness | Laryngeal lesion (VCP, papilloma) |
| Barking cough | Croup |
| Cough with aspiration | TOF, VCP, laryngeal cleft |
| Dysphagia/feeding difficulty | Epiglottitis, [[Retropharyngeal Abscess|retropharyngeal abscess]] |
| Apnoeic spells ("dying spells") | Severe tracheobronchomalacia |

## PART B: Causes of Stridor in Children

### Congenital Causes

**Supraglottic:** Laryngomalacia (most common congenital cause), saccular cysts, laryngocoeles, vascular malformations, bifid epiglottis

**Glottic:** Vocal cord paralysis (second most common congenital anomaly), anterior glottic webs (associated with 22q11 deletion), laryngeal atresia, cri-du-chat syndrome

**Subglottic:** Congenital [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]] (third most common), subglottic haemangioma (85% present within first 6 months; 50% have cutaneous haemangioma)

**Tracheal:** Tracheal stenosis, complete tracheal rings, primary tracheomalacia, secondary tracheomalacia (vascular compression)

### Acquired Causes — Neonates

Intubation trauma, surgical trauma (e.g. laser), arytenoid fixation, reflux laryngitis

### Acquired Causes — Children

Croup (most common acute cause — 90%), epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body, [[Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis Larynx|recurrent respiratory papillomatosis]], hereditary angioedema, epidermolysis bullosa, intubation trauma

## PART C: Evaluation of the Child with Stridor

### History

**Perinatal history:** Prematurity, neonatal intubation (even brief SCBU admission), difficult delivery (VCP risk). Stridor present at birth suggests a fixed congenital narrowing (web, SGS, bilateral VCP). Gradual onset in first few weeks suggests laryngomalacia. Progressive worsening implies enlarging lesion (haemangioma, mediastinal mass).

**Pattern:** Laryngomalacia — worse with activity/crying/feeding, better at rest/sleep. Pharyngeal obstruction — worse during sleep. Improvement with crying — nasal obstruction (bilateral choanal atresia). Positional variation — supine worsening suggests tongue base obstruction (micrognathia).

**Associated features:** Feeding difficulties, failure to thrive, apnoeic spells, cyanosis, cough, hoarseness, aspiration episodes. History of "recurrent croup" should raise suspicion of congenital mild subglottic stenosis.

### Examination

- Observe the child at rest before formal examination — assess degree of respiratory distress (recession, nasal flaring, head bobbing, accessory muscle use)
- Severity of recession is a better indicator of airway compromise than degree of stridor
- Paradoxically, stridor volume may decrease as obstruction worsens (diminishing airflow)
- Cyanosis is a very late sign suggesting severe or prolonged obstruction
- Assess nasal patency, jaw and tongue size, ears, nose, throat (avoid pharyngeal examination if epiglottitis suspected)
- Observe feeding if aspiration is a concern
- Look for cutaneous haemangioma (50% association with subglottic haemangioma)

### Investigations

**Pre-endoscopy imaging:** AP and lateral neck films (steeple sign of croup, subglottic cysts/papillomata), chest X-ray, contrast swallow (suspected vascular ring or TOF), echocardiography (vascular compression, congenital heart disease), laryngeal ultrasound ([[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal cord palsy]]), CT/MRI (tracheal compression, vascular anomalies — "virtual bronchoscopy")

**Awake flexible endoscopy:** Ultra-thin endoscopes (<2 mm diameter) allow office/ward assessment even in neonates. Confirms supraglottic collapse of laryngomalacia. Useful for dynamic assessment (VCP). However, view beyond glottis is suboptimal, and a second pathology may be missed.

**Polysomnography:** Rarely used in stridor assessment; more relevant for pharyngeal obstruction and OSA.

### Role of Microlaryngoscopy and Bronchoscopy (MLB)

LTB (laryngotracheobronchoscopy) is the **gold standard** for assessing the stridulous child. It is a highly technical procedure requiring an experienced surgeon, anaesthetist and nursing team.

**Indications for MLB:**
- All children with atypical stridor features
- Severe or progressive stridor
- Failure to thrive
- History of intubation (suspect acquired pathology)
- Cutaneous haemangioma (suspect subglottic haemangioma)
- Diagnostic uncertainty after flexible endoscopy
- Pre-operative assessment before airway surgery
- Suspected second pathology alongside laryngomalacia (present in up to 20%)

**Technique:** Spontaneous respiration is preferred over paralysis to maintain muscle tone and detect dynamic conditions. Sevoflurane (non-irritant, rapid onset) is the usual volatile agent. A nasopharyngeal "prong" delivers oxygen and anaesthetic without intubation, providing an unaltered view of the airway. Hopkins rod telescopes provide superior image quality compared with operating microscope.

**Systematic assessment:** Four-shot minimum view — supraglottis, glottis, subglottis, trachea/carina/main bronchi. Video documentation of dynamic pathologies (laryngomalacia, tracheobronchomalacia, VCP) is recommended. Standardised data capture form for documentation.

**Dynamic assessment on recovery:** Withdraw bronchoscope to just posterior to epiglottis to observe vocal cord movement and arytenoid function during emergence from anaesthesia.

### Clinical Anatomy of Bronchus on Bronchoscopy

At the carina, the trachea divides into right and left main bronchi. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertically oriented than the left — hence aspirated foreign bodies lodge more commonly on the right side. The carina should be a sharp ridge. The right upper lobe bronchus originates just 1–2 cm beyond the carina (the most proximal lobar bronchus). The right main bronchus gives rise to upper, middle and lower lobe bronchi. The left main bronchus is longer and more horizontal, dividing into upper and lower lobe bronchi. Tracheomalacia is observed by withdrawing the bronchoscope from the area and observing collapse without positive airway pressure.

## PART D: Laryngomalacia — Features and Management

### Pathophysiology

Laryngomalacia is characterised by partial or complete collapse of the supraglottic structures on inspiration. The anatomical abnormalities include: an omega-shaped (long, curled) epiglottis; short, tightly tethered aryepiglottic folds; redundant mucosa and submucosa over the aryepiglottic folds; and a tall, narrow supraglottis with a deep interarytenoid cleft. There may also be neuromuscular immaturity with incoordination of arytenoid movements.

### Clinical Features

- Characteristic high-pitched, fluttering inspiratory stridor
- Usually presents at or shortly after birth
- Variable — most noticeable when active or upset, may disappear during sleep
- Severity increases during first 9 months then gradually diminishes
- Typically resolves by age 2 years
- Approximately 90% of cases are mild and self-limiting

### Diagnosis

Confirmed by awake flexible fibre-optic laryngoscopy in the outpatient setting — supraglottic collapse on inspiration is easily seen. For severe cases or atypical features, MLB under general anaesthesia is indicated with the child breathing spontaneously under light anaesthesia, laryngoscope beak in the vallecula (collapse is not seen under deep anaesthesia or if the laryngoscope tip enters the vestibule).

### Management

**Conservative (90% of cases):** Reassurance and observation with regular weight monitoring. A 4-week course of antireflux medication for those with GORD.

**Surgical — Aryepiglottoplasty/Supraglottoplasty (10%):**

Indications:
- Failure to thrive
- Severe respiratory obstruction with substantial sternal/intercostal recession
- Feeding difficulties with consequent weight loss
- Cor pulmonale (rare)
- Severe pectus excavatum from chronic obstruction

Technique: Larynx visualised in suspension with beak in vallecula. Using cup forceps and microscissors — each aryepiglottic fold is divided to release it from the edge of the epiglottis, and redundant mucosa/submucosal tissue is excised from over the arytenoids, together with cuneiform cartilages if necessary. In less severe cases, incision of the tight aryepiglottic fold alone may suffice. The "bridge" of mucosa between the arytenoids must be carefully preserved to prevent interarytenoid scarring. Bleeding is minimal — controlled with topical adrenaline (1:10,000) on neurosurgical patties.

Outcome: Stridor is usually improved immediately. Antibiotics and steroids are not routinely given. Complications are very rare. Persistent stridor and feeding difficulties after surgery suggest an underlying hypotonic neurological disorder.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Stridor is a symptom, not a diagnosis — the aim of evaluation is to determine both the site/cause (diagnosis) and the effect on the airway (severity), including impact on feeding, sleep, growth and exercise
> 2. Poiseuille's law dictates that 1 mm of mucosal swelling in a 4 mm neonatal airway reduces cross-sectional area by 75% — the paediatric airway is disproportionately affected by oedema compared with adults
> 3. Inspiratory stridor localises to extrathoracic obstruction (larynx), expiratory to intrathoracic (bronchi), and biphasic to subglottis/trachea — the phase of stridor is a key localising feature
> 4. Laryngomalacia is the most common congenital cause of stridor, typically self-limiting in ~90% of cases — it presents with inspiratory stridor worse with activity and feeding, usually resolving by age 2 years
> 5. A second co-existing airway pathology accompanies laryngomalacia in up to 20% of cases — MLB should be performed if there are atypical features, failure to thrive, history of intubation or cutaneous haemangioma
> 6. Laryngotracheobronchoscopy is the gold standard for assessment — it requires spontaneous respiration under light anaesthesia to detect dynamic conditions; a minimum four-shot view (supraglottis, glottis, subglottis, carina) should be documented
> 7. Aryepiglottoplasty/supraglottoplasty is indicated for the ~10% with severe laryngomalacia causing failure to thrive — the aryepiglottic folds are divided and redundant mucosa excised, with preservation of the interarytenoid mucosal bridge
> 8. Stridor present at birth suggests a fixed congenital narrowing (web, SGS, bilateral VCP) — gradual onset in first few weeks is more typical of laryngomalacia
> 9. Worsening stridor volume paradoxically may indicate improving obstruction — conversely, decreasing stridor may indicate worsening as airflow diminishes
> 10. Recurrent croup in an infant should raise suspicion of congenital mild subglottic stenosis — endoscopy is mandatory in persistent cases

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The severity of recession (subcostal, intercostal, suprasternal) is a more reliable indicator of airway compromise than the loudness of stridor — a quiet child with marked recession is more concerning than a noisy child with minimal recession
> - Cyanosis is a very late sign in paediatric airway obstruction — by the time it appears, respiratory collapse may be imminent; a pink child receiving supplemental oxygen may still have critical obstruction
> - Never attempt pharyngeal examination with a tongue depressor in a child with suspected epiglottitis — this may precipitate complete airway obstruction
> - The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical than the left — this is why inhaled foreign bodies lodge preferentially on the right side and must be systematically sought at bronchoscopy
> - Persistent stridor and feeding difficulty after aryepiglottoplasty should prompt investigation for an underlying neurological disorder — the supraglottoplasty addresses the anatomical component but not neuromuscular incoordination
