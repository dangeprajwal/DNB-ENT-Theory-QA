# Subglottic Stenosis in Children — Aetiology, Classification and Management

> [!question] Questions
> Subglottic stenosis — causes, classification (Cotton-Myer grading). Evaluation and surgical management of congenital SGS including Grade 4. Diagnosis in term and preterm neonates. Congenital anomalies of the larynx.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapters 28 (Stridor), 30 (Congenital Disorders of the Larynx, Trachea and Bronchi) and 31 (Acquired Laryngotracheal Stenosis)

---

## Introduction

Subglottic stenosis (SGS) is the third most common congenital anomaly of the larynx, after [[Stridor and Laryngomalacia|laryngomalacia]] and vocal cord paralysis. Its true incidence is difficult to determine because many affected neonates are intubated and then classified as having acquired stenosis — the reality is often a combined congenital plus acquired stenosis. Congenital SGS results from defective canalisation of the cricoid cartilage and/or conus elasticus. Acquired SGS is predominantly caused by endotracheal intubation trauma, particularly in premature neonates. The Cotton-Myer grading system classifies SGS into four grades of severity and guides surgical management. Treatment ranges from observation (Grade I) through laryngotracheal reconstruction (LTR) with cartilage grafting (Grades II-III) to partial cricotracheal resection (PCTR) for severe Grade III and Grade IV stenosis.

---

## PART A: Congenital Anomalies of the Larynx

### Classification by Region

**Supraglottic anomalies:**
- Laryngomalacia (most common congenital anomaly)
- Saccular cysts and laryngocoeles
- Vascular malformations (lymphatic, venous, arteriovenous)
- Bifid epiglottis (Pallister-Hall syndrome)

**Glottic anomalies:**
- Vocal cord paralysis (second most common) — unilateral (usually acquired, left RLN injury from cardiac surgery) or bilateral (congenital abductor paralysis — 58% eventually recover, 10% take >5 years)
- Anterior glottic webs — associated with 22q11 deletion; characterised by weak, high-pitched cry from birth and recurrent croup
- Laryngeal atresia — incompatible with life unless TOF present or EXIT procedure performed
- Cri-du-chat syndrome — diamond-shaped glottis on phonation, chromosome 5p deletion

**Subglottic anomalies:**
- Congenital subglottic stenosis (third most common)
- Subglottic haemangioma

## PART B: Aetiology of Subglottic Stenosis

### Congenital SGS

Results from defective canalisation of the cricoid cartilage and/or conus elasticus:
- **Cartilaginous stenosis:** Gross thickening of the anterior lamina of the abnormal cricoid
- **Soft tissue stenosis:** Small, elliptical, thickened cricoid with excessive submucosal soft tissue
- **Anterior glottic web with subglottic extension:** Anterior fusion of vocal cords with subglottic component, seen in 22q11 deletion syndromes

Milder degrees present as inspiratory or biphasic stridor as the child becomes more active, or as "recurrent croup" due to superimposed oedema from upper respiratory infections.

### Acquired SGS

**Causes (predominantly intubation-related):**
- Endotracheal intubation trauma — the most common cause; mucosal ulceration at the subglottis (the narrowest part of the paediatric airway) leads to cicatrisation and stenosis
- Risk factors: younger age, larger tube size, serial intubation, longer duration of intubation, Down syndrome (may have element of congenital SGS as predisposing factor)
- Premature neonates may tolerate prolonged intubation better than older children due to softer, more flexible larynx
- No clear consensus on maximum safe intubation duration — premature babies may be intubated for several weeks; [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] should normally be considered in older children after 2-3 weeks

**Other acquired causes:** Surgical trauma (e.g. laser), gastro-oesophageal reflux laryngitis, external laryngeal trauma, high tracheostomy (damage to cricoid)

### Diagnosis in Term vs Preterm Neonates

In **term neonates**, SGS presents with stridor (inspiratory or biphasic) from birth or shortly after. A fixed congenital narrowing should be suspected if stridor is present with the first breath. History of recurrent croup is a key feature of milder congenital SGS.

In **preterm neonates**, diagnosis is complicated by the frequency of endotracheal intubation. Post-extubation stridor, failed extubation attempts, or the need for progressively smaller endotracheal tubes all suggest developing SGS. The true incidence of purely congenital SGS in this group is underestimated because intubation is almost universal and any subsequent stenosis is classified as acquired.

## PART C: Cotton-Myer Classification

The Myer-Cotton grading system is the most widely used classification for paediatric laryngotracheal stenosis. It is based on the percentage of obstruction of the subglottic lumen, assessed by passing endotracheal tubes through the stenosis at MLB.

| Grade | Percentage Obstruction |
|---|---|
| Grade I | 0-50% |
| Grade II | 51-70% |
| Grade III | 71-99% |
| Grade IV | 100% (no detectable lumen) |

### Assessment at MLB

The exact location of the stenosis relative to the vocal cords, tracheostome and carina is measured. The number of normal tracheal rings above the tracheostome is counted. Endotracheal tubes are passed through to size the stricture and determine the grade. The degree, maturity and mucosal quality of the stenosis are more accurately assessed at endoscopy than by cross-sectional imaging.

## PART D: Surgical Management

### Principles

**Grade I:** Usually requires no surgical intervention. Congenital stenosis can be expected to enlarge with growth.

**Cartilaginous stenosis:** Represents a strict contraindication to dilatation or laser resection — any endoscopic treatment is liable to worsen the condition, and attempted dilatation is inevitably ineffective as the thickened cricoid ring cannot be expanded.

**Severely compromised airway:** Tracheostomy is needed as the first step. In specialist centres, this may be avoided by single-stage airway reconstruction with post-operative endotracheal tube stenting for 5-7 days.

### Surgical Options

#### Anterior Cricoid Split
Described by Cotton and Seid. Vertical midline incision through the anterior cricoid and first two tracheal rings, allowing the airway to expand. Originally developed for extubation failure due to subglottic oedema in premature neonates. The child is stented with an endotracheal tube for 5-7 days post-operatively.

#### Laryngotracheal Reconstruction (LTR)
Devised primarily by Cotton in the early 1980s. Involves augmentation of the laryngotracheal complex by anterior and/or posterior midline incision of the cricoid with insertion of costal cartilage grafts to expand the airway.

| SGS Grade | LTR Approach |
|---|---|
| Grade II | Anterior cartilage graft +/- posterior cricoid split |
| Mild Grade III | Anterior graft + posterior cricoid split +/- posterior graft |
| Severe Grade III | Both anterior and posterior grafts |
| Grade IV | Anterior + posterior grafts with prolonged stenting |

**Single-stage LTR:** Post-operative endotracheal tube stent for 5-7 days, avoiding tracheostomy. Suitable for selected cases in specialist centres.

**Double-stage LTR:** Tracheostomy remains in situ; stented with a Montgomery T-tube or similar for 4-6 weeks. Deferred LTR at age 1-2 years when the larynx is larger allows more precise vocal cord dissection.

**Congenital cartilaginous SGS:** LTR may be combined with submucosal resection of cartilage to "core out" the thickened anterior cricoid ring. Stenting is essential.

#### Partial Cricotracheal Resection (PCTR)
Introduced into paediatric practice by Monnier in the early 1990s. Involves complete resection of the stenotic segment with end-to-end anastomosis of the tracheal stump to the thyroid cartilage. Particularly suited for severe Grade III and Grade IV stenosis.

For PCTR, the Cotton-Myer grading is not a predictor of success or failure, because the stenotic segment is completely resected.

### Management of Grade IV SGS

Grade IV stenosis demands the most aggressive approach:
- **LTR:** Anterior and posterior cartilage grafts with prolonged stenting
- **PCTR:** Complete resection of the stenotic segment — an alternative with much to commend it
- LTR remains the last surgical option after failed PCTR if it is impossible to resect further tracheal segments
- Tracheostomy is required as the initial step in virtually all Grade IV cases

### Outcomes

| Procedure | Grade I-II Success | Grade III-IV Success |
|---|---|---|
| LTR | >90% | 80-90% |
| PCTR (primary) | N/A | 98% decannulation |
| PCTR (salvage) | N/A | 94% decannulation |

The most experienced centres report PCTR decannulation rates of 98% for primary surgery and 94% for salvage surgery after failed previous airway reconstruction.

### Additional Considerations

**Subglottic haemangioma:** Previously required tracheostomy in 50-70% of cases. Treatment revolutionised by propranolol (1-3 mg/kg/day for minimum 12 months). Propranolol is now first-line treatment. Submucous surgical excision remains an alternative for non-circumferential lesions not involving the vocal cords.

**Anterior glottic web with subglottic extension:** May require LTR with anterior cartilage grafting in severe cases. Endoscopic keel placement can prevent recurrence after web division. In infants with a small larynx and significant SGS component, tracheostomy followed by deferred LTR at age 1-2 years is preferred.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Congenital SGS is the third most common congenital anomaly of the larynx — it results from defective canalisation of the cricoid cartilage (cartilaginous type) or excessive submucosal soft tissue (soft tissue type)
> 2. The Cotton-Myer grading system classifies SGS by percentage obstruction: Grade I (0-50%), Grade II (51-70%), Grade III (71-99%) and Grade IV (100%) — it predicts surgical outcome for LTR but not for PCTR
> 3. Endotracheal intubation trauma is the most common cause of acquired SGS — risk factors include young age, large tube, serial intubation, prolonged duration and Down syndrome
> 4. Cartilaginous congenital SGS is a strict contraindication to dilatation or laser — the thickened cricoid ring cannot be expanded endoscopically and treatment will worsen the condition
> 5. LTR involves augmentation with costal cartilage grafts (anterior +/- posterior) — success rates exceed 90% for Grades I-II and are 80-90% for Grades III-IV in experienced centres
> 6. PCTR (partial cricotracheal resection) involves complete excision of the stenotic segment with end-to-end anastomosis — decannulation rates of 98% for primary and 94% for salvage surgery make it the procedure of choice for severe Grade III and Grade IV SGS
> 7. Grade IV SGS requires both anterior and posterior grafts with prolonged stenting (LTR) or PCTR — virtually all patients need tracheostomy as the initial step
> 8. Premature neonates can tolerate longer periods of intubation due to softer laryngeal cartilage — tracheostomy should be considered after 2-3 weeks in older children
> 9. Recurrent croup in infancy should raise suspicion of congenital mild SGS — endoscopy is mandatory in persistent cases
> 10. Propranolol has revolutionised treatment of subglottic haemangioma — it is now first-line therapy at 1-3 mg/kg/day for minimum 12 months, with monitoring for hypoglycaemia, hypotension and bradycardia


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The distinction between congenital and acquired SGS is often artificial in the premature neonate — many have a combined congenital plus acquired stenosis, as pre-existing congenital narrowing predisposes to intubation injury
> - A child presenting with "recurrent croup" should always be investigated for underlying SGS — the narrowed subglottis becomes symptomatic with even minor mucosal swelling from viral upper respiratory infections
> - PCTR has superior results to LTR for severe stenosis because the pathological segment is completely removed rather than expanded — however, it is technically more demanding and carries risk if further tracheal resection is needed
> - In congenital cartilaginous SGS, never attempt balloon dilatation or laser treatment — the rigid thickened cricoid will not yield and endoscopic intervention invariably worsens the stenosis
> - Single-stage LTR avoiding tracheostomy is only appropriate in specialist centres with experienced teams — the child remains intubated as a stent for 5-7 days and requires intensive care monitoring
