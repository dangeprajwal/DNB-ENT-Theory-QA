# Pain in Malignancy

## Q64. Pain in malignancy — pathophysiology, evaluation, management (2019 Dec)

*Discuss the pathophysiology, evaluation and management of pain in malignancy.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapter 21: Palliative Care for Head and Neck Cancer (Mayland & Ellershaw); Volume 1, Chapter 30: Perioperative Care

---

### Introduction

Pain is one of the most common and distressing symptoms in head and neck cancer patients, with studies estimating the prevalence to be between 62% and 99%. In a retrospective review of 93 terminal head and neck cancer patients, patients had a mean number of 4.7 symptoms in the last 6 months of life, with pain consistently being the most frequently reported. The effective management of cancer pain requires a thorough understanding of its pathophysiology, systematic evaluation, and a structured approach to treatment based on the WHO analgesic ladder.

---

### Part A: Pathophysiology of Cancer Pain

Cancer pain is complex and multifactorial. It can be sub-divided into two main categories:

**1. Nociceptive Pain**
- Arises due to ongoing activation of primary afferent nerves by noxious stimuli (nociceptors)
- Results from direct tissue damage by the tumour
- Mechanisms include:
  - Local compression of surrounding structures
  - Invasion into bone, muscle, or soft tissue
  - Inflammation and release of chemical mediators (prostaglandins, bradykinin, substance P)
  - Distension of hollow viscera or stretching of organ capsules
- Typically described as aching, throbbing, or pressure-like
- Generally well-localized and responsive to opioid analgesics

**2. Neuropathic Pain**
- Due to dysfunction or disease affecting the peripheral and/or central nervous system
- Mechanisms include:
  - Direct tumour invasion or compression of neural structures (cranial nerves, cervical plexus, brachial plexus)
  - Treatment-related nerve injury (post-surgical, post-radiation fibrosis)
  - Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy
- Typically described as burning, shooting, stabbing, or electric shock-like
- Often associated with allodynia (pain from non-painful stimuli) and hyperalgesia
- Less responsive to conventional analgesics alone; requires adjuvant agents

**3. Mixed Pain**
Within clinical practice, there is often a mixture of both nociceptive and neuropathic pain which can arise either due to the cancer itself (local compression or invasion) or related to previous treatments. This mixed picture is particularly common in head and neck cancer due to the complex anatomy and the proximity of tumour to multiple cranial nerves and neurovascular structures.

**4. Pain Related to Specific Causes**
- **Tumour-related:** Direct invasion of bone (mandible, skull base), perineural spread along cranial nerves (especially V, VII, IX, X, XII), mucosal ulceration, infection of tumour
- **Treatment-related:** Post-surgical pain, radiation-induced mucositis, osteoradionecrosis, radiation fibrosis syndrome, chemotherapy-induced neuropathy
- **Associated conditions:** Muscle spasm (trismus), infection, lymphoedema

### Part B: Evaluation of Cancer Pain

Systematic evaluation of pain is essential for effective management:

**1. Pain History**
- Location, onset, duration, character (sharp, dull, burning, shooting)
- Severity — use validated pain assessment tools (Visual Analogue Scale, Numerical Rating Scale 0-10)
- Aggravating and relieving factors
- Temporal pattern — constant vs. intermittent vs. breakthrough
- Impact on daily activities, sleep, mood, and functional status
- Current and previous analgesic use and response
- Side effects of current pain medications

**2. Physical Examination**
- Thorough head and neck examination to identify local tumour extent
- Neurological assessment — cranial nerve function, sensory changes, motor weakness
- Assessment for trismus, lymphoedema, and infection
- Examination of referred pain sites (ear, shoulder, scalp)

**3. Psychological Assessment**
- Depression screening — particularly asking about anhedonia (loss of interest or pleasure in doing things) and low mood
- Depression is common in head and neck cancer patients and can independently predict poorer quality of life, requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment
- Head and neck cancer patients have a disproportionately higher suicide risk compared with the cancer population as a whole
- Assessment of anxiety, coping mechanisms, social support networks

**4. Classification of Pain Type**
- Determine whether nociceptive, neuropathic, or mixed
- Identify whether cancer-related, treatment-related, or concurrent pathology
- Assess for breakthrough pain — defined as 'a transient exacerbation of pain that occurs either spontaneously, or in relation to a specific predictable or unpredictable trigger, despite relatively stable and adequately controlled background pain' (Association of Palliative Medicine, UK)

**5. Investigations**
- Imaging (CT, MRI, [[PET CT Nuclear Medicine Scans|PET-CT]]) to evaluate tumour extent and identify cause of pain
- Bone scan if bony metastasis suspected
- Blood tests including renal function (critical for opioid metabolism and dosing)

### Part C: Management — The WHO Analgesic Ladder

The principles of effective pain management are governed using the WHO pain ladder, which remains a useful, practical approach to managing cancer pain. This step-wise approach advocates:

| Step | Pain Level | Analgesics | Examples |
|------|-----------|------------|----------|
| Step 1 | Mild | Non-opioid +/- adjuvant | Paracetamol, NSAIDs |
| Step 2 | Mild-moderate | Weak opioid + non-opioid +/- adjuvant | Codeine, tramadol |
| Step 3 | Moderate-severe | Strong opioid + non-opioid +/- adjuvant | Morphine, oxycodone |

**Key Principles:**
- Start with non-opioids first-line (e.g. regular paracetamol)
- Then add a weak opioid such as codeine if pain persists or increases
- Then substitute the weak opioid for a strong opioid
- Generally, the opioid of choice for moderate to severe pain remains morphine
- Drugs should be given orally as far as possible
- Given at regular intervals to achieve adequate pain relief with minimal side effects
- Titrated according to pain intensity using regularly measured pain severity scores

### Part D: Specific Pain Management Strategies

**1. Opioid-Induced Neurotoxicity**
The concept of opioid-induced neurotoxicity has become increasingly recognized due to accumulation of opioid metabolites. Clinically, the patient presents with:
- Myoclonus
- Peripheral shadows
- Hallucinations
- Confusion and drowsiness

Management considerations:
- Can the current dose of opioid be reduced (if pain is controlled)?
- Is the patient's renal function stable (as metabolites are more likely to accumulate if opioid is renally excreted)?
- Does the patient require an opioid switch?
- If switching is needed, change to an alternative such as oxycodone or hydromorphone
- If renal impairment is present, use opioids not excreted by the kidneys, e.g. alfentanil

**2. Breakthrough Pain Management**
- A short-acting preparation of morphine is given (e.g. oramorph or oxynorm)
- Newer formulations of strong opioids (fentanyl) can be administered via sublingual, buccal, and intranasal routes
- These have equal efficacy compared with traditional oral medications but quicker onset of action (though more expensive)
- Reserved for pain related to a particular event (e.g. movement) or pain that is sudden in onset and short-lived
- Prior to use, patients should be on at least 60 mg of oral morphine or equivalent

**3. Neuropathic Pain Management**
In addition to using the WHO ladder:
- First-line: Anti-convulsant (e.g. gabapentin) OR anti-depressant (amitriptyline)
- If evidence of nerve compression: trial of corticosteroids (dexamethasone)
- Other therapeutic interventions: methadone, ketamine, and potentially interventional pain procedures
- Involvement with specialist palliative care teams is beneficial for this advice

**4. Route of Administration — Challenges in Head and Neck Cancer**
Particular challenges arise as often the oral route cannot be used:

| Route | Details |
|-------|---------|
| Oral | First choice when available |
| Via gastrostomy | If PEG/RIG in situ; sustained release preparations must NOT be crushed |
| Modified-release granules | Zomorph — can be given via gastrostomy |
| Modified-release suspension | MST Continus — safe via gastrostomy |
| Transdermal patch | Fentanyl or buprenorphine; use only when pain is stable |
| Subcutaneous infusion (CSCI) | Continuous infusion; can be used earlier in disease trajectory for HN patients |
| Sublingual/buccal/intranasal | Rapid-onset fentanyl for breakthrough pain |

**Key Messages for Transdermal Patches:**
- Should only be used when pain control has been established and is generally stable
- Changed every 72 hours — do not have as much flexibility in dose titration
- Some buprenorphine patches (e.g. BuTrans) have a maximum dose range (up to 20 mcg, equivalent to just under 50 mg oral morphine)
- Not recommended to commence in dying patients due to difficulty with titration

**5. Subcutaneous Continuous Infusion (CSCI)**
- Often associated with care of a dying patient but can be used earlier in the disease trajectory for head and neck patients
- Can serve as a temporary measure until a gastrostomy is inserted
- Allows continuous delivery of opioids and other medications

### Part E: Holistic and Palliative Aspects of Pain in Malignancy

**1. Symptom Prevalence in Advanced Head and Neck Cancer**

| Symptom | Prevalence Range |
|---------|-----------------|
| Pain | 62-99% |
| Dysphagia | 45-90% |
| Weight loss/anorexia | 43-98% |
| Feeding difficulties | 32-89% |
| Breathing/airway difficulties | 22-52% |
| Bleeding | 9-47% |
| Fatigue/weakness | 39% |

**2. Palliative Care Integration**
- The WHO defines palliative care as 'total active care of patients whose disease is not responsive to curative treatment'
- Should encompass physical, psychological, social, and spiritual aspects of care
- Move palliative care 'upstream' in the disease trajectory and integrate with curative and rehabilitative therapies
- Approximately 20% of head and neck cancer patients qualify for palliative care input at initial diagnosis, with average survival of 5 months

**Indications for palliative care referral:**
- Unresectable locoregional disease and/or distant metastasis at initial presentation
- Recurrent unresectable locoregional disease and/or distant metastasis after treatment
- Patients unable to have anticancer treatments due to disease stage, comorbidity, functional status, and/or individual preference

**3. Emergency Palliative Situations**
- **Terminal haemorrhage:** Rupture of carotid artery system occurs in an estimated 3-5% of patients who have undergone major head and neck resections. Management includes dark towels, staying with the patient, and pre-emptive midazolam 10 mg deep intramuscular injection. Risk factors include tumour proximity to carotid artery, previous radical [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] and [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], coagulopathy, age >50 years, 10-15% loss of body weight
- **Airway obstruction:** Due to retention of secretions or tumour encroachment. Palliative options include [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]], tumour debulking, dexamethasone (8-16 mg subcutaneous) for stridor, and pre-emptive midazolam for terminal events

**4. Respiratory Tract Secretion Management**
- Three main anti-secretory drugs: glycopyrronium, hyoscine hydrobromide, hyoscine butylbromide
- Glycopyrronium may be preferred due to reduced likelihood of agitation and sedation
- Balance between reducing secretions and not making them too tenacious
- Transdermal hyoscine hydrobromide (Scopoderm) patches if oral/gastrostomy route unavailable

**5. Depression and Psychological Care**
- Depression is common and independently predicts poorer quality of life
- Brief screening: ask about anhedonia and low mood
- SSRIs generally first-line (but increase risk of GI bleeding — particular consideration in head and neck cancer)
- Mirtazapine — associated with weight gain and sedation, which may be acceptable side effects
- Advance care planning is particularly pertinent for head and neck cancer patients while they are able to communicate

**6. Ethical Principles in End-of-Life Care**
- Respect for autonomy — individual's right to decide
- Beneficence — duty to provide benefit
- Non-maleficence — duty to avoid harm
- Justice — fair use and distribution of resources
- Key ethical issues include decisions about nutrition and hydration, especially approaching end of life

**7. Priorities for Care of the Dying (UK National Guidance)**
1. Possibility of dying is recognized and communicated clearly
2. Sensitive communication between staff, dying person, and those important to them
3. Dying person and important others are involved in decisions about treatment and care
4. Needs of families are actively explored, respected, and met
5. Individual plan of care including food and drink, symptom control, and psychological, social and spiritual support is agreed, coordinated and delivered with compassion

#> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Pain prevalence in head and neck cancer ranges from 62-99%, making it the most common symptom in advanced disease
> 2. Cancer pain is classified as nociceptive (tissue damage activating nociceptors) or neuropathic (dysfunction of peripheral/central nervous system), with mixed pain being common in head and neck cancer
> 3. The WHO analgesic ladder provides a stepwise approach: non-opioids → weak opioids → strong opioids, with morphine remaining the strong opioid of choice
> 4. Breakthrough pain requires short-acting opioid preparations; patients should be on at least 60 mg oral morphine equivalent before using rapid-onset fentanyl formulations
> 5. Neuropathic pain management requires adjuvant agents: first-line gabapentin or amitriptyline; dexamethasone for nerve compression; methadone or ketamine for refractory cases
> 6. Opioid-induced neurotoxicity (myoclonus, hallucinations, confusion) results from metabolite accumulation — managed by dose reduction, renal function assessment, or opioid switching
> 7. Route of administration is a particular challenge in head and neck cancer; transdermal patches should only be used when pain is stable; CSCI can be used earlier in disease trajectory
> 8. Depression screening (anhedonia, low mood) is essential as head and neck cancer patients have disproportionately higher suicide risk compared with other cancers
> 9. Terminal haemorrhage occurs in 3-5% of patients post major head and neck resections — managed with dark towels, midazolam 10 mg IM, and staying with the patient
> 10. Palliative care should be integrated 'upstream' in the disease trajectory, with approximately 20% of head and neck cancer patients qualifying for palliative care at initial diagnosis

#> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Sustained release opioid preparations must NEVER be crushed when given via gastrostomy tube — use modified-release granules (Zomorph) or suspension (MST Continus) instead
> - In renal impairment, switch to opioids not excreted by the kidneys (e.g. alfentanil) to prevent neurotoxicity from metabolite accumulation
> - SSRIs, while first-line for depression, increase GI bleeding risk — a particular concern in head and neck cancer patients who may also be on NSAIDs or have tumour-related bleeding risk
> - Pre-emptive prescription of midazolam should be arranged for all patients at risk of terminal haemorrhage, even for those at home — coordinate with community healthcare team and ambulance service
> - BuTrans patches have a maximum equivalent of approximately 50 mg oral morphine — ensure this ceiling dose is recognized when using buprenorphine patches for cancer pain
> - Advance care planning discussions should be initiated while head and neck cancer patients can still communicate, as progressive disease often impairs speech capacity
