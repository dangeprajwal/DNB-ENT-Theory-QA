# Peritonsillar Abscess (Quinsy)

> [!question] Questions
> Define quinsy. Discuss the clinical features, management and complications of peritonsillar abscess.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 38 (Diseases of Tonsils, [[Tonsillectomy|Tonsillectomy]] and Tonsillotomy) and Volume 3, Chapter 51 (Pharyngitis)

---

## Introduction

A peritonsillar abscess (PTA), commonly known as quinsy, is a collection of pus between the fibrous capsule of the tonsil — usually at the upper pole — and the superior constrictor muscles of the pharynx. It is the most common suppurative complication of GABHS pharyngitis. Data from England showed an 18% increase in PTA incidence over 10 years to 2012, with 7,589 episodes in 2009–2010, possibly reflecting fewer tonsillectomies being performed during that period. The majority of abscesses occur in young adults between 20 and 39 years of age, which is surprising given that the incidence of tonsillitis peaks in childhood.

---

## PART A: Aetiology and Pathogenesis

### Risk Factors

- Age (20–39 years peak)
- Male gender
- Smoking
- Poor dental hygiene
- Immunodeficiency

### Pathogenesis — Two Proposed Mechanisms

**1. Complication of acute tonsillitis:** The most accepted theory, though up to 68% of patients with PTA had no preceding history of tonsillitis, and peak age does not correlate with the 5–15 year age group affected by acute tonsillitis.

**2. Weber gland blockage:** Weber's glands are minor salivary glands in the supratonsillar space. Blockage of these glands or their ducts from poor dental hygiene or localized infection predisposes to abscess formation. Reduction of the innate immunity provided by saliva may be a contributing factor.

### Bacteriology

- GABHS and anaerobes (Fusobacterium necrophorum, Streptococcus milleri) are the predominant causative organisms
- Two pathogenically different subtypes have been identified:
  - **Type 1:** Pure culture of a single organism, most often GABHS
  - **Type 2:** Heavy polymicrobial growth with facultative and obligate anaerobes — often more severe due to synergistic microbial interactions

---

## PART B: Clinical Features

### Symptoms

- Progressive, usually unilateral, sore throat over 3–4 days
- Odynophagia progressing from solids to liquids
- Drooling of saliva
- Trismus (limited mouth opening — virtually pathognomonic)
- Ipsilateral otalgia (referred pain)
- Headache
- Fever and lethargy
- 'Plummy' (muffled) voice secondary to oropharyngeal swelling

### Examination Findings

- Tonsil displaced medially by hyperaemic, bulging mucosa of the anterior pillar
- Tender and enlarged jugulodigastric lymph nodes
- Uvula may be oedematous and deviated to the opposite side
- Bilateral quinsies have been reported, including in association with infectious mononucleosis

---

## PART C: Investigations

- **Needle aspiration** — often curative and provides bacteriology in recurrent or non-responsive cases; differentiates cellulitis from PTA
- Routine bacteriology specimens are not required — reserve for patients with high likelihood of resistant organisms (diabetics, immunocompromised, recurrent PTA)
- **Infectious mononucleosis screening** — reserve for cases with high clinical suspicion (teenagers, young adults, suggestive systemic features); routine screening in all PTA patients is not supported
- **Transoral ultrasound** — effective non-invasive method to differentiate pus from cellulitis (sensitivity 92.3%, specificity 62.3%)
- **CT scan** — when complications are suspected (parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal or mediastinal spread)
- **MRI/MR angiography** — for suspected vascular complications

---

## PART D: Management

### Surgical Drainage

Surgical drainage is the mainstay of treatment for PTA.

**1. Needle aspiration (most common in UK):**
- Relatively pain-free
- Significantly contributes to pain relief compared with no aspiration
- No significant complications
- Success rate: 94%
- Recurrence rate: 10–15%

**2. Incision and drainage (quinsy knife):**
- Used when needle aspiration has failed
- More painful than needle aspiration
- Less painful if carried out with proper local anaesthetic infiltration rather than spray alone
- Recurrence rate: approximately 10% (comparable to needle aspiration)

Since recurrence rates are comparable, clinician experience and patient choice should guide the decision.

### Medical Management

- **Antibiotics:** Benzyl penicillin combined with metronidazole is preferred (polymicrobial nature). For penicillin allergy, use erythromycin or clarithromycin.
- **Corticosteroids:** A single dose of intravenous steroid in addition to antibiotics significantly reduces throat pain, hospital stay, fever and trismus
- **Supportive care:** Adequate analgesia, IV fluid resuscitation if unable to maintain oral intake

### Inpatient vs. Outpatient Management

- Inpatient admission is routine practice in the UK
- Outpatient management is safe if systems exist for recognizing airway compromise/dehydration, short-term monitoring and easy access for admission if the condition worsens

### Role of Tonsillectomy

**Abscess tonsillectomy (quinsy tonsillectomy):**
- Not popular in the UK but practised in other countries with good results and minimal morbidity
- Advantage: avoids recurrence and the need for delayed elective tonsillectomy; the dissection plane is already created by the pus
- Post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage rates are comparable to elective tonsillectomy
- Concern: trismus and associated intubation difficulty increase the risk of GA
- In paediatric patients requiring GA for drainage, abscess tonsillectomy may be the treatment of choice

**Interval tonsillectomy:**
- Recommended after recurrent PTA
- Not recommended after a single episode alone — after tonsil-conserving treatment approximately 11% recur by 5 years and 22% by 17–35 years
- Recurrence is rare after age 40 — elective tonsillectomy is not warranted

## PART E: Complications

### Local Complications

- **Parapharyngeal abscess** — spread of infection to the [[Parapharyngeal Space|parapharyngeal space]]; presents with severe trismus and airway compromise; diagnosis confirmed by ultrasound or CT
- **[[Retropharyngeal Abscess|Retropharyngeal abscess]]** — spread posteriorly; confirmed by CT; treatment with high-dose IV antibiotics and surgical drainage under GA
- **[[Deep Neck Space Infections|Deep neck space infection]]s and mediastinitis** — described in 1.8% of PTA cases; mediastinitis has significant mortality (23%) even with aggressive management
- **Necrotizing fasciitis** — rare but life-threatening; requires broad-spectrum antibiotics, abscess tonsillectomy and debridement
- **Lemierre's syndrome** — septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein with metastatic abscesses; caused by Fusobacterium; treatment is 6 weeks of antibiotics (penicillin + metronidazole or co-amoxiclav); anticoagulation may be considered if there is spreading thrombophlebitis

### Systemic Complications

- Septicaemia
- Airway obstruction (rare)

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. A peritonsillar abscess is a collection of pus between the tonsil capsule and the superior constrictor — most commonly at the upper pole
> 2. Trismus with progressive unilateral sore throat, drooling and 'plummy' voice is virtually pathognomonic of PTA
> 3. Two pathogenic mechanisms: complication of acute tonsillitis and Weber gland blockage — up to 68% have no preceding tonsillitis
> 4. GABHS and anaerobes (Fusobacterium necrophorum, Streptococcus milleri) are the predominant organisms
> 5. Needle aspiration is the management of choice in the UK — success rate 94%, recurrence 10–15%

---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Always screen for infectious mononucleosis when clinical suspicion is high (teenagers, young adults, bilateral PTA) — PTA in IM is not related to steroids
> - The Weber gland theory explains why PTA peaks at 20–39 years rather than in childhood when tonsillitis is most common
> - Fibrosis from previous PTA makes interval tonsillectomy difficult — abscess tonsillectomy, when feasible, takes advantage of the pre-formed dissection plane
> - If initial mediastinal drainage does not lead to resolution, reoperation including contralateral thoracotomy may be required
> - Lemierre's syndrome should be suspected in any patient with severe neck pain and septicaemia following oropharyngeal infection — significant mortality is associated with this condition
