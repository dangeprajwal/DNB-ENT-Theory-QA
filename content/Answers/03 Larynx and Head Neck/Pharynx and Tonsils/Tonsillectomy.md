# Tonsillectomy — Indications, Techniques and Complications

**Question:** *Discuss the indications of tonsillectomy in light of current evidence. Describe tonsillectomy techniques with their complication rates. How would you manage secondary haemorrhage after tonsillectomy? Describe the anatomy and clinical significance of the tonsillar bed.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 38 (Diseases of Tonsils, Tonsillectomy and Tonsillotomy) and Volume 3, Chapter 51 (Pharyngitis)

---

## Introduction

Paediatric tonsillectomy is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures. In the UK, approximately 49,000 tonsillectomies were performed in 2008–2009, of which 27,400 were in children. The proportion performed for obstruction rather than infection has been rising year-on-year, likely reflecting more stringent criteria for surgery for infections from better trial evidence, combined with increasing awareness of [[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|obstructive sleep apnoea]] and its consequences in children.

## PART A: Anatomy of the Tonsillar Bed

### Structure

The palatine tonsils are paired structures of lymphoid tissue located in the tonsillar fossa between the anterior tonsillar pillar (palatoglossus muscle) and the posterior tonsillar pillar (palatopharyngeus muscle). Histologically, tonsils consist of lymphocyte aggregates arranged in a follicular manner embedded in connective tissue stroma. The epithelial lining is stratified squamous, which invaginates into the parenchyma forming crypts.

### Waldeyer's Ring

The palatine tonsils, together with the adenoids, lingual tonsils, tubal tonsils and diffuse aggregates of pharyngeal submucosal lymphoid tissue, constitute Waldeyer's ring.

### Immunological Function

- Both T- and B-lymphocytes are present; B-lymphocytes predominate
- Tonsils serve both cell-mediated and humoral immune functions
- Tonsils have no afferent lymphatics
- B-cells synthesize specific antibodies (IgG and IgA) on antigen exposure
- There is no evidence that tonsillectomy results in impaired immunity

### Clinical Significance of the Tonsillar Bed

- The tonsillar bed is formed by the superior constrictor muscle of the pharynx
- The glossopharyngeal nerve lies posterior to the bed — dissection beyond the pharyngeal musculature risks injury
- The carotid sheath lies deep to the bed — overly aggressive dissection can cause catastrophic haemorrhage
- The paratonsillar (external maxillary) vein lies at the lower pole — a common source of primary haemorrhage
- The tonsillar branch of the facial artery is the main arterial supply to the tonsil

### Microbiology

- Most common surface organism: Group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus (GABHS)
- GABHS is found on culture in nearly 40% of asymptomatic individuals
- In recurrent tonsillitis: Haemophilus influenzae and S. aureus (including MRSA) predominate, with mixed flora being common
- Latent viruses (EBV, adenoviruses, herpes simplex) may sensitize pathogenic bacteria

## PART B: Indications for Tonsillectomy

### Recurrent Tonsillitis — Paradise Criteria (SIGN Guidelines)

All of the following must be met:

- Sore throats are due to tonsillitis
- The episodes are disabling and prevent normal functioning
- **Seven or more** well-documented, clinically significant, adequately treated sore throats in the preceding year, OR
- **Five or more** such episodes in each of the preceding 2 years, OR
- **Three or more** such episodes in each of the preceding 3 years

### Evidence Base

- Paradise RCT (1984): tonsillectomy efficacious for 2 years in severely affected children, sometimes for a third year
- NESSTAC trial: significant improvements in sore throat episodes, consultations, quality of life and health costs vs. medical management
- Trials in children with milder symptoms (not meeting Paradise criteria) failed to show significant advantage over watchful waiting
- Scottish tonsillectomy audit: 97% patient satisfaction rate at 1 year
- Cochrane review (adults): limited evidence; modest benefit of 1.5 fewer episodes in 6 months

### Obstructive Indications

Adenotonsillectomy is the treatment of choice for otherwise healthy children with obstructive sleep apnoea, with improvement in 90% of cases including behaviour, growth and development.

### Other Indications

- [[Peritonsillar Abscess|Peritonsillar abscess]] (quinsy) — interval tonsillectomy after recurrent episodes
- Suspected tonsillar neoplasia (asymmetric tonsil with irregular surface)
- Tonsillitis and psoriasis (limited evidence)
- Spontaneous tonsillar bleeding unresponsive to conservative measures

## PART C: Tonsillectomy Techniques

### Cold Steel Dissection (Gold Standard)

The tonsil is pulled medially, mucosa over the tonsil capsule is incised, and dissection continues in the plane of loose areolar tissue between the tonsil and pharyngeal muscles. The tonsil is excised completely. Haemostasis is achieved with ties or diathermy as required. Cold steel dissection with ties has the lowest post-operative haemorrhage rates.

### Technique Comparison — NPTA Data

| Technique | Primary Bleed | Secondary Bleed |
|---|---|---|
| Cold steel + ties/packs | 0.8% | 1.0% |
| Cold steel + bipolar | 0.5% | 2.3% |
| Bipolar diathermy forceps | 0.4% | 4.3% |
| Monopolar diathermy | 1.1% | 5.5% |
| Coblation | 1.0% | 3.6% |

### Key NPTA Recommendations

- Post-operative bleeding is more frequent with diathermy than cold steel alone
- Monopolar dissection is associated with more pain and has little to recommend it
- All trainee surgeons should become competent in cold steel dissection and ties before learning other techniques
- Surgeons wishing to start new techniques (e.g. Coblation) should undergo appropriate training

### Tonsillotomy

Tonsillotomy involves removing part of the tonsil lymphoid tissue, leaving the capsule intact. Techniques include microdebrider, laser, or Coblation. In Scandinavia, tonsillotomy has become the most common operation for obstructive symptoms. Recovery-related outcomes (pain-free days, secondary haemorrhage rate) for intracapsular tonsillotomy are better than for total tonsillectomy in children with obstructive symptoms.

## PART D: Complications and Management

### Pain

- Post-tonsillectomy sore throat is normal for at least 1 week
- Average return to school or work: 1–2 weeks

### Peri-operative Complications

- Temporomandibular joint dysfunction (mouth opened too widely)
- Glossopharyngeal nerve injury (dissection beyond pharyngeal muscles)
- Carotid sheath injury (rare but catastrophic)
- Grisel syndrome (non-traumatic atlantoaxial subluxation)

### Haemorrhage

**Primary haemorrhage** (within 24 hours): NPTA readmission rate for all bleeding was 4.57%, with 1.44% requiring return to theatre.

**Secondary haemorrhage** (after 24 hours, up to 2 weeks): Most likely due to infection with Streptococcus in the granulating tonsil bed.

### Management of Secondary Haemorrhage

1. **Assess severity** — resuscitate with IV fluids if needed, group and save blood
2. **Antibiotics** — the mainstay; bleeding usually settles with antibiotic therapy alone (penicillin-based or cephalosporin)
3. **Hydrogen peroxide gargles** — may help dislodge clots and promote haemostasis
4. **Severe or persistent bleeding** — return to theatre for examination under GA, evacuation of clot, identification and control of bleeding point with diathermy or ligation
5. **Surgery is rarely needed** for secondary haemorrhage

### Peri-operative Management

- **Analgesia:** Paracetamol is the drug of choice; NSAIDs are safe (concerns about increased bleeding have been unfounded); codeine should NOT be used in children post-tonsillectomy; aspirin is contraindicated (Reye syndrome risk)
- **Steroids:** Cochrane review supports single IV dose of dexamethasone at induction — reduces nausea, vomiting and pain (Grade A evidence)
- **Antibiotics:** Cochrane review (2012) advocated against routine post-operative antibiotics
- **Day-case surgery:** Demonstrated safe for children with mild OSA in tertiary units

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Paradise criteria (7/5/3 rule) remain the standard threshold for tonsillectomy for recurrent infection — validated by the NESSTAC trial
> 2. Adenotonsillectomy improves 90% of children with obstructive sleep apnoea including behaviour, growth and development
> 3. Cold steel dissection with ties has the lowest combined haemorrhage rates (primary 0.8%, secondary 1.0%) — the gold standard
> 4. Monopolar diathermy has the highest secondary haemorrhage rate (5.5%) and more post-operative pain — not recommended
> 5. NPTA readmission rate for all post-tonsillectomy bleeding is 4.57%, with 1.44% requiring return to theatre
> 6. Secondary haemorrhage is usually infective — antibiotics are the mainstay and surgery is rarely needed
> 7. Tonsillectomy does not impair immunity due to the extensive back-up in the immune system (Waldeyer's ring)
> 8. A single IV dose of dexamethasone at induction reduces post-operative nausea, vomiting and pain (Cochrane Grade A evidence)
> 9. Codeine must not be used in children after tonsillectomy — ultra-rapid metabolizers can develop fatal respiratory failure from toxic morphine doses
> 10. Tonsillotomy (intracapsular technique) has better recovery outcomes than tonsillectomy for obstructive indications and is increasingly popular


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Differentiate recurrent tonsillitis from recurrent pharyngitis — the natural history of tonsillitis is for episodes to become less frequent with time
> - Ampicillin must be avoided in suspected infectious mononucleosis — 90% develop a significant rash from transient immunostimulation
> - The glossopharyngeal nerve and carotid sheath lie deep to the tonsillar bed — dissection must never extend beyond the pharyngeal musculature
> - The 'coroner's clot' is a blood clot pooling in the nasopharynx during adenoidectomy that falls onto the larynx during recovery — the nasopharynx must be suctioned before removing the gag
> - Lemierre's syndrome (septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein) should be suspected when there is severe neck pain and septicaemia following oropharyngeal infection — treatment is 6 weeks of antibiotics
