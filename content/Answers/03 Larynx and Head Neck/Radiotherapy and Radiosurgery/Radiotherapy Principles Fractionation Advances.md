## Introduction

Radiotherapy is the therapeutic use of ionizing radiation, typically high-energy X-rays (photons), to treat malignant disease. In head and neck cancer, it is employed as primary treatment for early-stage disease, as concurrent chemoradiation for organ preservation in advanced disease, and as adjuvant therapy post-operatively. Ionizing radiation interacts with biological material by ejecting electrons, creating a cascade of ionizations. Free radicals generated from interactions with water molecules — principally hydroxyl radicals (OH-) — are the primary mediators of radiation-induced DNA damage. Both single-strand and double-strand DNA breaks result; double-strand breaks are the critical lethal events leading to sterilization of tumour cells and loss of proliferative capacity. However, radiation also targets the plasma membrane, initiating signal transduction pathways that lead to radiation-induced apoptosis through ceramide generation via sphingomyelinase. DNA damage triggers three cellular responses: cell cycle arrest, DNA repair and apoptosis. The conventional standard regimen for curative radiotherapy in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is 70 Gy delivered in 35 fractions over 7 weeks.

## Part A — Types of Radiotherapy in Head and Neck Cancer

### Classification by Modality

| Type | Technique | Key Features |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Conventional EBRT | Opposed lateral fields | Simple; significant dose to normal structures |
| 3D-Conformal RT | CT-planned, multiple shaped beams | Better tumour conformality than 2D |
| IMRT | Modulated beam intensity | Sharp dose gradients; parotid sparing |
| VMAT | Dynamic arc IMRT | Gantry rotates during delivery; faster treatment |
| Proton Beam | IMPT (intensity-modulated) | Reduced low-dose bath to normal tissue |
| Brachytherapy | Interstitial/intracavitary sources | High local dose with rapid dose fall-off |
| SRS/SRT | Single or few high-dose fractions | Sub-mm precision; skull base tumours |

### External Beam Radiotherapy (EBRT)

EBRT using megavoltage photons (4-6 MV) from linear accelerators is the mainstay of H&N radiotherapy. Photons cause ionization resulting in secondary electrons which, in the presence of oxygen, generate free radicals that damage DNA.

**Intensity-Modulated Radiotherapy (IMRT):** The most significant advance in RT delivery. IMRT uses multiple beams with varying intensity across the field to produce highly conformal dose distributions with steep dose gradients. It may be delivered as fixed-beam step-and-shoot IMRT (from 5-7 set beam angles while the gantry is stationary) or as dynamic arc IMRT (delivered while the gantry rotates around the patient). The principal advantage is parotid gland sparing, which reduces late xerostomia — the most impactful quality-of-life benefit of modern RT.

**Intensity-Modulated Proton Therapy (IMPT):** May be considered for certain H&N tumours due to the ability to reduce the 'low-dose bath' compared with photon IMRT. Protons deposit their energy at a specific depth (the Bragg peak) with minimal exit dose beyond the target. This physical property results in less radiation to surrounding normal tissues, potentially reducing late toxicity. (See also File 2: Stereotactic Radiosurgery & Proton Beam Therapy for detailed coverage.)

**Image-Guided Radiotherapy (IGRT):** Daily cone-beam CT scanning or megavoltage imaging verifies patient positioning, checks for changes in contour due to weight loss or tumour shrinkage, and allows adaptive planning. IGRT enables smaller planning target volume (PTV) margins (3-5 mm), further reducing normal tissue irradiation.

### Brachytherapy

Brachytherapy involves placement of radioactive sources directly within or adjacent to the tumour. It delivers a very high dose to the tumour with rapid dose fall-off, minimizing normal tissue damage. In H&N cancer, interstitial brachytherapy is used for oral cavity and oropharyngeal tumours. Iridium-192 wires or seeds are the commonest sources.

### Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS) and Stereotactic Radiotherapy (SRT)

SRS uses a well-defined coordinate base for precise tumour localization and delivers ablative doses in one or a few fractions with sub-mm accuracy. It is used for skull base tumours ([[Vestibular Schwannoma|vestibular schwannoma]], meningioma, pituitary adenoma, [[Glomus Tumour|glomus tumour]]s) and is delivered via modified linear accelerators, Gamma Knife or CyberKnife. (See File 2 for detailed coverage.)

## Part B — Fractionation of Radiotherapy

### Concept and Rationale

A fraction is the individual dose of radiation delivered at a single session. In conventional radiotherapy, fractions are given once daily, five days per week, typically for 4-7 weeks. This fractionation schedule exploits the differential response between acute-reacting and late-reacting tissues:

- **Acute-reacting tissues** (bone marrow, skin, mucosa, SCC): Manifest radiation effects within days; cells are rapidly dividing; surviving clonogens can rapidly repopulate and replace lost cells
- **Late-reacting tissues** (connective tissue, bone, neural tissue, spinal cord): Effects may appear only years later; limited repopulation capacity; late reactions may continue to progress with time

The severity of late effects limits the delivered dose of radiation. The fundamental rationale for fractionation is to deliver a total dose that controls the tumour while keeping late toxicity within acceptable limits.

### Fractionation Schedules

| Schedule | Fraction Size | Total Dose | Overall Time | Key Feature |
|----------|-------------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Conventional | 2 Gy/day, 5 days/week | 70 Gy/35# | 7 weeks (46 days) | Standard regimen |
| Hyperfractionation | 1.15 Gy x 2/day | 80.5 Gy/70# | 7 weeks | Higher total dose; same late effects |
| Accelerated (DAHANCA) | 2 Gy/day, 6 days/week | 68 Gy/34# | 38 days | Shorter overall time |
| CHART | 1.5 Gy x 3/day (continuous) | 54 Gy/36# | 12 continuous days | Very accelerated; lower total dose |
| Dose-escalated hyper | 1.2 Gy x 2/day | 84 Gy/70# | 46 days | Escalated dose; same late toxicity |
| Hypofractionation | >2 Gy/fraction | Variable | Shorter | Used in palliative settings |

### Hyperfractionation

By using many small fractions, a higher total dose can be delivered without increasing late damage, while achieving a greater effect on acute-reacting tissues (including SCC). The theoretical benefits were confirmed in a randomized trial comparing conventional 70 Gy/7 weeks with hyperfractionated 80.5 Gy (two fractions of 1.15 Gy daily, also over 7 weeks). Better local control was achieved in the hyperfractionation arm with no increase in late side effects. A meta-analysis of altered fractionation trials confirmed that an overall survival benefit was only found in the hyperfractionated dose-escalated group — an 8% survival advantage at 5 years compared with conventional fractionation alone, approximately equivalent to the benefit of adding synchronous chemotherapy.

### Accelerated Fractionation

Prolongation of overall treatment time reduces tumour control due to accelerated repopulation of tumour clonogens during treatment. Accelerated fractionation shortens the overall treatment time to counteract this. The CHART (Continuous Hyperfractionated Accelerated Radiation Therapy) regime delivers 54 Gy in 36 fractions over 12 continuous days (3 fractions daily, no weekend breaks). To make very accelerated regimens tolerable to the acute mucosa, the total dose must be reduced. The DAHANCA schedule (68 Gy in 34 fractions over 38 days, 6 fractions per week) represents a more moderate acceleration with minimal dose reduction.

A critical problem with accelerated schedules is that increasing the effect on the tumour also increases the effect on adjacent normal mucosa, causing severe acute reactions that may sometimes lead to an increase in late reactions.

## Part C — Radiobiological Modelling

### The Linear Quadratic (LQ) Equation

The relationship between radiation dose and cell survival is described by the linear quadratic equation:

**SF = N/N0 = e^-(alphaD + betaD^2)** or equivalently **-ln(SF) = alphaD + betaD^2**

Where:
- N0 = initial number of cells; N = number surviving after dose D
- alpha = linear component of cell kill (intrinsic radiosensitivity; lethal hit component) in Gy^-1
- beta = quadratic component of cell kill (combined sublethal damage) in Gy^-2
- The alpha component relates to double-strand breaks; the beta component to combined single-strand breaks
- The beta component is inversely proportional to the repair ability of the tissue

### The alpha/beta Ratio

The alpha/beta ratio is the dose at which the contribution to cell kill from the alpha (non-reparable) component equals that of the beta (reparable) component. It reflects the sensitivity of different tissues to changes in fraction size.

| Tissue | alpha/beta Ratio | Sensitivity to Fraction Size |
|--------|----------|------------------------|
| Head and neck SCC | ~10 Gy | Low (shallow survival curve) |
| Acute mucosal reaction | ~10 Gy | Low |
| Late mucosal damage | ~3 Gy | High (curvy survival curve) |
| Late neural tissue damage | ~2 Gy | Very high |

The lower the alpha/beta ratio, the greater the sensitivity to changes in fraction size. Late-reacting tissues (low alpha/beta) have a more curvy dose-response graph (larger shoulder) — meaning increasing the fraction size even modestly causes disproportionately greater late damage. This is the radiobiological basis for hyperfractionation.

### Biologically Effective Dose (BED)

BED permits comparison of different dose fractionation schedules. Two different schedules that result in the same BED in a defined tissue will produce the same biological effect.

**BED = D (1 + d / (alpha/beta))**

Where D = total dose (n x d) and d = dose per fraction.

Example: For late mucosal toxicity (alpha/beta = 3 Gy):
- 55 Gy in 20# (d=2.75): BED = 55(1 + 2.75/3) = 105 Gy3
- 70 Gy in 35# (d=2.0): BED = 70(1 + 2/3) = 117 Gy3 (higher late mucosal damage expected)

### Equivalent Dose in 2 Gy Fractions (EQD2)

BED is often converted to EQD2 for clinical comparison:

**EQD2 = BED / (1 + 2/(alpha/beta))**

### Effect of Time on BED

For SCC during radiotherapy, accelerated repopulation commences at approximately 21 days (kick-off time, Tk) with a cell turnover time (Tp) of 3 days. A modified BED equation accounts for overall treatment time (T):

**BED = D(1 + d/(alpha/beta)) - (ln2 / (alpha x Tp)) x (T - Tk)**

This time correction applies only to tumour and acute-reacting tissues, not to late effects. Late-responding tissues depend on total dose and fraction size only. This equation is clinically useful for devising revised schedules when unintended treatment gaps occur, as prolonging the overall treatment time demonstrably reduces tumour control.

## Part D — Radiosensitizers

Radiosensitizers are agents that enhance the lethal effects of radiation, ideally showing a therapeutic gain by selectively sensitizing tumour cells while sparing normal tissues. Numerous approaches have been explored.

### Approaches to Overcome Tumour Hypoxia

Hypoxic cells within tumours are relatively radioresistant to X-rays compared with fully oxygenated cells. The diffusion distance for oxygen is less than 150-200 micrometres from an adjacent blood vessel; tumour cells beyond this distance are chronically hypoxic. Acute (transient) hypoxia also occurs due to the opening and closing of tumour blood vessels.

| Category | Agent/Method | Mechanism | Clinical Evidence |
|----------|-------------|-----------|------------------|
| Hyperbaric O2 | HBO tanks | Increase tumour oxygenation | Failed to show benefit in RCTs |
| Nicotinamide + carbogen | ARCON protocol | Nicotinamide dilates vessels (acute hypoxia); carbogen improves oxygenation (chronic hypoxia) | Phase III: locoregional benefit, no survival gain |
| Nitroimidazoles | Nimorazole, misonidazole, metronidazole | Electron-affinic; mimic O2 in hypoxic cells; not rapidly metabolized | Only nimorazole showed benefit in one positive RCT (Danish trial) |
| Halogenated pyrimidines | IUdR, BrdUrd | Incorporated into dividing tumour DNA, increasing radiation susceptibility | Useful for high growth fraction tumours; skin-limiting with BrdUrd |
| Erythropoietin | EPO | Correct anaemia to improve O2 delivery | DAHANCA study: no benefit; patients fared worse |

**ARCON (Accelerated Radiotherapy with Carbogen and Nicotinamide):** Combines accelerated RT with nicotinamide (to deal with acute hypoxia by dilating small tumour vessels) and carbogen breathing (a mixture of O2 and CO2, to improve chronic oxygenation). A phase III trial showed locoregional benefits with acceptable toxicity but no gain in overall survival.

**Nitroimidazoles:** Extensively evaluated as hypoxic cell sensitizers. They are electron-affinic agents that mimic O2, increasing the sensitivity of radioresistant hypoxic cells. Among the many agents tested (misonidazole, metronidazole, benznidazole, etanidazole, pimonidazole, nimorazole, ornidazole, doranidazole), only one major randomized trial has shown therapeutic benefit — a Danish trial demonstrating benefit for nimorazole during radiotherapy of pharyngeal and supraglottic laryngeal cancers. Despite this, nitroimidazoles are still not widely used outside Denmark.

### Bioreductive Drugs

These agents are relatively non-toxic under normal oxygenated conditions but are reduced intracellularly to form cytotoxic agents under hypoxic conditions (via cytochrome P450 and DT diaphorase reductases). Three major groups exist: quinones, nitroaromatics and organic nitroxides.

- **Mitomycin C** (quinone): Antitumour antibiotic that is bioreduced to cross-link DNA; cell cycle non-specific; more toxic to hypoxic than aerated cells; major toxicity is myelosuppression
- **Tirapazamine/SR-4233** (organic nitroxide): Most widely known bioreductive drug; impressive selectivity for hypoxic cells in vitro (50-300-fold) diminishes to 3-5-fold in vivo; phase III trials have not shown clinical benefit
- **Indoloquinone EO9**: Synthetic analogue of mitomycin C; better substrate for DT-diaphorase; disappointing phase II results due to rapid clearance in humans

To date, there is little clinical evidence from large RCTs of bioreductive agents showing increased efficacy when combined with conventional RT.

### Concurrent Chemotherapy as a Radiosensitizer

A clinically more successful strategy has been combining conventional chemotherapy with radiotherapy. Meta-analyses have convincingly shown that the addition of cisplatin gives better results than RT alone — an 8% survival advantage at 5 years. The standard regimen is cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on the Monday of weeks 1, 4 and 7 of radiotherapy. Concurrent cisplatin-based chemoradiation remains the gold standard for locally advanced head and neck cancer unless renal function precludes platinum administration.

### Targeted Therapies

**Cetuximab:** A chimeric humanized monoclonal antibody that competitively binds to EGFR (with 2-log higher affinity than TGF-alpha and EGF). Once bound, the receptor-antibody complex is internalized and the receptor rendered inactive. A landmark phase III trial demonstrated improved locoregional control (24.4 vs 14.9 months) and improved survival (49 vs 29.3 months) with little increase in toxicity compared with RT alone. Cetuximab may also act as an antiangiogenic agent. Despite these results, concurrent cisplatin remains the gold standard over cetuximab-RT.

**Small Molecule Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs):** Gefitinib and erlotinib compete for the ATP-binding site of the EGFR kinase domain; can be administered orally. However, anticipated benefits have not been translated into studies with radiotherapy.

**Anti-PD1 Monoclonal Antibodies:** A recently published study in recurrent SCC of the head and neck has shown potential for significant activity with anti-PD1 antibodies. The question being evaluated is whether such activity can be translated into the curative setting alongside RT.

**Vascular Disrupting Agents (VDAs):** Alter tumour blood flow via activity on the colchicine binding site on tubulin, causing changes in endothelial cell shape and increased vascular permeability.

## Part E — Radioprotective Agents

### Amifostine

Amifostine is the most extensively studied radioprotective agent in H&N cancer. It is a free-radical scavenger that preferentially protects normal tissues over tumour cells.

- A meta-analysis of four investigations concluded that amifostine is effective in reducing the chances of developing Grade 2-3 acute xerostomia and late xerostomia by 76%
- Intravenous amifostine can cause adverse effects — hypotension (in 62% of patients) and nausea — which occur less frequently with subcutaneous administration
- There is no evidence that amifostine has tumour-protective activity or reduces efficacy of anti-cancer therapy
- All randomized studies of amifostine involved patients receiving conventional RT; it is unclear if adding amifostine to IMRT provides additional benefit in reducing xerostomia, given that IMRT already achieves significant parotid sparing

### Glutathione (Natural Radioprotector)

Glutathione (GSH) is the body's natural radioprotector — a radical scavenger that eliminates reactive radicals before they damage DNA. Cells depleted of GSH become more responsive to radiosensitizers. GSH depletion can be accomplished with thiol-binding agents (diamide, diethyl maleate) or inhibition of GSH synthesis with buthionine sulfoximine.

## Part F — Recent Developments in Radiation Therapy for H&N Malignancies

### Intensity-Modulated Radiotherapy (IMRT)

IMRT is now the standard of care for H&N RT. Parotid-sparing IMRT reduces late xerostomia — proven in randomized studies. Fixed-beam step-and-shoot IMRT (5-7 beam angles) and dynamic arc IMRT (VMAT) are both widely used. IMRT allows differential dosing: 70 Gy to the gross tumour volume (GTV) and 56 Gy to prophylactic volumes, all delivered in 35 fractions using a simultaneous integrated boost technique.

### Image-Guided Radiotherapy (IGRT)

Daily imaging (cone-beam CT or megavoltage imaging) verifies positioning and detects contour changes from weight loss or tumour shrinkage. IGRT enables smaller PTV margins (3-5 mm vs 10 mm historically), further sparing normal tissues and allowing adaptive replanning during the course of treatment.

### Intensity-Modulated Proton Therapy (IMPT)

Proton therapy exploits the Bragg peak — protons deposit maximum energy at a precise depth with minimal exit dose. IMPT can reduce the 'low-dose bath' to normal tissues compared with photon IMRT. This is particularly advantageous for re-irradiation, paediatric patients and tumours close to critical structures such as the brainstem and optic pathways. IMPT is considered for selected H&N tumours and is the gold standard for skull base chondrosarcoma, where doses exceeding 70 Gy are required in close proximity to the brainstem.

### Altered Fractionation with Chemoradiation

Two randomized trials of accelerated fractionation chemoradiation versus conventional fractionation chemoradiation have failed to show the expected increase in local control predicted by radiobiological modelling. One hypothesis is that accelerated repopulation may be compensated for by synchronous chemotherapy, removing the rationale for acceleration when chemotherapy is used concurrently.

### Adaptive Radiotherapy

Real-time adaptation of RT plans based on tumour response and anatomical changes during treatment represents the next frontier. This is facilitated by IGRT and may optimize the therapeutic ratio by reducing margins and adapting dose distribution as the tumour shrinks.

### Gamma Knife and Other SRS Platforms

Gamma Knife stereotactic radiosurgery is an established therapeutic modality for select skull base tumours in the head and neck region — particularly vestibular schwannoma, meningioma, pituitary adenoma and glomus tumours. CyberKnife and modified linear accelerators are alternative SRS platforms. (See File 2: Stereotactic Radiosurgery & Proton Beam Therapy for comprehensive coverage of SRS principles, platforms, indications, outcomes and complications.)

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **CHART is the only schedule delivering 3 fractions daily** — 54 Gy in 36 fractions over 12 continuous days (no weekends off); the lower total dose compensates for the very accelerated schedule to maintain mucosal tolerability
> - **Late effects have NO time correction in BED calculations** — late-responding tissues depend only on total dose and fraction size (BED = D(1 + d/(alpha/beta))); time correction applies only to tumour and acute mucosa (adding -(ln2/alpha.Tp)(T-Tk))
> - **Parotid-sparing IMRT has made amifostine less relevant** — since IMRT already achieves significant parotid sparing, the additional benefit of amifostine in the IMRT era is uncertain; all positive amifostine trials used conventional RT
> - **A 1.2% change in local control occurs for every 1% change in BED** — this validates the LQ equation as a clinically useful tool for predicting outcomes in H&N RT
> - **Accelerated fractionation may be unnecessary when chemotherapy is given concurrently** — two RCTs showed no benefit of acceleration with chemoradiation vs conventional chemoradiation; chemotherapy may itself compensate for accelerated repopulation
> - **Cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on weeks 1, 4 and 7** is the standard concurrent chemoradiation regimen, but is contraindicated in patients over 70 years (dose-escalated hyperfractionated RT is not evidence-based in this cohort either)
