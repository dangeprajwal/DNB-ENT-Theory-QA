# Stereotactic Radiosurgery and Proton Beam Therapy in Head and Neck

## Introduction

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and proton beam therapy represent precision radiation techniques that have transformed the management of select skull base and head and neck tumours. SRS uses a well-defined coordinate system to precisely localize the tumour and deliver ablative radiation doses in a single treatment (SRS) or in a small number of fractions (stereotactic radiotherapy, SRT). Proton beam therapy exploits the physical properties of protons — specifically the Bragg peak — to reduce radiation dose to surrounding normal tissues. Both modalities offer distinct advantages over conventional radiotherapy for specific indications and have expanded the therapeutic armamentarium in otolaryngology.

---

## Part A — Principles of Stereotactic Radiosurgery

### Definition and Concept

The term 'stereotactic radiosurgery' is applied to techniques involving a well-defined coordinate base that enables the precise localization of a tumour and targeting of radiotherapy treatment. Localization may be achieved through a fixed or removable headframe, or using the patient's own anatomy to determine a Cartesian-based coordinate system. Combined with MRI, it is used to target pathological lesions including skull base tumours.

SRS uses ionizing radiation to ablate a volume of tissue, or to alter its biological activity, thus preventing further growth. The key principle is delivery of a very high dose to a precisely defined target with steep dose gradients — the dose falls off rapidly outside the target volume, sparing adjacent critical structures.

### Key Characteristics

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Target size | Generally restricted to tumours <3 cm in diameter |
| Dose delivery | Single fraction (SRS) or 2-5 fractions (SRT) |
| Dose gradients | Steep; essential due to proximity of critical structures |
| Localization precision | Sub-millimetre accuracy |
| Fractionation decision | Based on delivery equipment and need for normal tissue recovery |
| Treatment intent | Tumour control (growth arrest) rather than eradication |

### SRS vs Fractionated Radiotherapy

SRT (multi-fraction stereotactic treatment) is preferred over single-fraction SRS in the following situations:
- Tumours within 2 mm of the optic nerves or chiasm (optic pathway dose tolerance is <8 Gy in a single fraction)
- Larger volume tumours (>8 cm3 or >3 cm)
- Tumours with infiltrating margins (e.g., atypical meningiomas)

## Part B — Delivery Platforms for SRS

### Modified Conventional Linear Accelerators

Modified linear accelerators (LINAC-based SRS) offer flexibility as they can treat a wide range of indications. A secondary high-resolution small-field multi-leaf collimator (MLC) is added. Additional stereoscopic X-ray images are acquired for positioning, and a modified treatment couch with patient fixation system achieves sub-mm delivery precision. LINAC-based SRS is the most widely available platform.

### Dedicated Stereotactic Machines

| Platform | Gamma Knife (Elekta) | CyberKnife (Accuray) |
|----------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Radiation source | Fixed array of 192-201 cobalt-60 sources | Miniaturized linear accelerator |
| Mounting | Fixed unit; sources arranged around patient | Robotic treatment arm |
| Localization | Surgically attached rigid stereotactic headframe | Stereoscopic X-ray imaging (frameless) |
| Accuracy | Sub-mm (frame-based) | <0.5 mm intracranial; <1 mm extracranial |
| Beam angles | Multiple (several hundred); changed by blocking sources and moving patient | Unrestricted; robotic arm not limited by fixed gantry |
| Target scope | Intracranial and skull base only | Intracranial + extracranial (can use spine/fiducial markers) |
| Fractionation | Single fraction primarily (frame limits repeat treatments) | Equally precise for single or multiple fractions (no frame needed) |
| Motion correction | Not applicable (rigid frame) | Intrafractional movement detected via stereoscopic X-ray; corrected by robotic path changes |

### Patient Treatment Workflow

1. **Pre-treatment imaging:** MRI for target definition (CT or other modalities depending on pathology)
2. **Localization imaging:** CT performed in the treatment immobilization device; for Gamma Knife, this is done after frame application on the day of treatment; for CyberKnife, this can be done in advance
3. **Image fusion and target delineation:** Images from multiple modalities are fused; target and critical structures are defined by a multidisciplinary team (radiation oncology, neurosurgery, neuroradiology, medical physics)
4. **Treatment planning:** A plan is produced to conform the prescribed dose to the target while minimizing dose to organs at risk; subject to rigorous quality assurance
5. **Treatment delivery:** Typical delivery time is approximately 1 hour; patient is positioned using the appropriate immobilization device; the patient does not feel anything but can see and hear the machine moving; generally outpatient; GA not usually required but may be considered for young children

## Part C — Indications and Outcomes by Tumour Type

### Meningiomas

- Most skull base meningiomas are benign (WHO Grade 1), well demarcated, but can be difficult to excise due to risks of damaging adjacent neurovascular structures (e.g., within cavernous sinus)
- SRS is used following partial resection (to prevent regrowth) or as primary treatment when there is confidence in the radiological diagnosis
- **Dose:** 12-15 Gy single fraction for benign meningiomas
- **Outcomes:** In a multicentre analysis of 3768 patients with benign meningiomas, 5-year and 10-year progression-free survival (PFS) were 95.2% and 88.6% respectively, with a permanent morbidity incidence of 6.6%
- **Limitations:** SRS typically used for tumours <8 cm3 (<3 cm); for larger volumes, multi-session SRT (up to 5 fractions) using CyberKnife is increasingly used
- **Optic pathway constraint:** Radiation-induced optic neuropathy (RION) is rare if dose is limited to <8 Gy in a single fraction; tumours within 2 mm of the optic pathway require fractionated RT rather than SRS
- **Cranial nerve complications:** In a series of 763 sellar/parasellar meningiomas, new or worsening deficits occurred in 9.6%, with the trigeminal nerve most commonly affected
- **Atypical/anaplastic meningiomas (Grade 2-3):** SRS is generally not suited to tumours with infiltrating margins; 5-year PFS estimated at 59% for Grade 2 and 13% for Grade 3

### Vestibular Schwannoma (see Part D for detailed coverage)

### Pituitary Adenomas

- **Non-functional adenomas (NFAs):** SRS or fractionated RT reserved for recurrence/progression after incomplete surgical resection; actuarial PFS of 95%, 91% and 85% at 5, 8 and 10 years respectively; new or worsened hypopituitarism occurs in 21%
- **Functional adenomas:** SRS is a useful adjunct when surgical cure is not possible; biochemical remission rates vary — Cushing's disease (~50%), acromegaly (~43.6%), prolactinomas (~30%); there is a latent period of at least one year post-SRS before biochemical remission during which medical therapy must continue
- **Long-term risks:** Hypopituitarism, potential increased risk of stroke (carotid stenosis documented as rare event), and second malignancy (case reports exist but one population-based study failed to show increased risk)

### Chondrosarcoma and Chordoma

- Small, well-demarcated residual tumours may be targets for SRS
- However, fractionated radiotherapy with doses exceeding 70 Gy reports the highest rates of local control; chondrosarcoma outcomes are more favourable than chordoma
- Proton therapy, fractionated SRT or image-guided IMRT are preferred for delivering such high doses near the brainstem
- **Proton beam therapy is the gold standard for skull base chondrosarcoma**

### [[Glomus Tumour|Glomus Tumour]]s (Paragangliomas)

- Rare benign tumours in and around the ear (glomus tympanicum, glomus jugulare); often intimately involved with surrounding neurovascular structures
- SRS preferred for relatively small, well-demarcated tumours in difficult surgical locations
- Meta-analysis of 276 patients: pooled local control following radiotherapy (both fractionated and SRS) as initial, combined and salvage treatment ranged from 79% to 100%
- Treatment intent is tumour control rather than eradication
- Timing of intervention remains debated given that many tumours remain relatively indolent for many years

## Part D — Role of SRS in Management of Vestibular Schwannoma

### Overview

Vestibular schwannomas (VS) are slow-growing benign tumours. Management options include observation with serial MRI, surgical resection, and radiotherapy (SRS or fractionated SRT). Radiotherapy aims to stop growth and avoid surgery; some centres also use it to preserve auditory function. Numerous published reports of high local control rates with minimal toxicity have established SRS as an accepted alternative to surgical resection.

### Dose and Technique

- Current recommended marginal dose: **12-13 Gy** (reduced from historical dose of 16 Gy, which lowered cranial nerve complications without adversely affecting local control)
- SRS is generally recommended for tumours **<3 cm** in extra-canalicular diameter with **no significant brainstem compression** (post-radiosurgery tumour swelling can cause 4th ventricular distortion with obstructive hydrocephalus)
- Treatment is predominantly delivered using Gamma Knife, but similar outcomes are reported with CyberKnife and LINAC-based SRS

### Outcomes

| Study | Follow-up | Patients | Platform | Marginal Dose | Local Control | CN V Preservation | CN VII Preservation |
|-------|-----------|----------|----------|---------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Series 1 | 2.9 years | 232 | Gamma Knife | 15 Gy | 97% | 98.5% | 99% |
| Series 2 | 5.6 years | 216 | Gamma Knife | 13 Gy | 98.3% (10-yr) | 94.9% | 100% |
| Series 3 | 8.1 years | 75 | LINAC | 14 Gy | 92% | 100% | 92% |
| Series 4 | 5 years | 103 | Gamma Knife | 13 Gy | 91.1% | 99% | 95% |

### Pseudoprogression

A critical phenomenon unique to SRS-treated VS. Transient enlargement of the tumour followed by stability or even regression occurs in **23% of cases**, most commonly regressing at 24 months post-treatment. Only tumours that begin or continue to enlarge **after 24 months post-SRS** should be considered treatment failures. Further intervention (most commonly resection) should not be rushed. Routine imaging follow-up is recommended for 10 years post-SRS.

### Cranial Nerve Preservation

- **Trigeminal nerve (CN V):** Preservation rates 78.7-100% with 12-13 Gy marginal dose; risk factors for trigeminal dysfunction include tumour volume, maximum dose to the nerve, length of nerve irradiated and tumour contact with the nerve due to post-SRS swelling
- **Facial nerve (CN VII):** Risk of persisting palsy is low with contemporary doses; pre-existing palsy or hemifacial spasm is associated with increased risk of worsening CN VII dysfunction
- **Hearing preservation:** For patients with serviceable hearing (Gardner-Robertson Grades I-II), some centres advocate early radiosurgery to preserve auditory function; one prospective study reported hearing preservation of 77%, 70% and 64% at 3, 4 and 5 years with early SRS (12 Gy), compared with 75%, 52% and 41% in the observation group

### VS in Neurofibromatosis Type 2 (NF2)

Patients with NF2 usually have bilateral VS and are at risk of bilateral deafness, trigeminal and facial neuropathies. Results of surgery and radiotherapy for NF2-associated VS are worse than for sporadic unilateral tumours — tumour control rates are lower and iatrogenic hearing loss is more common. Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody) is increasingly used as first-line management in NF2 when intervention is required; it may improve hearing in some patients and reduce tumour volume in the majority.

## Part E — Proton Beam Therapy in Head and Neck Cancer

### Principles

Protons deposit their energy at a specific depth within tissue — the **Bragg peak** — with minimal exit dose beyond the target. This physical property results in significantly less radiation to surrounding normal tissues compared with photon-based RT. Intensity-Modulated Proton Therapy (IMPT) applies the principles of IMRT to proton beams, further optimizing dose distribution.

### Key Advantages over Photon RT

| Advantage | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Bragg peak dose deposition | Maximum dose at tumour depth; minimal exit dose |
| Reduced low-dose bath | Less integral dose to normal tissues |
| Better OAR sparing | Reduced dose to brainstem, spinal cord, optic pathway, posterior fossa |
| Reduced late toxicity | Potentially lower rates of xerostomia, dysphagia, hypothyroidism |
| Paediatric advantage | Reduced risk of growth retardation and second malignancies |

### Indications in Head and Neck Cancer

1. **Skull base chondrosarcoma** — gold standard; allows delivery of >70 Gy doses adjacent to brainstem with acceptable toxicity; local control rates for chondrosarcoma are more favourable than for chordoma
2. **Skull base chordoma** — high-dose fractionated RT (>70 Gy) required; proton therapy, fractionated SRT or highly conformal photon IMRT are the delivery options
3. **Re-irradiation of recurrent H&N cancer** — reduced low-dose bath minimizes additional toxicity to previously irradiated normal tissues
4. **Paediatric H&N tumours** — reduced integral dose lowers the risk of growth disturbance, developmental effects and radiation-induced second malignancies
5. **[[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|Nasopharyngeal carcinoma]]** — proximity to brainstem, optic pathways and temporal lobes; IMPT may reduce neurological toxicity
6. **Sinonasal malignancies** — complex anatomy with multiple critical structures in close proximity; proton therapy optimizes therapeutic ratio
7. **Tumours requiring very high doses** — where dose escalation beyond conventional photon limits is needed for local control

### Technique

1. **Patient immobilization:** Custom thermoplastic mask and headrest for reproducible daily positioning
2. **Imaging:** CT simulation (2 mm slices) with IV contrast; MRI fusion for target delineation
3. **Target and OAR delineation:** GTV, CTV and PTV defined; organs at risk include brainstem, spinal cord, optic nerves/chiasm, cochlea, parotids, temporal lobes
4. **Treatment planning:** IMPT plan generated using inverse planning algorithms; multiple beam angles optimized to maximize target coverage while minimizing OAR doses
5. **Treatment delivery:** Pencil beam scanning (PBS) is the modern technique; each treatment session typically 20-30 minutes; daily image guidance verifies positioning
6. **Quality assurance:** Rigorous dosimetric verification; range uncertainty is a key consideration in proton planning (typically 3.5% range uncertainty margin added)

### Limitations of Proton Beam Therapy

- High capital and operational cost; limited availability (few centres worldwide)
- Range uncertainty — proton dose distribution is sensitive to anatomical changes (weight loss, tumour shrinkage, nasal cavity filling); requires frequent adaptive planning
- Lack of mature RCT evidence — most data are from retrospective series and dosimetric comparison studies; randomized evidence comparing protons vs photon IMRT in H&N cancer is ongoing
- Relative biological effectiveness (RBE) uncertainty — the standard RBE of 1.1 used clinically may vary at the distal edge of the Bragg peak

## Part F — Complications of Stereotactic Radiosurgery

### Early Complications

| Complication | Features |
|-------------|----------|
| Headache, nausea | Common; related to headframe application (Gamma Knife) or high-dose irradiation; self-limiting |
| Perilesional oedema | May cause transient neurological worsening; managed with short course of dexamethasone |
| Seizures | Rare; more common in cortical lesions; anticonvulsant prophylaxis for at-risk patients |

### Delayed Complications

| Complication | Features |
|-------------|----------|
| Pseudoprogression | Transient tumour enlargement (23% of VS); most regress by 24 months; must be distinguished from true treatment failure |
| Cranial neuropathy | CN V (78.7-100% preservation), CN VII (>92% preservation), hearing loss; risk related to tumour volume, maximum nerve dose and length of nerve irradiated |
| RION | Radiation-induced optic neuropathy; rare if <8 Gy to optic pathway; visual loss can be permanent |
| Hypopituitarism | 21% after pituitary region SRS; requires long-term endocrine monitoring |
| Carotid stenosis | Documented as rare event after pituitary SRS; high carotid doses may be unavoidable |
| Obstructive hydrocephalus | Risk with VS >3 cm; post-SRS swelling can distort 4th ventricle |
| Second malignancy | Case reports exist; one population-based study showed no increased risk |

### Factors Affecting Complication Risk

- **Tumour volume** — larger tumours have higher complication rates; SRS generally limited to <3 cm or <8 cm3
- **Marginal dose** — reduction from 16 Gy to 12-13 Gy for VS significantly reduced cranial nerve complications
- **Proximity to critical structures** — optic pathway (<2 mm), brainstem, cranial nerves
- **Prior treatment** — previous surgery or radiation increases complication risk
- **Tumour biology** — NF2-associated VS have worse outcomes than sporadic tumours

## Brief Note: Liposomal Amphotericin B (Q2024-111, Part 2)

Liposomal amphotericin B (L-AmB) is the gold-standard antifungal agent for invasive fungal sinusitis, an aggressive and potentially fatal condition particularly affecting immunocompromised patients. L-AmB encapsulates amphotericin B within liposomal vesicles, which reduces nephrotoxicity compared with conventional amphotericin B deoxycholate while maintaining antifungal efficacy. The dose is typically 3-5 mg/kg/day IV. It is the drug of choice for rhinocerebral [[Invasive Fungal Rhinosinusitis|mucormycosis]] (caused by Mucor, Rhizopus species) and invasive aspergillosis that is refractory to voriconazole. L-AmB acts by binding to ergosterol in fungal cell membranes, creating pores that lead to cell death. Management of invasive fungal sinusitis requires a combined approach of aggressive surgical debridement, systemic antifungal therapy (L-AmB), correction of immunosuppression and close multidisciplinary follow-up.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. SRS uses a well-defined coordinate base for sub-mm tumour localization and delivers ablative doses in one or few fractions with steep dose gradients; it is restricted to tumours <3 cm in diameter
> 2. Three SRS delivery platforms exist: Gamma Knife (192-201 cobalt sources, rigid headframe, intracranial only), CyberKnife (robotic arm, frameless, <0.5 mm accuracy, can treat extracranial) and modified linear accelerators
> 3. SRS achieves >90% local control for vestibular schwannoma with a marginal dose of 12-13 Gy (reduced from historical 16 Gy), with CN V preservation of 78.7-100% and CN VII preservation >92%
> 4. Pseudoprogression occurs in 23% of VS after SRS — transient enlargement followed by stability or regression; tumours enlarging after 24 months post-SRS should be considered treatment failures
> 5. For meningiomas, SRS with 12-15 Gy achieves 5-year PFS >95% for Grade 1 tumours; RION is rare if the optic pathway dose is limited to <8 Gy in a single fraction
> 6. Proton beam therapy exploits the Bragg peak for maximum dose deposition at tumour depth with minimal exit dose; IMPT reduces the low-dose bath compared with photon IMRT
> 7. Proton beam therapy is the gold standard for skull base chondrosarcoma; other H&N indications include chordoma, re-irradiation, paediatric tumours and nasopharyngeal carcinoma
> 8. Complications of SRS include cranial neuropathy, pseudoprogression, RION, hypopituitarism (21% after pituitary SRS), hydrocephalus (VS >3 cm) and rare second malignancy
> 9. Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) is increasingly used as first-line management for NF2-associated VS, as SRS and surgery have worse outcomes in NF2 than in sporadic tumours
> 10. Liposomal amphotericin B is the gold-standard antifungal for invasive fungal sinusitis (mucormycosis, invasive aspergillosis); liposomal formulation reduces nephrotoxicity while maintaining efficacy at 3-5 mg/kg/day IV

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **The dose reduction from 16 Gy to 12-13 Gy for VS radiosurgery was a pivotal advance** — it significantly reduced cranial nerve complications without compromising local control rates (>90% at 10 years)
> - **Do not rush to intervene after apparent VS enlargement post-SRS** — pseudoprogression is expected in nearly one-quarter of cases and most tumours regress by 24 months; only sustained growth beyond 24 months indicates true treatment failure
> - **Gamma Knife cannot treat extracranial disease** — the headframe-based system is restricted to intracranial and skull base pathology; CyberKnife can target disease extending into the neck using spine or fiducial markers for localization
> - **The 8 Gy threshold for optic pathway safety is the single most important dose constraint in skull base SRS** — tumours within 2 mm of the optic nerves/chiasm must be treated with fractionated RT rather than single-fraction SRS to avoid RION
> - **Proton therapy's limitation is range uncertainty** — proton dose distribution is exquisitely sensitive to anatomical changes such as weight loss, tumour shrinkage and nasal cavity filling (mucous vs air); this necessitates frequent adaptive replanning not required for photon IMRT
> - **For functional pituitary adenomas, SRS achieves good local control but variable biochemical remission** — Cushing's disease responds best (~50% remission), followed by acromegaly (~44%); prolactinomas respond poorly (~30%); there is always a latent period of at least one year before biochemical remission
