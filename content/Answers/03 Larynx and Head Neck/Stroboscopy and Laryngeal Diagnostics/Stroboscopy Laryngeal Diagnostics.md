# Videolaryngostroboscopy, Contact Endoscopy and Laryngeal Diagnostic Techniques

**Question:** *Video laryngostroboscopy — principle and interpretation. Stroboscopy — definition, parameters, clinical uses. Contact endoscopy — procedure and applications in ENT. Video laryngoscopy — principle and clinical applications.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapters 59 (Physiology of the Larynx), 61 (Assessment and Examination of the Larynx), 62 (Evaluation of the Voice) and 63 (Structural Disorders of the Vocal Cords); Volume 1, Chapter 51 (Contact Endoscopy of the Upper Aerodigestive Tract)

---

## Introduction

Accurate assessment of the larynx requires visualization not only of the structure but also of the dynamic function of the vocal folds. The vocal folds vibrate at frequencies usually exceeding 100 cycles per second, far beyond the ability of the retina to process individual images (five per second). Standard white-light endoscopy therefore cannot reveal the mucosal wave pattern, which is critical for diagnosis of vocal fold pathology. Videolaryngostroboscopy uses a pulsed light source synchronised to the fundamental frequency of the voice to create a slow-motion illusion of vocal fold vibration, allowing assessment of the mucosal wave, glottal closure and symmetry of vibration. Contact endoscopy provides microscopic assessment of the epithelial cells and microvascular network in vivo. Together with newer techniques such as narrow-band imaging (NBI), autofluorescence and optical coherence tomography (OCT), these modalities form a comprehensive toolkit for laryngeal diagnosis. Laryngostroboscopy will remain the most useful clinical tool in the evaluation of voice disorders for the foreseeable future.

---

## PART A: Videolaryngostroboscopy — Principle

### The Stroboscopic Principle

Stroboscopy exploits the persistence of vision (Talbot's law). A strobe light flashes at a frequency slightly different from the fundamental frequency of vocal fold vibration. Each flash illuminates the vocal folds at a slightly different point in the vibratory cycle. The human eye integrates these successive snapshots into an apparent slow-motion image of vibration. This allows assessment of the mucosal wave, which is otherwise invisible to the naked eye.

### The Vibratory Cycle

Each vibratory cycle of the vocal folds consists of three phases — adduction, aerodynamic separation and recoil:

1. Increased subglottic air pressure overcomes the resistance of the adducted vocal folds
2. The vocal folds peel apart from their inferior border; when they separate at their superior margin, a puff of air is released
3. The resulting negative pressure (Bernoulli effect) causes the vocal folds to be sucked together rapidly, inferior margins closing first

The mucosal wave is an undulation of the mucous membrane cover over the firm body of the vocalis muscle (cover-body theory of Hirano). These undulations can only be observed using laryngostroboscopy or high-speed photography.

### Phases of the Vibratory Cycle

| Phase | Description |
|---|---|
| Closing phase | VF begin to close rapidly from lower margin |
| Closed phase | Medial edges in full contact |
| Opening phase | VF begin to separate from lower margin, peeling apart |
| Open phase | VF separated (longest part of normal vibratory cycle) |

## PART B: Equipment and Technique

### Rigid Videostroboscopy (Transoral)

A rigid 70° or 90° Hopkins rod telescope is placed transorally. This is the favoured technique for laryngologists in the voice clinic because the wider rigid rod lens produces much higher optical resolution for detailed assessment of phonation. The patient phonates during the examination to generate vibration. The strobe unit contains a microphone that detects the fundamental frequency and synchronises the light flashes.

**Advantages:** Superior image quality, wider field of view, better visualisation of mucosal wave detail.
**Disadvantages:** Cannot be used in patients with strong gag reflex, does not assess dynamic laryngeal function during connected speech.

### Flexible Videostroboscopy (Transnasal)

A flexible videolaryngoscope is passed transnasally. Modern distal-tip chip (CCD) videoscopes provide images approaching the quality of rigid telescopes. The transnasal approach is associated with less gag reflex and allows assessment during connected speech, singing and other phonatory tasks.

**Advantages:** Better tolerated, functional assessment during speech, assessment of supraglottic function.
**Disadvantages:** Historically lower image quality (now improving with HD chip-tip endoscopes).

### Clinical Setting

Laryngeal assessment should ideally be performed in a multidisciplinary voice clinic by a laryngologist and speech therapist. Photo and video documentation is becoming an essential part of practice for case review and medicolegal purposes.

## PART C: Interpretation of Stroboscopic Features

Stroboscopy improves diagnostic accuracy — it has led to changes in diagnosis in approximately 30% of cases when compared to examination with continuous light alone.

### Key Parameters Assessed

| Parameter | What to Assess |
|---|---|
| Glottal closure pattern | Complete, anterior/posterior gap, hourglass, spindle-shaped, irregular |
| Closed phase duration | Absent, reduced, normal, prolonged |
| Mucosal wave (R/L) | Symmetry of amplitude, symmetry of phase |
| Periodicity | Regular, variable, irregular |
| Degree of mucosal wave | Absent, decreased, normal, increased |
| VF opening/closing (R/L) | Range (full/reduced), normal/lag, spasm/tremor |
| Supraglottic appearance | False cord medial constriction, anteroposterior constriction, prominence/lesions |
| Arytenoid symmetry | Sagittal, coronal and axial planes |
| Lesion description | Colour, shape, surface, single/multiple |

### Clinical Significance of Findings

**Absent or reduced mucosal wave:** Indicates stiffness of the vocal fold cover — seen in scarring, sulcus vocalis, carcinoma, post-radiation changes, Reinke's space pathology.

**Asymmetry of phase:** One fold opens or closes ahead of the other — may indicate a mass lesion, differential tension or neurological impairment.

**Asymmetry of amplitude:** One fold has greater lateral excursion — may indicate unilateral pathology.

**Irregular periodicity (aperiodicity):** Random vibration pattern — suggests severe stiffness, mass lesion or neurological disorder.

**Incomplete glottal closure:** Posterior gap (common in young women, may be normal), anterior gap (elderly women), hourglass (nodules), spindle-shaped (paresis).

**Supraglottic constriction:** Medial compression of false cords and/or anteroposterior squeezing — hallmark of muscle tension dysphonia or compensatory behaviour.

## PART D: Clinical Uses of Stroboscopy

1. **Diagnosis of vocal fold lesions:** Differentiation between cysts, polyps, nodules, sulcus vocalis and Reinke's oedema — the mucosal wave pattern differs characteristically for each
2. **Detection of early malignancy:** A stiff, absent mucosal wave in a focal area raises suspicion of carcinoma even before a visible mass is apparent
3. **Assessment of [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal fold paralysis]]/paresis:** Asymmetric vibration, phase lag, reduced amplitude on the affected side
4. **Evaluation of muscle tension dysphonia:** Supraglottic hyperfunction with excessive false cord adduction and anteroposterior compression
5. **Post-operative assessment:** Monitoring mucosal wave recovery after phonosurgery
6. **Pre- and post-treatment evaluation:** Documenting vocal fold vibration before and after voice therapy, [[Injection Laryngoplasty|injection laryngoplasty]] or framework surgery
7. **Professional voice assessment:** Essential for evaluation of singers and voice professionals where subtle mucosal changes affect performance
8. **Treatment outcome measurement:** Stroboscopy provides visual evidence of therapeutic change

## PART E: Contact Endoscopy

### Definition and Principle

Contact endoscopy is a non-invasive technique that allows microscopic assessment of the epithelial cells and microvascular network of the larynx in vivo and in situ. A modified Hopkins rod telescope (Karl Storz 8715 AA — 5.8 mm diameter, 24 cm length) is applied directly against the mucosal surface. Magnification of 60x and 150x is available, controlled by a ring near the proximal ocular end.

### Technique

**For cellular assessment:**
- The mucosa is stained with methylene blue (or Lugol's iodine)
- The contact endoscope is gently applied to the mucosal surface
- Colouration lasts 4-5 minutes
- Cytological characteristics of the first three layers of epithelial cells are visualised — nuclei, cytoplasm and nucleus:cytoplasm ratio

**For microvascular assessment:**
- The endoscope is applied directly without staining
- The microcirculation is visible with regular endoscopy light
- NBI illumination (Olympus system, 415 nm) enhances visualisation by selective absorption of haemoglobin

**Setting:** At the larynx and hypopharynx, contact endoscopy is performed under general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation. At the nasal cavity, oral cavity and oropharynx, it can often be performed without anaesthesia.

### Normal and Pathological Patterns

| Pattern | Contact Endoscopy Findings |
|---|---|
| Normal squamous | Polyhedric cells, round dark nuclei, light blue cytoplasm, regular N:C ratio |
| Normal ciliated | Round dark nuclei, indistinct cytoplasm borders, bundles of cilia visible |
| Chronic inflammation | Homogeneous pattern but nuclei larger, increased N:C ratio, higher vascular density |
| Keratosis | Isolated cells without nuclei, amorphous/laminar structure in heavy keratinisation |
| Dysplasia | Altered size, shape and colouration of nuclei, dyskaryosis, anisokaryosis |
| Carcinoma | Marked cellular irregularity and heterogeneity, irregular N:C ratio, atypical vessels with thrombosis |

### Diagnostic Accuracy

Sensitivity and specificity of 90% and 94% respectively, with 88% agreement with histopathological findings after appropriate training. Contact endoscopy is designed not as a substitute for histology but as a clinical method that adds to the information available during endoscopic examination.

### Applications in ENT

1. Non-invasive pathological evaluation during endoscopic examination
2. Guidance of biopsies — directing the surgeon to the most suspicious areas
3. Establishment of safe surgical margins during resection
4. Assessment of transition areas between normal and abnormal mucosa
5. Identification of early stages of disease (subclinical staging)
6. Mapping of disease across multiple anatomical sites (field cancerisation)
7. Evaluation of irradiated patients — avoids complications of unnecessary biopsy in previously treated tissue
8. Assessment of fungal infections (spores, hyphae visible)

### Limitations

- Superficial depth of penetration (first 3 layers of epithelium only)
- Cannot distinguish between intraepithelial neoplasia and invasive carcinoma
- Impaired visualisation in the presence of scarring, chronic inflammation or keratosis
- Experience required to minimise trauma with the endoscopes

## PART F: Other Laryngeal Diagnostic Techniques

### Narrow-Band Imaging (NBI)

Uses filtered blue light (415 nm) and green light (540 nm) to highlight superficial and subepithelial vascular patterns. Intraepithelial papillary capillary loops (IPCLs) are associated with precancerous and malignant lesions. Sensitivity 89% and specificity 93% for primary laryngeal lesions. NBI mode is a standard feature on some videoendoscopes. Particularly useful for assessing post-radiation/chemoradiation surveillance (reduces need for tissue biopsy).

### Autofluorescence

Normal mucosa fluoresces green when stimulated with blue/UV light (375-460 nm). Neoplastic mucosa has reduced autofluorescence and emits red-violet colour. Sensitivity 87-97%, specificity 78-87% for invasive carcinoma. Limitations include false positives from granulation tissue, scarring and chronic inflammation.

### Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)

An analogue of ultrasound using light instead of sound to produce high-resolution (micrometre) cross-sectional images similar to vertical histological sections. Can significantly increase sensitivity of determining benign versus malignant lesions when combined with microlaryngoscopy.

### Laryngeal Ultrasonography

Ultrasound probe applied against the external surface of the larynx during microlaryngoscopy. Holds potential for assessing invasion of deeper compartments (paraglottic and pre-epiglottic spaces).

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Stroboscopy exploits Talbot's law (persistence of vision) — the strobe light flashes at a frequency slightly different from the fundamental frequency, creating an apparent slow-motion image of vocal fold vibration
> 2. The mucosal wave is an undulation of the mucous membrane cover over the vocalis muscle body (cover-body theory) — it can only be visualised by stroboscopy or high-speed photography, not by continuous-light endoscopy
> 3. Stroboscopy changes the diagnosis in approximately 30% of cases compared to continuous-light examination alone — it is the most useful clinical tool for evaluation of voice disorders
> 4. Key stroboscopic parameters include glottal closure pattern, mucosal wave symmetry (amplitude and phase), periodicity, closed phase duration, and supraglottic constriction
> 5. An absent or reduced mucosal wave indicates stiffness of the vocal fold cover — this is the hallmark of scarring, sulcus vocalis, early carcinoma and post-radiation changes
> 6. Contact endoscopy uses a modified Hopkins rod at 60x-150x magnification with methylene blue staining — it allows in vivo assessment of epithelial cells and microvascular networks with 90% sensitivity and 94% specificity
> 7. Contact endoscopy is not a substitute for histology but adds information during endoscopic examination — its main applications include guiding biopsies, establishing surgical margins and assessing irradiated tissue
> 8. NBI uses filtered blue (415 nm) and green (540 nm) light to highlight neoangiogenic patterns — intraepithelial papillary capillary loops are associated with precancerous and malignant lesions (sensitivity 89%, specificity 93%)
> 9. Rigid videostroboscopy provides superior image quality and is preferred in voice clinics — flexible videostroboscopy is better tolerated and allows functional assessment during connected speech
> 10. Incomplete glottal closure may be a normal finding — posterior glottal gap is common in young and middle-aged women, and should not be automatically classified as pathological


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Stroboscopy cannot function in an aperiodic voice (severely dysphonic or aphonic patients) because the strobe requires a detectable fundamental frequency to synchronise — high-speed digital imaging is an alternative in these cases
> - A focal area of absent mucosal wave on an otherwise normal-appearing vocal fold should raise high suspicion for submucosal pathology (cyst, early carcinoma, sulcus) — this is the unique diagnostic advantage of stroboscopy over white-light endoscopy
> - Supraglottic constriction (false cord medial compression with anteroposterior squeezing) on stroboscopy is the hallmark of muscle tension dysphonia — this compensatory behaviour may mask underlying pathology and should prompt careful assessment
> - Contact endoscopy combined with NBI illumination dramatically improves visualisation of deeper mucosal vessels — the combination allows mapping of field cancerisation across the upper aerodigestive tract
> - The closing phase of the vibratory cycle is more rapid than the opening phase — this asymmetry is a normal feature of vocal fold vibration and should not be mistaken for pathology
