## Q149. Emergency management of acute corrosive poisoning — airway, long-term sequelae (2013 Jun)

*Discuss the emergency management of acute corrosive poisoning with particular reference to airway management and long-term sequelae.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 34 (Foreign Bodies in the Ear, Nose and Throat) & Chapter 45 (Oesophageal Disorders in Children); Volume 3, Chapter 49 (Causes and Assessment of Dysphagia), Chapter 53 (Oesophageal Diseases), Chapter 56 (Chronic Aspiration), Chapter 71 (Laryngotracheal Injury)

---

### Introduction

Corrosive poisoning refers to the ingestion of caustic substances — either strong alkalis or acids — that cause chemical burns to the lips, oral cavity, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus and sometimes the stomach. In children, ingestion is most commonly accidental, particularly in infants and toddlers who gain access to household cleaning agents stored in containers without child-proof caps. In older teenagers and adults, a significant proportion of caustic ingestions are intentional as suicide attempts. The incidence of caustic ingestion is rising, with newer formulations of laundry detergents in tablet and 'liquitab' form being increasingly implicated. In the UK alone, liquitabs (liquid detergent parcels/tablets) are responsible for approximately 1500 incidents each year. The management of acute corrosive poisoning requires urgent airway assessment and stabilization, followed by systematic evaluation of the depth and extent of injury and planned long-term management of sequelae including stricture formation and malignant transformation.

---

### Part A: Aetiology and Pathophysiology

**1. Types of Caustic Agents**

| Parameter | Alkali Ingestion | Acid Ingestion |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| Mechanism | Liquefaction necrosis | Coagulation necrosis |
| Depth of injury | Deep, penetrating | Superficial (eschar limits) |
| Main site affected | Oesophagus | Stomach and pylorus |
| Common agents | NaOH, KOH, bleach, detergents | HCl, H2SO4, oxalic acid |
| Overall severity | More damaging to oesophagus | Less damaging to oesophagus |

**2. Pathophysiology of Alkali Injury**
- Strong alkalis cause injury by tissue liquefaction and necrosis
- The liquefaction process allows deep penetration through all layers of the oesophageal wall
- Thrombosis of the supplying vessels results in reduced blood flow, which hampers healing
- The combination of deep tissue destruction and impaired vascular supply leads to progressive full-thickness injury
- Caustic liquids typically have no flavour or odour, making accidental ingestion by children more likely

**3. Pathophysiology of Acid Injury**
- Acids cause coagulative necrosis with eschar formation
- The eschar acts as a barrier, limiting further penetration (unlike alkalis)
- Acid ingestion is generally less damaging to the oesophagus than alkali ingestion
- Acids cause more damage to the stomach and the pylorus, where they pool
- Gastric perforation is a risk with concentrated acid ingestion

**4. Mechanism of Injury Progression**

The injury to the oesophageal wall progresses through predictable phases:
- **Acute phase (0–48 hours):** Oedema, inflammation, mucosal necrosis, haemorrhage
- **Subacute phase (3–14 days):** Sloughing of necrotic tissue, granulation tissue formation, maximum risk of perforation (weakest point of healing wall)
- **Chronic phase (>3 weeks):** Fibrosis, cicatrization, stricture formation — most strictures occur within 8 weeks of caustic ingestion
- **Late phase (years–decades):** Risk of malignant transformation at the site of stricture

**5. Specific Concerns**
- Caustic substances can also be inhaled, leading to oedema and burns of the larynx and trachea
- Ingestion of caustic substances can cause full-thickness airway burns, leading to significant morbidity
- The liquid within liquitabs can be inhaled and cause ocular injury; death has also been reported
- Impulsive behaviour and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder have been shown to be risk factors for caustic ingestion in children

### Part B: Clinical Presentation

**1. Immediate Symptoms**
A child or adult who has swallowed a caustic substance usually presents immediately with:
- Crying, spitting, coughing and stridor
- Drooling and inability to swallow
- Severe pain in the mouth, throat and chest
- Potential airway compromise — this is a serious concern in all cases

**2. Early Symptoms (Hours)**
- Chest pain and retrosternal discomfort
- Dysphagia and odynophagia
- Vomiting (may contain blood or mucosal debris)
- Fever and tachycardia

**3. Signs on Examination**
- Burns may be visible on the lips, perioral skin, and within the oral cavity
- However, the absence of oral burns does NOT exclude oesophageal or laryngeal injury — a critical clinical point
- Stridor or hoarseness suggests laryngeal involvement
- Abdominal tenderness, guarding or rigidity suggests perforation
- Signs of mediastinitis: fever, tachycardia, subcutaneous emphysema

---

### Part C: Emergency Management

**1. Initial Assessment — ABCDE Approach**

The first priority in all cases is assessment and protection of the airway:

- **Airway:** Oedema of the larynx caused by the caustic substance may occlude the airway. Prompt endotracheal intubation may be required. If intubation fails, surgical airway ([[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]]) should be performed
- **Breathing:** Assess for respiratory distress, stridor, tachypnoea. Inhalational injury to the larynx and trachea can cause progressive airway oedema
- **Circulation:** IV access, fluid resuscitation, monitor for shock (perforation with mediastinitis/peritonitis)
- **Disability:** Assess conscious level, pain assessment
- **Exposure:** Full examination including oral cavity, neck, chest, abdomen

**2. Specific Emergency Measures**

| Action | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Secure airway | Early intubation if stridor present |
| NPO (nil by mouth) | All patients should be fasted |
| IV fluids | Commence intravenous fluids |
| Do NOT induce vomiting | Re-exposure to caustic worsens injury |
| Do NOT attempt dilution | Drinking water may cause exothermic reaction |
| Do NOT attempt neutralization | Unknown pH, risk of exothermic reaction |
| Analgesia | Adequate pain control (IV opioids) |
| Avoid NG tube (blind) | Risk of perforation through damaged wall |

**3. Critical 'Do NOT' Principles**

Three actions are specifically contraindicated:
- **Do NOT give water to wash or dilute** — attempts to wash the caustic substance away by drinking water may result in an exothermic chemical reaction which may only serve to worsen the injury
- **Do NOT attempt neutralization** — attempts to neutralize the ingested caustic substance may fail if the pH of the ingested substance is not known, and again may result in an unwanted exothermic chemical reaction
- **Do NOT pass blind NG tube** — risk of perforation through necrotic oesophageal wall. If NG tube placement is required, this should be done under endoscopic guidance during the initial assessment endoscopy

**4. Airway Management in Detail**

Airway compromise results from:
- Direct caustic burns to the epiglottis, aryepiglottic folds and supraglottis
- Laryngeal and supraglottic oedema
- Inhalation of caustic fumes or aspirated caustic liquid

Management:
- All patients require continuous monitoring of respiratory status
- Early intubation should be performed if any signs of airway compromise (stridor, hoarseness, dyspnoea, drooling)
- Intubation should ideally be performed by an experienced operator — the oedematous and friable tissues make intubation hazardous
- If intubation is not possible, a surgical airway (tracheostomy) must be established
- Ingestion of caustic substances can lead to full-thickness airway burns. Reflex closure of the glottis occurs as a protective mechanism, but if the caustic agent reaches the subglottis or trachea, the resulting injury is severe and leads to significant morbidity

### Part D: Investigation and Grading

**1. Imaging**
- **Chest X-ray:** Assess for pneumomediastinum (suggesting perforation), pleural effusion, or aspiration
- **Lateral soft-tissue neck X-ray:** Loss of natural lordosis of C-spine, widening of prevertebral space, air bubble in oesophagus
- **CT with contrast:** If perforation is suspected — chest/abdomen CT to identify mediastinitis, free air, or peritonitis
- **Contrast swallow:** Only with non-ionic water-soluble contrast if perforation suspected (does not interfere with subsequent endoscopy)

**2. Early Endoscopy**

Early endoscopy is usually indicated in ALL cases to assess the extent and severity of the injury. This is the cornerstone of assessment.

**3. Grading of Caustic Oesophageal Injury**

| Grade | Endoscopic Findings | Prognosis |
|-------|---------------------|-----------|
| Grade 1 | Erythema and oedema only | Conservative management, good |
| Grade 2a | Mucosal haemorrhage, exudates, superficial ulcers | Generally favourable |
| Grade 2b | Deep or circumferential ulcers | Risk of stricture formation |
| Grade 3a | Focal necrosis, deep ulcers with eschar | High stricture risk |
| Grade 3b | Extensive necrosis, luminal obliteration | Perforation risk, poor prognosis |
| Grade 4 | Perforation | Poor prognosis, surgical emergency |

Key endoscopic findings by severity:
- **Superficial injury:** Mucosal erythema, oedema, mild mucosal damage
- **Intermediate injury:** Haemorrhage, exudates, mucosal sloughing, pseudomembrane formation, granulation tissue
- **Severe injury:** Luminal narrowing or obliteration, severe oedema, eschar formation
- **Perforation:** Mediastinitis is possible in severe cases

**4. NG Tube Placement**
- Blind passage of an NG tube is contraindicated
- Placement during endoscopy is indicated to facilitate enteral nutrition and to maintain the integrity of the oesophageal lumen
- The end of a blind-ending NG tube should be cut off to allow passage of a guidewire in the future — this is helpful if the NG tube needs to be replaced or if a stricture develops and might benefit from balloon dilatation under imaging control

**5. Role of Antibiotics and Steroids**
- The use of antibiotics and steroids to limit the effects of the injury and prevent stricture formation is controversial
- Conflicting studies have shown both benefit and none
- Current practice varies — some centres advocate short courses of systemic steroids for Grade 2b/3a injuries, while others do not

---

### Part E: Long-Term Sequelae and Management

**1. Oesophageal Stricture**
- This is the most common long-term sequela
- Most strictures occur within 8 weeks of caustic ingestion
- Circumferential injuries (Grade 2b and above) are most likely to develop strictures
- Strictures may be short-segment or long-segment

Management of strictures:
- **Serial balloon dilatations** are the preferred treatment of a fibrous stricture
- There is an inherent risk of oesophageal perforation during dilatation
- **Oesophageal stenting** is less well established but may be considered
- **Oesophageal resection and replacement** may be required for long and the most resistant strictures
  - Replacement options include colonic transposition (transverse colon) or gastric tube interposition
  - Transverse colon transposition has been used successfully for patients with caustic injury to the pharynx, oesophagus and gastroduodenal areas
  - Hypopharyngeal strictures following caustic ingestion may require lateral pharyngeal augmentation techniques with rotational or free myocutaneous or bowel flaps

**2. Oesophageal Carcinoma**
- The risk of subsequent carcinoma is significantly higher — reported as 1000-fold increased risk compared to the general population
- The carcinoma develops at the site of the stricture
- Long latency period — may develop decades after the initial injury
- Usually squamous cell carcinoma
- Necessitates long-term endoscopic surveillance

**3. Airway Sequelae**
- Laryngotracheal stenosis may develop following caustic burns to the larynx and upper trachea
- Caustic substances can lead to full-thickness airway burns, leading to:
  - Ulceration → fibrinoid necrosis → fibrosis → cicatrix → stenosis
  - Granulation tissue in the posterior glottis can draw the posterior vocal folds together, leading to progressive airway obstruction
- Other sequelae include glottic incompetence, dysphonia, aspiration and dysphagia
- The chronicity of changes may allow time for glottic compensation

**4. Chronic Aspiration and Dysphagia**
- Internal trauma from caustic ingestion is a recognized cause of an unsafe swallow
- Pharyngeal and oesophageal scarring leads to reduced compliance and impaired peristalsis
- Post-stricture dysphagia may be permanent despite repeated dilatations
- Some patients require permanent gastrostomy feeding

**5. Perforation and Mediastinitis**
- Oesophageal perforation may occur acutely or during the subacute phase (days 3-14)
- Late perforation may occur during dilatation procedures
- Mediastinitis requires aggressive management with IV antibiotics, antifungals, fasting and parenteral nutrition

---

### Part F: Special Considerations

**1. Paediatric Considerations**
- Most caustic ingestions in young children are accidental
- Children suspected of ingesting caustic agents should be admitted for evaluation
- A chest X-ray may be useful for initial assessment
- Early intubation may be needed if the airway is at risk
- All affected children should be fasted and intravenous fluids administered
- Early endoscopy is indicated in all cases
- Newer formulations of household cleaning products (liquitabs) pose increasing risk

**2. Button Battery Ingestion — Related Caustic Injury**
- Button batteries cause caustic burns through release of electrical current and leakage of contents
- Burns occur within 2–2.5 hours of impaction
- The highest risk is a 20 mm battery in children under 4 years old
- 54% of cases with a fatal outcome were misdiagnosed
- Complications include oesophageal perforation, haemorrhage (including erosion into great vessels), [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal cord palsy]], tracheo-oesophageal fistula, and death
- These require removal as a matter of urgency — the mechanism is similar to caustic chemical burns

**3. Intentional Ingestion (Adults)**
- A significant proportion in older teenagers and adults are suicide attempts
- Psychiatric assessment is mandatory once medically stable
- Often involves ingestion of larger quantities and more concentrated agents
- Higher grades of injury and worse outcomes compared to accidental ingestion
- May involve multiple agents

#> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Caustic ingestion causes tissue injury by two mechanisms: alkalis cause liquefaction necrosis (deep penetration), while acids cause coagulative necrosis (surface eschar limits depth); alkali ingestion is more damaging to the oesophagus while acids cause more damage to the stomach and pylorus
> 2. Airway assessment is the FIRST priority — oedema of the larynx from caustic exposure may occlude the airway, requiring early intubation or tracheostomy
> 3. Three critical contraindications: do NOT induce vomiting, do NOT give water or attempt dilution (risk of exothermic reaction), and do NOT attempt neutralization (unknown pH, risk of exothermic reaction)
> 4. Blind NG tube passage is absolutely contraindicated — if required, it should be placed under endoscopic guidance during assessment endoscopy
> 5. Early endoscopy is indicated in ALL cases to grade the injury (Grades 1-4 based on depth of injury), which determines management and predicts stricture risk
> 6. Most oesophageal strictures develop within 8 weeks of caustic ingestion; serial balloon dilatation is the preferred treatment, with oesophageal resection and colonic/gastric tube replacement reserved for resistant cases
> 7. The risk of oesophageal carcinoma at the site of stricture is 1000-fold higher than the general population, developing decades later and requiring long-term endoscopic surveillance
> 8. Caustic ingestion can cause full-thickness airway burns leading to laryngotracheal stenosis through the pathway of ulceration → fibrinoid necrosis → fibrosis → cicatrix → stenosis
> 9. Button battery impaction in the oesophagus causes similar caustic injury through electrical current and chemical leakage, with burns occurring within 2-2.5 hours and a 20 mm battery in a child under 4 years carrying the highest risk
> 10. Liquitabs (liquid detergent parcels/tablets) are responsible for approximately 1500 caustic ingestion incidents per year in the UK, with the liquid capable of causing ingestion, inhalation and ocular injuries


---

#> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The absence of oral or lip burns does NOT exclude significant oesophageal or laryngeal injury — always perform endoscopy regardless of visible external signs
> - The subacute phase (days 3-14) represents the period of maximum perforation risk as the necrotic tissue sloughs but the healing wall is at its weakest — avoid endoscopy and dilatation during this window
> - When placing an NG tube during initial endoscopy, cut off the blind end to allow future guidewire passage — this simple manoeuvre facilitates subsequent dilatation if stricture develops
> - Steroids for stricture prevention remain controversial with conflicting evidence — if used, they are typically limited to Grade 2b/3a injuries where the risk-benefit ratio is most favourable
> - The 1000-fold increased carcinoma risk means lifelong surveillance is necessary — carcinoma develops at the stricture site, often decades later, and is usually squamous cell type
> - In paediatric cases, 54% of fatal button battery outcomes involved initial misdiagnosis — the double-contour sign on X-ray and a high index of suspicion in any young child with acute dysphagia are essential for timely recognition
