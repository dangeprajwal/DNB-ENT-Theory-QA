# Cricopharyngeal Dysphagia and Zenker's Diverticulum

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Discuss the aetiopathogenesis, clinical features and management of cricopharyngeal dysphagia. What is a pharyngeal pouch? Describe the aetiopathogenesis of Zenker's diverticulum. Discuss endoscopic management and outcomes of pharyngeal pouch surgery.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 52 (Cricopharyngeal Dysphagia)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Cricopharyngeal dysphagia is difficulty swallowing due to dysfunction of the cricopharyngeal muscle — the most inferior part of the pharyngeal constrictors forming the upper oesophageal sphincter (UES). Dysfunction is failed relaxation due to fibrosis or muscle spasm. This can occur with or without formation of a pharyngeal pouch (Zenker's diverticulum) — a herniation of pharyngeal mucosa through Killian's dehiscence, the area of natural weakness between the oblique thyropharyngeus and horizontal cricopharyngeus fibres. Incidence of pharyngeal pouch is approximately 0.47–1 per 100,000 per year, predominantly in Caucasian males over 50 years, with peak presentation in the sixth to ninth decades.

---

## PART A: ANATOMY AND AETIOPATHOGENESIS

### Applied Anatomy of the UES

The UES is not a simple circular sphincter but an inverted cone-shaped functional zone at the pharyngo-oesophageal junction. The cricopharyngeus muscle has horizontal fibres arising from both sides of the cricoid cartilage and is normally in a state of tonic contraction. It relaxes at the end of the second stage of swallowing due to both active muscle relaxation and mechanical pull from laryngeal elevation. The triangular area between the oblique thyropharyngeus above and horizontal cricopharyngeus below is Killian's dehiscence — described by Killian in 1908 — where very few muscle fibres exist, creating a site of potential herniation.

### Three Theories of Pharyngeal Pouch Formation

| Theory | Proponent | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Anatomical weakness | Van Overbeek | Large Killian's dehiscence; descent of human larynx creates the gap; individual variations in muscle fibre arrangement |
| Failed CP relaxation | Negus, Belsey, Cook | Persistent tonic spasm or fibrosis of cricopharyngeus prevents bolus passage; creates high pressure above → herniation |
| Incoordination | Ardran, Kemp, Lund; Ellis | Premature closure of cricopharyngeus before pharyngeal peristalsis complete → high intraluminal pressure → herniation |

### Consensus on Pathophysiology

It is unlikely that a single pathophysiological process causes pharyngeal pouch formation. In general terms, incoordination between the descending peristaltic wave and failed cricopharyngeal muscle relaxation creates abnormally high intraluminal pressure, subsequently leading to mucosal herniation through Killian's dehiscence.

### Contributing Factors

- **Gastro-oesophageal reflux:** Reported incidence of GORD in pouch patients ranges from 5% to 100%; LPR may cause cricopharyngeal spasm or fibrosis (Cook's theory)
- **Ageing:** Loss of tissue elasticity and decreased muscle tone after age 40
- **Inclusion body myositis:** Primary muscle disorder causing failed cricopharyngeal relaxation → fibrosis → pouch formation
- **Cervical osteophytes:** Can create pseudopouches over which the pharynx moves
- **Anterior cervical spine surgery:** Traction pouches from adhesions (rare)
- **Familial predisposition:** Van Overbeek reported 9 patients from 4 families in 545 cases

## PART B: CLINICAL FEATURES AND DIAGNOSIS

### Symptoms

| Symptom | Features |
|---------|----------|
| Dysphagia | Most common; insidious onset; initially solids, progresses to liquids |
| Regurgitation | Undigested food; worse lying down; may evacuate pouch by neck pressure |
| Halitosis | Food stasis in pouch; foul taste |
| Pulmonary | Cough, aspiration, chest infections, pneumonitis, lung abscess |
| Hoarseness | Laryngitis from aspiration or coexisting reflux; "wet" voice quality |
| Neck swelling | Intermittent compressible swelling (left anterior triangle); cervical borborygmi |
| Weight loss | Progressive; can be severe with malnutrition |

Pain on swallowing is unusual and should raise suspicion of malignancy.

### Examination

- General: nutritional state, hydration
- Neck: rarely a soft compressible swelling, usually left side
- Flexible nasoendoscopy: usually normal larynx; may show saliva pooling
- "Rising tide" sign on FEES: small amounts of swallowed fluid rise up from postcricoid region after bolus passes

### Investigation

- **Barium swallow / contrast video swallow:** Diagnostic investigation of choice — demonstrates pouch, its size, and pharyngo-oesophageal function; must include lower oesophagus and stomach to exclude coexisting carcinoma
- Differential diagnosis: globus, scleroderma, achalasia, oesophageal neoplasm, stricture, neuropathies, myopathies

### Pathology and Malignant Change

- Pouch wall: stratified squamous epithelium and submucosa surrounded by fibrous tissue; scanty muscle fibres near neck
- Carcinoma within a pouch: rare (0.32–0.4%); associated with long-standing pouch (average >7 years); predisposed by chronic irritation from food retention
- Warning signs: rapidly increasing dysphagia, pain, blood-stained regurgitated food

## PART C: MANAGEMENT

### Approach Based on Pouch Size

| Pouch Size | First-Line Treatment | Recurrence/Failure |
|------------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Small (<2 cm) | Balloon dilatation of CP or laser division of CP bar | Endoscopic laser division or open CP myotomy |
| Moderate (2–4 cm) | Endoscopic stapling | Revision stapling or open excision + myotomy |
| Large (>4 cm) | Endoscopic stapling | Revision stapling or open excision + myotomy |

### Management Options

**1. Conservative**
- No drug therapies are effective for cricopharyngeal dysphagia
- Botulinum toxin injection: variable success; reports of protracted dysmotility and temporary laryngeal paralysis

**2. Endoscopic Stapling (First-Line for Most Pouches)**
- Technique: under GA with endotracheal intubation; Weerda diverticuloscope inserted; cricopharyngeal bar identified; linear cutting stapling gun divides septum between pouch and oesophagus while simultaneously sealing edges with triple staggered rows of staples
- Pouch mucosa inspected with Hopkins telescope to exclude carcinoma
- Access difficult in ~20% (prominent teeth, retrognathia)
- Post-operative: soft diet for 1 week; many patients discharged same day; nasogastric tube only if significant mucosal damage

**3. CO2 Laser Division**
- Cricopharyngeal bar divided under microscope using CO2 laser via Weerda scope
- Advantage over stapling: better in limited access; can treat smaller pouches
- Disadvantage: higher perforation risk; requires experience to judge division depth

**4. Open Cricopharyngeal Myotomy (Sole Procedure)**
- Indicated for very small symptomatic pouches or prominent CP bar without true pouch
- Approach through left neck; sternomastoid retracted laterally; identify RLN near inferior thyroid artery
- CP fibres divided over at least 4 cm length; oesophagoscope used to transilluminate fibres

**5. Open Pouch Excision**
- Indications: carcinoma suspected in pouch, large perforation during endoscopic attempt, young patients with large pouches
- Pouch mobilized, neck dissected to pharyngeal wall defect, excised with autosuture stapler; separate CP myotomy performed
- Hospital stay >7 days; complication rate ~20%

## PART D: OUTCOMES OF PHARYNGEAL POUCH SURGERY

### Summary of Outcomes (Summated From Major Series)

| Method | No. Patients | Success | Revision Rate | Major Complications | Hospital Stay |
|--------|-------------|---------|---------------|--------------------:|:--------------|
| Excision ± myotomy | 278 | 91% | 14.3% | RLN palsy 12.6%, fistula 11.9%, mediastinitis 1.4% | 12+ days |
| Endoscopic stapling | 587 | 90% | 12.6% | Perforation 2.4%, dental 2.6% | 2 days |
| Laser division | 463 | 90.6% | 7.3% | Perforation 3.6%, bleeding 0.9%, mediastinitis 0.86% | 4 days |

### Key Findings

- NCEPOD (1995–96) recommended endoscopic stapling as treatment of choice; mortality of pouch surgery 1–2%
- NCEPOD also recommended that only 1–2 surgeons per ENT department should perform pouch surgery — 75% of surgeons were doing fewer than 3 operations/year
- Surgeon experience is critical: Van Overbeek's series (544 patients, single surgeon) showed 91.5% success; multiple surgeons doing Dohlman's procedure achieved only 70%
- Endoscopic revision after recurrence is straightforward — a major advantage over open revision which is difficult and higher risk
- Long-term recurrence may be higher with endoscopic techniques than open excision, but ease of revision compensates

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Killian's dehiscence is the triangular area of weakness between the oblique thyropharyngeus and horizontal cricopharyngeus muscle fibres — the site of pharyngeal pouch herniation
> 2. Three aetiology theories: anatomical weakness (Van Overbeek), failed CP relaxation/spasm (Negus/Belsey/Cook), and incoordination of pharyngeal peristalsis (Ardran/Kemp/Lund)
> 3. Pharyngeal pouch is a pulsion diverticulum; incidence ~1 per 100,000/year; almost exclusively Caucasians; M:F = 2:1; peak 6th–9th decades
> 4. Cardinal symptoms: progressive dysphagia, regurgitation of undigested food, halitosis, aspiration complications — pain suggests malignancy
> 5. Diagnosis: barium/contrast video swallow — must include lower oesophagus to exclude coexisting carcinoma
> 6. Carcinoma within a pouch is rare (0.4%) but requires vigilance — always inspect pouch lining with Hopkins telescope at surgery
> 7. Endoscopic stapling is the first-line treatment for moderate/large pouches — 90% success, 2-day stay, minimal morbidity
> 8. CO2 laser division: similar success rate (90.6%) to stapling; better for limited access and small pouches; higher perforation risk
> 9. Open excision reserved for suspected carcinoma, large perforation, or young patients; complication rate ~20% (RLN palsy 12.6%, fistula 11.9%)
> 10. Surgeon experience is paramount — NCEPOD recommended 1–2 specialist surgeons per department; large single-surgeon series have far better outcomes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The "rising tide" sign on FEES is characteristic of pharyngeal pouch — swallowed fluid rises back up from the postcricoid region after the bolus has passed
> - Gastro-oesophageal reflux commonly coexists with pharyngeal pouch — treating GORD usually improves symptoms of all motility disorders
> - Difficult endoscopic access (20% of patients) is predicted by prominent teeth and retrognathia — always have a plan for external surgery as backup
> - Gun jamming during stapling occurs when too much tissue is placed between jaws — never pull a jammed gun; gently manipulate the release lever
> - Post-operative contrast studies are often unhelpful after endoscopic procedures — barium coats the pouch wall and radiologists invariably report "recurrence" even in asymptomatic patients
