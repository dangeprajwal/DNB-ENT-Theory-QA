# Dysphagia — Classification, Assessment and Management of Intractable Aspiration

> [!question] Questions
> Classify causes of dysphagia. Clinical assessment of a case of dysphagia after head and neck surgery. Role of various swallowing maneuvers in management. Discuss various surgical options for intractable aspiration.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 49 (Causes and Assessment of Dysphagia), Chapter 55 (Rehabilitation of Swallowing Disorders), Chapter 56 (Chronic Aspiration)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Dysphagia affects approximately 22% of the population over the age of 50. It can be classified as oropharyngeal (high) or oesophageal (low) dysphagia. In head and neck cancer patients, dysphagia may result from the tumour itself, surgical resection, or chemo[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] effects. Aspiration — contamination of the tracheobronchial tree with secretions or food — can be life-threatening. Intractable aspiration that fails conservative measures requires surgical intervention ranging from medialization [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|thyroplasty]] to narrow-field laryngectomy.

---

## PART A: CLASSIFICATION OF CAUSES OF DYSPHAGIA

### By Anatomical Level

| Level | Causes |
|-------|--------|
| Oropharyngeal (high) | Neurological, muscular, structural pharyngeal/laryngeal diseases |
| Oesophageal (low) | Motility disorders, strictures, tumours, extrinsic compression |

### By Aetiology

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| Congenital | Cleft palate, cerebral palsy, vascular rings, laryngeal clefts |
| Neurological | CVA (most common), Parkinson's, MND, MS, myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré |
| Muscular | Muscular dystrophy, inclusion body myositis, dermatomyositis |
| Inflammatory | GORD, LPR, eosinophilic oesophagitis |
| Autoimmune | Scleroderma/CREST, SLE, Sjögren's, pemphigus |
| Neoplastic | Oral, oropharyngeal, hypopharyngeal, laryngeal, oesophageal tumours |
| Traumatic | FB ingestion, caustic burns, head/neck trauma |
| Post-treatment | Post-surgical, post-radiotherapy, post-CRT |
| Motility disorders | Achalasia, diffuse oesophageal spasm, cricopharyngeal dysfunction |
| Miscellaneous | Globus, pharyngeal pouch, presbydysphagia, cervical osteophytes |

### Post-Treatment Dysphagia in HNC

- **Post-surgical:** Tissue loss, scarring, reduced tongue base function, pharyngeal constrictor damage, RLN injury, pharyngeal plexus damage
- **Post-radiotherapy:** Mucositis (acute), fibrosis (late), xerostomia, reduced pharyngeal contraction, trismus, cricopharyngeal stricture
- **Post-CRT:** Combined effects; late fibrosis may progress for 10-20 years post-treatment

## PART B: CLINICAL ASSESSMENT OF DYSPHAGIA

### History

- Duration, onset (acute vs progressive), type (solids vs liquids vs both)
- Aspiration symptoms: coughing during/after meals, wet voice, recurrent chest infections
- Weight loss, nutritional status
- Previous treatment: surgery (type, extent), radiotherapy (fields, dose), chemotherapy
- Neurological symptoms

### Examination

- General: nutritional state, hydration, respiratory status
- Oral cavity: tongue mobility, palatal function, dentition
- Cranial nerves: V (mastication), VII (lip seal), IX (gag), X (palate, larynx), XII (tongue)
- Flexible nasoendoscopy: vocal cord mobility, pharyngeal pooling, laryngeal sensation, mucosal changes

### Instrumental Assessment

| Investigation | Purpose |
|--------------|---------|
| FEES | Bedside assessment of pharyngeal residue, aspiration, laryngeal sensation |
| VFSS | Gold standard for oral/pharyngeal dynamics |
| Manometry | Oesophageal motility, cricopharyngeal function |
| CT/MRI | Structural assessment, tumour recurrence |
| Transnasal oesophagoscopy | Direct oesophageal assessment without sedation |

### Objective Scoring Tools

- **Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS):** 8-point scale from FEES/VFSS data (1 = no penetration; 8 = silent aspiration)
- **EAT-10:** Subjective 10-item dysphagia questionnaire; score >3 is abnormal
- **MBSImp:** Standardized VFSS measurement tool

## PART C: SWALLOWING MANOEUVRES AND REHABILITATION

### Compensatory Techniques (Immediate Effect)

| Technique | Mechanism | Indication |
|-----------|-----------|------------|
| Chin tuck | Widens valleculae, narrows airway entrance | Premature spillage, delayed trigger |
| Head rotation (to weak side) | Closes weaker pyriform, directs bolus to stronger side | Unilateral pharyngeal paresis |
| Head tilt (to strong side) | Gravity directs bolus to functioning side | Unilateral oral/pharyngeal weakness |
| Dietary modification | Thickened liquids, pureed diet | Thin liquid aspiration |

### Rehabilitative Manoeuvres (Strengthen Swallow)

| Manoeuvre | Technique | Effect |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| Supraglottic swallow | Inhale → hold → swallow → cough | Voluntary airway closure before/during swallow |
| Super-supraglottic | Inhale → bear down → swallow → cough | Arytenoid tilting + cord closure |
| Effortful swallow | Swallow with maximum effort | Increased tongue base retraction and pharyngeal pressure |
| Mendelsohn manoeuvre | Hold larynx elevated at peak of swallow | Prolongs UES opening; improves bolus clearance |

### Newer Approaches

- **Tongue strengthening exercises:** Iowa Oral Performance Instrument (IOPI) — resistance training improves lingual pressure and swallow efficiency
- **Expiratory muscle strength training (EMST):** Improves subglottic pressure and cough effectiveness
- **Thermal-tactile application (TTA):** Cold stimulation of faucial pillars — may improve swallow trigger (evidence limited)
- **Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS):** Cortical modulation of swallowing pathways — research stage
- **Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS):** Non-invasive cortical stimulation — research stage

## PART D: SURGICAL OPTIONS FOR INTRACTABLE ASPIRATION

When conservative measures, dietary modification, and swallowing therapy fail to prevent life-threatening aspiration, surgical options are considered in a stepwise approach from least to most invasive.

### Hierarchy of Surgical Interventions

**1. Cricopharyngeal sphincter surgery**
- Botulinum toxin injection into cricopharyngeus (80-120 units in 3 aliquots under direct laryngoscopy) — "medical myotomy"; temporary, can be repeated
- Endoscopic CO2 laser myotomy — more permanent option
- External cricopharyngeal myotomy — rarely needed

**2. Vocal fold medialization**
- [[Injection Laryngoplasty|Injection laryngoplasty]] (temporary or semi-permanent materials)
- Medialization thyroplasty (Type I) — for unilateral VF paralysis with aspiration
- Bilateral medialization thyroplasty — for bilateral bowing/atrophy
- Arytenoid adduction — combined with medialization for posterior glottic gap

**3. Hypopharyngeal pharyngoplasty**
- For high vagal injury with hypopharyngeal dilatation
- Excision/plication of redundant pharyngeal mucosa on affected side
- Combined with cricopharyngeal myotomy and medialization

**4. Enhanced laryngeal suspension**
- Thyroid cartilage partially excised above glottic level
- Thyrohyoid approximation with braided monofilament sutures
- Combined with cricopharyngeal myotomy ± medialization thyroplasty
- Elevates larynx away from pooled hypopharyngeal secretions

**5. Glottic/supraglottic closure**
- Montgomery procedure (1975): vocal cord mucosa stripped bilaterally, cords sutured together → fibrous union
- Biller supraglottic laryngoplasty (1975): two-layer vertical supraglottic closure with small epiglottic opening
- Limitations: [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] dependent, no voice, high breakdown risk, not easily reversible

**6. Laryngotracheal separation (Lindeman procedure)**
- Trachea divided below subglottis; distal trachea brought out as stoma; proximal trachea closed as blind end
- Theoretically reversible but high complication rate (40%): wound breakdown, fistulae, stenosis
- Many patients ultimately require tracheostomy or laryngectomy

**7. Narrow-field laryngectomy**
- Definitive procedure for intractable aspiration
- Removes laryngeal skeleton; spares hyoid, strap muscles, hypopharyngeal mucosa
- Provides permanent separation of respiratory and digestive tracts
- Avoids tracheostomy; allows oral intake
- Voice restoration possible with tracheo-oesophageal puncture
- Reserved for patients with no prospect of swallowing recovery and reasonable life expectancy

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Dysphagia classified as oropharyngeal (high) vs oesophageal (low); by aetiology: neurological, neoplastic, traumatic, post-treatment, motility disorders
> 2. CVA is the most common neurological cause of dysphagia; post-CRT fibrosis may progress for 10-20 years
> 3. Assessment: history, cranial nerve examination, flexible nasoendoscopy, FEES and/or VFSS
> 4. Penetration-Aspiration Scale (8-point, from FEES/VFSS) and EAT-10 (subjective questionnaire) are standard scoring tools
> 5. Compensatory techniques (chin tuck, head turn) provide immediate effect; rehabilitative manoeuvres (Mendelsohn, effortful swallow) aim to strengthen swallow mechanism
> 6. Mendelsohn manoeuvre: patient holds larynx elevated at peak of swallow → prolongs UES opening → improves pharyngeal clearance
> 7. Intractable aspiration management is stepwise: botulinum toxin to cricopharyngeus → medialization → laryngeal suspension → closure procedures → laryngectomy
> 8. Tracheostomy is NOT the ideal solution for chronic aspiration — it reduces laryngeal elevation, compresses oesophagus via cuff, and bypasses laryngeal airflow reducing sensitivity
> 9. Narrow-field laryngectomy is the definitive procedure for intractable aspiration — permanently separates airway from digestive tract
> 10. All aspiration patients should be managed in MDT: otolaryngologist, SLT, neurologist, gastroenterologist, dietitian


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A tracheostomy cuff does NOT prevent aspiration — secretions can pass around even an inflated cuff; the cuff also anchors the trachea reducing laryngeal elevation
> - "Silent aspiration" (aspiration without cough) occurs when laryngeal sensation is impaired — diagnosed on FEES/VFSS; associated with worse outcomes
> - Post-radiotherapy dysphagia is multifactorial: fibrosis, reduced pharyngeal contraction, impaired sensation, xerostomia — may be progressive and irreversible
> - Cervical osteophytes can overhang the laryngeal inlet acting as a "ski-slope" directing secretions into the airway — trans-oral removal risks worsening dysphagia
> - Trans-oral laser surgery to posterior pharyngeal wall tumours may cause severe dysphagia due to pharyngeal plexus damage — even for small tumours
