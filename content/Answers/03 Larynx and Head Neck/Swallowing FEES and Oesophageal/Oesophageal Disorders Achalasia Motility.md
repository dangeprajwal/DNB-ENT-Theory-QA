# Oesophageal Disorders — Achalasia, Motility Disorders and Oesophageal Diseases

**Question:** *Discuss achalasia cardia — aetiology, clinical features, investigation and management. Describe oesophageal motility disorders. Discuss Barrett's oesophagus, caustic stricture and oesophageal carcinoma. Describe the classification, investigation and management of oesophageal perforation.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 53 (Oesophageal Diseases)

---

## INTRODUCTION

A broad spectrum of benign and malignant conditions affects the oesophagus. Motility disorders — particularly achalasia — are important causes of dysphagia that may first present to the ENT surgeon. Barrett's oesophagus, a consequence of chronic GORD, increases the risk of oesophageal adenocarcinoma. Oesophageal carcinoma is the eighth most common cancer worldwide. Oesophageal perforation remains a rare but potentially fatal condition requiring prompt diagnosis and management. All these conditions are managed in specialist oesophagogastric centres to optimize outcomes.

---

## PART A: ACHALASIA CARDIA

### Definition and Aetiology

Achalasia is an uncommon condition (incidence 1 per 100,000/year) resulting from degeneration of the ganglionic neurons within the myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus. It is characterized by:

- Failure of relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) on swallowing
- Absence of peristalsis in the oesophageal body
- May be associated with higher resting oesophageal tone

| Type | Features |
|------|----------|
| Primary achalasia | Idiopathic; degeneration of myenteric plexus |
| Secondary achalasia | Chagas' disease (Trypanosoma cruzi); post-antireflux surgery (fundoplication too tight) |
| Vigorous achalasia | Subtype with simultaneous strong peristaltic contractions → severe retrosternal pain |

### Clinical Features

- Dysphagia to both liquids and solids (distinguishes from mechanical obstruction which typically affects solids first)
- Regurgitation of stagnant oesophageal content
- Recurrent chest infections from aspiration
- Retrosternal chest pain (vigorous achalasia)
- Weight loss (long-standing disease)
- Equal sex distribution; most common in middle and older age groups

### Investigation

| Investigation | Findings |
|---------------|----------|
| Flexible endoscopy | May be normal in early disease; tight OGJ, dilated oesophagus, food within oesophagus; essential to exclude malignancy |
| Barium swallow | "Bird's beak" appearance — dilated non-peristaltic oesophagus tapering to a LOS that fails to relax |
| Oesophageal manometry | Confirmatory — absent peristalsis with failure of LOS relaxation; high-resolution manometry identifies subtypes that may respond differently to treatment |

### Management

| Treatment | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Balloon dilatation | Under sedation; day-case; 30 mm balloon initially → sequential 35/40 mm if needed; risk of perforation increases with larger balloons; requires experienced endoscopist |
| Laparoscopic cardiomyotomy | Muscles split for 5 cm along oesophagus + 3 cm into cardia; some combine with partial fundoplication to reduce post-operative reflux; risk of mucosal perforation |
| European RCT results | Balloon dilatation 93% vs laparoscopic cardiomyotomy 90% at 2 years — no significant difference |
| POEM (Per-oral endoscopic myotomy) | Newer technique from Japan; submucosal tunnel created endoscopically before circular muscle fibre division; being evaluated in RCTs |
| Botulinum toxin injection | Into LOS; not a long-term solution; significant early failure; reserved for frail patients unsuitable for other modalities |

### Surveillance

Patients with achalasia should undergo endoscopic surveillance because of increased risk of both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Whether successful treatment alters this risk is currently unknown.

## PART B: OTHER MOTILITY DISORDERS

### Diffuse Oesophageal Spasm

- Rare condition; non-peristaltic high-pressure contractions of increased duration in the smooth muscle of the lower two-thirds of the oesophagus
- Symptoms: severe retrosternal pain during swallowing ± dysphagia
- Diagnosis: high-amplitude simultaneous contractions on manometry following an apparently normal swallow; may be seen on barium swallow
- Treatment: calcium channel blockers (nifedipine), nitrates, botulinum toxin — variable results; long oesophageal myotomy rarely performed

### Nutcracker Oesophagus

- Rare condition; severe retrosternal pain during swallowing similar to diffuse oesophageal spasm
- Diagnosis: normal peristalsis with higher than normal amplitude contractions on manometry
- Distinguished from diffuse oesophageal spasm by preserved peristalsis

### Scleroderma Oesophagus

- Part of systemic sclerosis/CREST syndrome
- Smooth muscle fibrosis → absent peristalsis in lower two-thirds of oesophagus
- LOS pressure markedly reduced → severe GORD
- Results in peptic stricture, Barrett's oesophagus
- Management: aggressive PPI therapy; may require dilatation of strictures

## PART C: BARRETT'S OESOPHAGUS AND OESOPHAGEAL CARCINOMA

### Barrett's Oesophagus

- Normal oesophageal stratified squamous epithelium undergoes metaplasia to columnar epithelium
- Results from chronic inflammation secondary to acid, alkaline and bile reflux
- Increasing incidence in Western world; genetic factors may contribute
- Increases risk of developing oesophageal adenocarcinoma
- Formal diagnosis requires two separate specimens reported by a specialist GI pathologist

**Dysplasia classification:**

| Grade | Risk | Management |
|-------|------|------------|
| No dysplasia | Low | Address reflux risk factors; PPI; endoscopic surveillance |
| Low-grade dysplasia | Moderate | Increased surveillance; refer to specialist centre; RFA may be indicated |
| High-grade dysplasia | High (may already harbour invasive adenocarcinoma) | Full staging (CT, EUS, endoscopy); EMR, RFA, or oesophagectomy depending on staging |

**Treatment of high-grade dysplasia:**
- Radiofrequency ablation (RFA): destroys dysplastic epithelium endoscopically; requires flat mucosa with no invasive cancer; long-term results awaited; concerns about occult neoplasia beneath ablated epithelium
- Oesophagectomy: gold standard for high-grade dysplasia; definitive cure; mortality ~1% in specialist centres

### Oesophageal Carcinoma

**Epidemiology:** 8th most common cancer worldwide; incidence ~480,000/year; M:F = 2–4:1; peak 70–74 years

| Subtype | Distribution | Location | Risk Factors |
|---------|-------------|----------|:-------------|
| Adenocarcinoma | Predominates in Western world (>2/3 of oesophageal cancers) | Middle and lower third; OGJ | Obesity, GORD, Barrett's, smoking, alcohol |
| Squamous cell carcinoma | Predominates in Far East | Cervical and upper thoracic | Smoking, high alcohol, low socioeconomic status, hot drinks |

**Staging (TNM 7th edition):**

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| T1a/T1b | Lamina propria/submucosa |
| T2 | Muscularis propria |
| T3 | Adventitia |
| T4a/T4b | Adjacent structures (resectable/unresectable) |
| N1/N2/N3 | 1–2 / 3–6 / >6 regional nodes |

**Management principles:**
- Early disease (cT1/2 N0): EMR for intramucosal disease; oesophagectomy for submucosal involvement
- Locally advanced (T3/4 or N+): Neoadjuvant chemotherapy/chemo[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] → restaging → surgery → consider adjuvant chemotherapy
- Oesophagectomy with radical 2-field lymphadenectomy; in-hospital mortality reduced to 2.9% in UK
- Squamous cell carcinoma: more sensitive to chemoradiotherapy; definitive CRT an option if total disease length ≤10 cm
- Palliative: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, oesophageal stents for dysphagia

## PART D: OESOPHAGEAL PERFORATION AND FOREIGN BODIES

### Oesophageal Perforation

A rare condition with high mortality requiring a high index of suspicion.

**Causes:**
- Spontaneous: Boerhaave syndrome (perforation in otherwise normal oesophagus, usually after forceful vomiting)
- Iatrogenic: therapeutic endoscopy, diagnostic/therapeutic rigid endoscopy, revisional antireflux surgery

**Management depends on contamination and sepsis:**

| Approach | Indication | Details |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| Non-operative | Contained perforation; no systemic sepsis; minimal contamination | NBM, IV fluids, IV PPI, IV antibiotics + antifungals, NG decompression, enteral nutrition (jejunostomy/NJ tube), close monitoring |
| Operative | Significant contamination; systemic sepsis | Repair of defect after thorough debridement and lavage; T-tube for controlled fistula; intercostal muscle flaps for reinforcement |

- Cervical oesophageal perforations: more likely to respond to non-operative management provided no mediastinal contamination
- Oesophageal stents: variable results; unlikely to hold position in normal oesophagus (no stricture to anchor stent); risks of migration, bleeding, stricture

### Foreign Body Ingestion

- Most common in children and psychiatric patients; food bolus in any patient group
- May be associated with underlying Schatzki ring, peptic stricture, eosinophilic oesophagitis, or first presentation of malignant stricture
- Indications for intervention: airway compromise, absolute dysphagia, sharp objects, button batteries, prolonged impaction
- Rigid oesophagoscopy: appropriate for pharynx/cervical oesophagus objects in experienced hands
- Flexible endoscopy: investigation of choice; lower perforation risk; never push food bolus distally without first passing endoscope beyond the bolus

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Achalasia results from degeneration of myenteric plexus ganglia → failure of LOS relaxation + absent oesophageal peristalsis; incidence 1 per 100,000/year
> 2. "Bird's beak" appearance on barium swallow is characteristic of achalasia — dilated non-peristaltic oesophagus tapering at the LOS
> 3. Oesophageal manometry is confirmatory for achalasia; high-resolution manometry identifies subtypes with different treatment responses
> 4. Balloon dilatation and laparoscopic cardiomyotomy have equivalent results at 2 years (93% vs 90% — European RCT); choice depends on available expertise
> 5. POEM (per-oral endoscopic myotomy) is a promising newer technique; botulinum toxin injection to LOS is reserved for frail patients as it has high early failure rates
> 6. Diffuse oesophageal spasm: non-peristaltic high-pressure contractions; nutcracker oesophagus: normal peristalsis with high-amplitude contractions — both cause retrosternal pain
> 7. Barrett's oesophagus: columnar metaplasia from chronic reflux; increases risk of adenocarcinoma; surveillance and treatment based on dysplasia grade
> 8. Oesophageal adenocarcinoma predominates in Western world (>2/3 of cases); squamous cell carcinoma predominates in Far East — different risk factors, locations, and treatment responses
> 9. Oesophageal perforation requires high index of suspicion; management depends on degree of contamination and sepsis; Boerhaave syndrome is spontaneous perforation
> 10. Achalasia patients require long-term endoscopic surveillance due to increased risk of both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Achalasia causes dysphagia to both liquids AND solids from the outset — this distinguishes it from mechanical obstruction (e.g., stricture, tumour) where dysphagia to solids precedes liquids
> - Always perform endoscopy BEFORE diagnosing achalasia to exclude pseudoachalasia (cancer mimicking achalasia at the OGJ)
> - Eosinophilic oesophagitis may present as recurrent food bolus impaction — look for fixed rings, furrows, and exudates on endoscopy; biopsy to confirm eosinophilic infiltration
> - Oesophageal stents should NOT be inserted in operable tumours — they cause transmural inflammation that renders the cancer incurable and increases perforation risk
> - In-hospital mortality for oesophagectomy has reduced to 2.9% in the UK through centralization to specialist oesophagogastric centres — volume-outcome relationship is well established
