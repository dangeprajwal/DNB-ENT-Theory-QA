# Tracheostomy — Indications, Techniques, Complications and Paediatric Considerations

**Question:** *Tracheostomy techniques specific to children. Indications, technique, problems and complications of tracheostomy in infants. Post-operative care and complications in paediatric tracheostomy. Difficult decannulation in a child — evaluation and management. Prolonged tracheostomy in a child — causes, sequelae and investigations. Percutaneous vs classical tracheostomy. RLN anatomy on both sides.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 35 (Paediatric Tracheostomy) and Volume 3, Chapter 72 (Upper Airway Obstruction and Tracheostomy)

---

## Introduction

Tracheostomy is one of the oldest documented surgical procedures. Paediatric tracheostomy has evolved significantly — once commonly performed for acute laryngeal infections (croup, epiglottitis), it is now largely undertaken for congenital airway obstruction, prolonged ventilation, and complex medical conditions. In a series of 153 paediatric tracheostomies, Line et al. reported that prior to 1980, 38% were performed for acute airway infections; after 1980 this figure had dropped to 12%. Currently virtually no tracheostomies are performed for acute airway infections alone. Tracheostomy in children is now an uncommon operation — a US survey of 2065 tracheostomies estimates a rate of 6.6 per 100,000 child years, with the highest incidence in the first year of life and a second peak in the late teens due to trauma. The indications, technique, complications and post-operative care differ substantially between adults and children. Tracheostomy-related mortality in children ranges from 0% to 3.6% in published series, with tube obstruction and accidental decannulation being the leading causes. Non-tracheostomy-related mortality (underlying medical conditions) is consistently high at 7–36% in all series, reflecting the complex medical conditions of children requiring tracheostomy.

## PART A: Definitions, Effects and Indications

### Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Tracheotomy | A surgical opening in the trachea |
| Tracheostomy | Creation of a stoma at the skin surface leading into the tracheal lumen |

This difference has become an important distinction as surgical technique in children's tracheostomies has evolved — a true stoma is now fashioned using maturation sutures.

### Effects of Tracheostomy

- Laryngeal bypass — loss of cough and phonation
- Reduction in respiratory dead space
- Loss of nasal mucosal filtration and humidification
- Increased risk of infection
- Tube acts as foreign body causing local inflammation
- Sump above tracheostome and below larynx where mucus collects

### Indications

**General indications for tracheostomy:**
- To relieve upper airway obstruction
- To prevent complications of prolonged intubation
- To reduce anatomical dead space
- To allow suction toilet of the trachea
- As part of another procedure (laryngectomy, major oropharyngeal resection)

**Adult indications:**

- **Upper airway obstruction** — tumour, infection, trauma, bilateral [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal cord palsy]]. Tracheostomy is the ultimate form of airway control.
- **Prolonged ventilation** — the commonest indication for percutaneous tracheostomy in the ICU. The reduced dead space assists weaning of respiratory support. Early tracheostomy in trauma patients reduces length of ventilation and hospital stay.
- **Removal of secretions** — tracheostomy reduces dead space and makes aspiration of secretions easier with less patient discomfort.
- **Part of another procedure** — permanent tracheostomy after [[Laryngectomy Rehabilitation Voice Prosthesis|total laryngectomy]]; temporary tracheostomy for open oropharyngeal or laryngeal resections to protect against post-operative swelling/bleeding and aspiration.

**Paediatric indications (current):**

| Indication | Percentage |
|---|---|
| Cardiopulmonary disease | 34% (commonest) |
| Neurological impairment | 32% |
| Airway obstruction | 19% |
| Other (ventilation, toilet) | 15% |

**Airway obstruction — examples potentially requiring tracheostomy:**

| Anatomical Site | Example |
|---|---|
| Oropharynx, tongue base | Macroglossia, Treacher Collins/Goldenhar, cystic hygroma |
| Nose, nasopharynx | Choanal atresia |
| Supraglottis | Supraglottic cyst |
| Glottis | Vocal cord palsy, physical trauma |
| Subglottis | [[Subglottic Stenosis|Subglottic stenosis]], haemangioma |
| Trachea | Tracheomalacia, high tracheal stenosis |

**Prolonged intubation:** The long-term complications of prolonged endotracheal intubation are well recognized — ulceration at the level of the glottis and subglottis can lead to cicatrization and stenosis. The neonatal larynx, being softer and more flexible, tolerates intubation for relatively longer periods than the adult. Premature babies may be intubated for several weeks before damage becomes a risk. In older children, tracheostomy should normally be considered after 2–3 weeks of endotracheal intubation, although there is no clear consensus on maximum safe duration.

**Long-term and home ventilation:** An increasing number of children now survive previously lethal conditions. In a 2011 UK survey, out of 933 home-ventilated children, 206 had tracheostomies. Indications include congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, spinal injury, congenital myopathy, airway malacia and chronic lung disease. The demands on the tracheostomy are higher and there is a higher risk of complications in home-ventilated children.

**Tracheal toilet:** Very few children now require tracheostomy purely for airway toilet. The presence of a tracheostomy can itself predispose to aspiration and increase the risk of respiratory tract infection.

**Changing indications in children:** Tracheostomy for acute airway infections has fallen from 38% (pre-1980) to 12% (post-1980) and virtually zero currently. Endotracheal intubation has replaced tracheostomy for epiglottitis and croup. Single-stage LTR and cricoid split have reduced the need for tracheostomy in SGS. Propranolol has largely eliminated tracheostomy for subglottic haemangioma.

## PART B: Surgical Technique — Open (Classical) Tracheostomy

### Open Surgical Tracheostomy (Adult)

**Positioning:** Supine, neck extended over sandbag or pillow under shoulders. Patient positioned square on table with shoulders at the same level — this ensures midline structures are truly in the midline throughout the operation. Under local anaesthesia, over-extension may be necessary to compromise on to avoid further restricting the airway.

**Incision:** Horizontal, sited halfway between the sternal notch and the lower border of the cricoid cartilage.

**Dissection:** Through subcutaneous tissues to strap muscles, which are separated in the midline by blunt dissection and retracted laterally. The thyroid isthmus is exposed, clamped, divided and transfixed.

**Tracheal entry:** The cricoid cartilage is identified to plan the point of entry. The tracheotomy should ideally be made between the 2nd and 4th tracheal rings. Before entering the trachea, an appropriately sized tracheostomy tube is selected and the cuff and all connecting equipment checked so ventilation can continue uninterrupted. Good communication between surgeon and anaesthetist is essential. A vertical slit between stay sutures is made — this causes least disruption to the trachea and maintains cartilage integrity. Avoid damage to the cricoid cartilage. The anaesthetist withdraws the ETT under the surgeon's direct vision until the tip is just above the tracheotomy; the tracheostomy tube is then inserted.

**Tube selection at operation:** The tube placed at the time of tracheostomy should be cuffed to allow ventilation and prevent aspiration of blood/secretions. An inner tube should ideally be available for easy clearing of secretions in the immediate post-operative period.

**Closure:** Incision closed loosely. The tube is secured with tapes, sutures or both. Tapes fastened with a secure knot on both sides of the neck, with the neck in a neutral position (not extended, or tapes will be too long).

### Paediatric-Specific Modifications

**Vertical skin incision:** Now standard practice in most major children's units. Advantages over horizontal incision: keeps dissection in the midline, reducing tendency to stray away from the trachea in a difficult dissection; allows easier placement of maturation sutures. The incision is placed halfway between the cricoid ring and sternal notch. The incision need not be much longer than what allows insertion of the selected tube.

**Removal of subcutaneous fat and maturation sutures:** A disc of subcutaneous fat immediately surrounding the incision is removed after completing the skin incision. This allows the skin edges to invert slightly to create a stoma lined with healthy squamous epithelium. This effect is increased by suturing the edge of the skin incision to the edge of the tracheal incision (maturation sutures) using absorbable sutures (e.g. 4/0 Vicryl Rapide). The resulting opening is more secure and tends to stay open even without the tube in situ — a true surgically fashioned stoma rather than an incision held open only by the tube itself. This dramatically reduces the risk of a false passage at an emergency post-operative tube change. It has been disproven in larger series (n > 100) that stomal maturation sutures increase the risk of tracheocutaneous fistula formation after decannulation.

**Stay sutures:** Placed in the tracheal wall on either side of the vertical tracheal incision using removable suture (e.g. 4/0 Prolene). Taped to the chest wall. In accidental decannulation, upward and lateral traction on the sutures opens the tracheostomy for easier tube reinsertion. Left in situ until the first tube change.

**Tracheal incision:** Vertical, in midline, tracheal rings 3–4. The cricoid cartilage must be identified first before the incision is made. Avoid removing any cartilaginous tissue — animal experiments suggest tracheal flaps may lead to an increased risk of long-term stenosis. Too high an incision in the trachea predisposes to subglottic stenosis. The simple vertical incision is associated with the lowest risk of long-term complications.

**Dissection:** Monopolar or bipolar diathermy to minimize blood loss. In infants, the thyroid isthmus is usually divided with bipolar diathermy (not clamped and transfixed as in adults). Regular palpation of the trachea throughout dissection to ensure midline orientation. Given the relatively small size of the infant neck and trachea, and the relative proximity of the carotid sheath, straying from the midline must be avoided. Extension of the neck in infants theoretically increases the risk of injury to the great vessels in the root of the anterior neck, but this is rarely a problem with careful dissection. Overextension risks exposing intrathoracic trachea, leading to an incision that is too low.

**Securing tubes:** Inelastic linen tapes initially, tied with neck flexed (not extended — if the neck is extended when tapes are fastened, the tapes will be too long and the tube may be coughed out). Allow one finger between tapes and neck skin. Do NOT suture flanges to skin in children — infant skin is too thin and flexible to provide reliable anchorage. Velcro fastening after first tube change at 7 days, which allows easier changing and is less traumatic to the neck skin. The tapes may be threaded inside a sleeve of Tubigrip or large plastic tubing to protect neck skin from ulceration.

## PART C: Percutaneous Tracheostomy and Comparison with Open

### Percutaneous Tracheostomy (Adult)

First described more than 50 years ago. Now the commonest procedure for provision of an alternative airway for ICU patients. The growth followed the introduction of commercial kits based on well-defined techniques. The most commonly employed kit in the UK uses the dilatational technique originally described by Ciaglia. The introduction of percutaneous tracheostomy has led to a doubling in the number of tracheostomies being carried out — it may be that the ease of access has resulted in reduced duration of intubation.

**Technique:**
1. Patient positioned as for open surgical tracheostomy (supine, neck extended)
2. Trachea punctured just below the first tracheal cartilage ring with needle and cannula
3. Syringe half-filled with saline attached — air aspirated through saline confirms correct tracheal entry
4. Needle withdrawn, guide wire inserted through cannula
5. Cannula withdrawn; either a single dilator or a series of dilators with increasing diameter passed over guide wire
6. Dilators create a passage wide enough for a standard tracheostomy tube
7. Tube inserted and secured in position
8. Bronchoscopic guidance is advisable — viewing the internal tracheal lumen during the procedure may reduce complications

### Percutaneous vs Classical (Open) Tracheostomy

| Feature | Percutaneous | Open Surgical |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | ICU bedside | Operating theatre |
| Anaesthesia | Local + sedation/GA | GA (or local if awake) |
| Commonest indication | Prolonged ventilation in ICU | All indications |
| Paediatric use | Not standard | Standard |
| Post-major HN surgery | Not appropriate | Appropriate |
| Wound infection rate | Lower | Higher |
| Cosmetic result | Better | Variable |
| Time to perform | Shorter | Longer |
| Bronchoscopic guidance | Advisable | Not required |
| Risk of tube displacement | Higher (tight pre-tracheal tissues) | Lower |
| Training opportunities | Common (ICU setting) | Decreasing (NCEPOD report) |

**Why percutaneous is NOT appropriate after major HN surgery:** The pre-tracheal tissues fit tightly around the tube after percutaneous insertion. In the event of tube displacement, the tissues can collapse inwards, closing the airway with a potentially fatal outcome. Open surgical tracheostomy allows a more secure stoma.

**Why percutaneous is NOT standard in children:** The paediatric airway is smaller, landmarks are less reliable, and the requirement for a mature, secure stoma with stay sutures and maturation sutures makes the open surgical technique mandatory.

**NCEPOD "On the right trach?" report:** The majority of open tracheostomies are performed as part of another surgical procedure (major HN resection) or in "unfavourable" patients for percutaneous. Consequently, training opportunities for open tracheostomy are decreasing.

**Contraindications to tracheostomy:** There are no absolute contraindications. However, in patients with a terminal prognosis, very careful consideration must be given to the psychological effect and quality of life aspects.

## PART D: Tracheostomy Tubes in Children

### Sizing

Tubes are sized by internal diameter (mm) and length (mm). A chart relating age to appropriate tube size should be available in every unit. The tube should sit comfortably proximal to the carina — at least 2 cm inside stoma, 1–2 cm clear of carina. Tube tip position can be assessed on chest X-ray, at regular rigid bronchoscopy or by passing a flexible endoscope down the tube lumen. A child with a long-standing tracheostomy should undergo regular age-appropriate "upsizing." Too large a tube will predispose to suprastomal collapse and may increase the risk of granulation and stenosis. In some cases a smaller tube allowing air leakage around the tube on expiration aids normal speech production.

### Approximate Guide to Tube Size

| Age | Tube ID (mm) |
|---|---|
| Preterm – 1 month | 2.5–3.0 |
| 1–6 months | 3.0–3.5 |
| 6–18 months | 3.5–4.0 |
| 18 months – 3 years | 4.0–4.5 |
| 3–6 years | 4.5–5.0 |
| 6–9 years | 5.0–5.5 |
| 9–12 years | 5.5–6.0 |
| 12–14 years | 6.0–7.0 |

### Material

Soft or siliconized PVC is now the most widely used material. Materials may be hydrophobic to reduce deposition of secretions. The flexibility of newer tubes reduces the risk of mucosal trauma during neck movements and the soft flange is less likely to cause skin injury. Metal tubes are largely superseded — one advantage was that a thinner wall allowed a larger lumen for the same outside diameter, permitting inner tubes to be used.

### Types

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Common tubes (UK) | Shiley and Bivona |
| Inner tube | Impractical in small tubes — wall thickness reduces lumen. Only practical in largest children's tubes |
| Fenestration | Impractical in small children — fenestration becomes a focus for granulation and mucosal trauma on suctioning. Air leakage around a smaller tube achieves adequate phonation |
| Cuff | Rarely needed in children (see below) |

**Specialised tubes:**

- **Bivona Flextend:** Silicone tube with a longer external component than standard — allows greater flexibility in mechanically ventilated children. Particularly useful in very small infants with a limited gap between chin and chest wall. Disadvantage: increased dead space and obstruction risk from longer length.
- **Bivona Hyperflex:** Adjustable flange allowing variable intra-tracheal length. Useful where a low tracheal obstruction needs to be bypassed and a standard tube cannot be used. Disadvantage: expensive, thick wall resulting in relatively small lumen.
- **Custom-made tubes:** Available for children with specific anatomical difficulties; manufacturers should be approached directly.

### Cuff Indications in Children

Traditionally cuffed tubes are rarely indicated in paediatric practice. Until adolescence, a sufficient seal to allow positive pressure ventilation can normally be achieved with an uncuffed tube. Two specific indications:

1. Significant aspiration risk (although a cuff will not completely protect against aspiration)
2. Decreased lung compliance requiring raised ventilation pressures temporarily (e.g. intercurrent infection in a ventilated child)

The presence of a cuff increases the risk of mucosal ischaemia and tracheal stenosis, particularly if high cuff pressures are employed. In the last decade there has been an increase in the use of cuffed tubes in paediatric ICUs — it is not yet clear if this will increase the rate of subglottic and tracheal stenosis.

### Speaking Valves

One-way valves that allow inhalation through the tube but force air upwards through the glottis on exhalation, creating sufficient subglottic pressure for phonation. Benefits include improved lung function and reduced aspiration. It is beneficial for a child to get used to expiration through the normal airway before decannulation.

| Valve | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Passy-Muir | Closed except during inspiration — encourages use of normal airway even in quiet respiration |
| Rusch | Open except during significant expiration |

Speaking valves should be used under supervision, not while the child is asleep. Small infants may not tolerate the valve and learn to blow it off by coughing. The Passy-Muir valve may be modified by drilling small holes to allow some expired air to escape. Many children learn to occlude the end of the tube by flexing the neck to achieve the same phonatory effect.

## PART E: Post-Operative Care

### Immediate Post-Operative Period

The first 2–3 post-operative days are critical. The tract can close very quickly in the early period, making reinsertion difficult. It is difficult to overstate the importance of tracheostomy nursing care — with meticulous and skilled care, most complications can be avoided. Inexperienced staff are often reluctant to intervene. Hospitals must maintain a high standard of internal training, and many units have specific teams and regular training programmes.

**Suction:** Immediately after tracheostomy, the change from warmed, humidified upper airway air to dry, cold air leads to a rapid increase in secretions. This gradually reduces after a few weeks. Secretions dry on the inside of the tube and reduce the effective lumen. Suctioning is required as often as necessary to keep the tube and airway clear. Insert the suction catheter only as far as the tip of the tracheostomy tube and withdraw with a finger occluding the side port. The exact distance should be measured and marked on the suction tube. Overzealous suctioning causes mucosal trauma in the distal trachea, leading to granulation formation at the tube tip and potential tube obstruction. Irrigation with sterile saline to loosen secretions prior to suctioning is often advocated but there is little evidence to support this and it may increase contamination of the lower airway.

**Humidification:** Essential — initially via nebulizers and tracheostomy mask. After a few weeks, secretions reduce and less humidification is required. Longer-term options include a Swedish nose (heat and moisture exchanger/HME) which contains a filter that becomes saturated by moisture in exhaled air, humidifying inspired air in turn. Tracheostomy bibs work similarly. Both devices also act as filters for inspired air.

**Skin care:** The wound heals by secondary intention and eventually the tract becomes lined with squamous epithelium and organized scar tissue. Maturation sutures hasten the development of an epithelialized tract (a "mature" stoma). Until this point there is usually considerable discharge from the wound. A dry gauze or foam dressing (e.g. Lyofoam) is placed between peristomal skin and tube flange, changed regularly. Changing dressings increases the risk of accidental decannulation — adequate training is essential. Large skin incisions that gap will break down; closure is futile due to bacterial colonization, and wound products similar to a healing ulcer are needed. Tapes can cause ulceration if too tight or left too long.

**First tube change:** At approximately day 7, by an otolaryngologist. This allows time for stoma maturation but is short enough to reduce risk of tube obstruction from dried secretions. In children who are difficult to intubate (retrognathia, laryngeal stenosis), the first change should be done in the operating theatre with full surgical backup. If oral intubation is easy, the first change is done on ICU with an intubation trolley and senior ITU staff. If uneventful, nursing staff can carry out subsequent changes. Stay sutures removed after first successful change. Parents must be taught tube changing technique in a secure environment before discharge home.

**Tube change frequency:** No standard interval — varies between children and with season and lower airway health. The tube needs changing before dried secretions start to reduce the lumen. The removed tube should be inspected for degree of contamination. If the tube visibly contains dried secretion, has an audible whistle due to obstruction ("if you can hear a tube, you should change it"), or if the suction catheter cannot pass, the tube must be changed. If the suction catheter still does not pass freely after changing the tube, an otolaryngologist should be sought. Maximum recommended interval: 28 days.

**Cuff management (adults):** The cuff should be deflated once aspiration risk has passed. In most cases, there is no need for cuff inflation after the first 12 hours.

**Swallowing:** Swallowing difficulties are common after tracheostomy — due to cuff pressure on the upper oesophagus and reduced laryngeal elevation from the tethering effect of the tube.

**Monitoring:** If doubts about tube position or lumen obstruction arise, a flexible nasal endoscope can usually be passed through the tube to inspect the lumen and trachea.

## PART F: Complications of Tracheostomy

### Classification (Paediatric)

| Timing | Complications |
|---|---|
| General (any time) | Tube obstruction, accidental decannulation, death |
| Early (<1 week) | Haemorrhage, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema, infection, apnoea |
| Late (>1 week) | Granulation (peristomal & suprastomal), bleeding, suprastomal collapse, skin complications, aphonia/speech delay, psychological effects, adverse effects on family |

### Adult Complications (by timing)

| Timing | Complications |
|---|---|
| Immediate | Haemorrhage (commonest fatal complication), air embolism, local damage to carotid sheath/oesophagus/RLN/cricoid cartilage |
| Intermediate | Accidental extubation, tube obstruction, subcutaneous emphysema, infection, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, tracheo-arterial fistula |
| Late | Tracheocutaneous fistula, tracheal stenosis |

### Complication Rates (Paediatric)

Overall complication rates: 25–77% in paediatric series. Tracheostomy-related mortality: 0–3.6%. Complications are more common in:

- Children under 2 years of age, particularly preterm infants
- Emergency procedures
- Longer duration of tracheostomy
- The smaller airway diameter in young children means it is more easily occluded by secretions, granulation or suprastomal collapse

Non-tracheostomy-related mortality (underlying medical conditions): 7–36%.

### Key Complications in Detail

**Accidental decannulation:** Can be fatal if no natural airway above the tracheostomy or if the child is ventilator-dependent. Risk factors: insufficiently tight ties, too short a tube, excessive traction from ventilator tubing. In the first few days before the tract matures, tube reinsertion is harder. Wetmore et al. report accidental decannulation in 29 of 420 (6.9%) children in the first week. Risk reduced by meticulous nursing care; surgical techniques (stay sutures, maturation sutures) reduce morbidity by allowing easier and safer reinsertion. In the event of accidental decannulation, the tube must be reintroduced in a controlled manner to prevent a false passage. In adults, if the tube is displaced into the pre-tracheal space, this may not become immediately apparent — the patient continues to breathe as soft tissues gradually prolapse around the tracheal opening. Dyspnoea slowly increases and by the time displacement is apparent, the tube may be impossible to replace.

**Tube obstruction:** The most common cause of tracheostomy-related death. Initially from dried secretions; later from granulation at the tube tip. Prevention: regular suction, humidification. In adults, an inner tube can be removed, cleaned and replaced without disruption to the airway.

**Pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum, surgical emphysema:** In infants, the domes of the pleura extend well into the neck. Straying from the midline increases the risk. Small pneumothoraces are treated conservatively; larger ones require chest drainage. Rare in elective tracheostomy, more common in emergency. If the wound is closed too tightly around the tube or dressings are too tight, air may leak into the soft tissues (surgical emphysema) or track down into the mediastinum. Treatment: reopen wound, insert corrugated drain. A routine chest X-ray is standard after paediatric tracheostomy to check tube position and exclude pneumothorax.

**Haemorrhage:** Early bleeding usually from failure of haemostasis — wound edge, anterior jugular veins or thyroid isthmus. If direct pressure fails, pack with haemostatic gauze (Surgicel, Kaltostat). Re-exploration is rarely required. Later, minor bleeding from granulation — controlled with cautery, steroid/antibiotic ointment.

**Tracheo-innominate artery fistula:** Rare but lethal (adult estimate 0.4%). The innominate artery crosses the front of the trachea from left to right at a variable height. In some children the artery lies abnormally high in the neck. If found at surgery: reconsider tracheostomy; if no safe alternative, place a higher tracheal incision accepting the risk of SGS. An abnormally low tracheostomy also increases the risk. Fistula forms from erosion of arterial wall by direct tube pressure. Presentation: sudden massive haemorrhage without premonitory signs (any bleeding from the tracheostomy tube itself that appears to arise from the anterior tracheal wall rather than tube-tip granulation must be investigated). Management: change immediately to cuffed tube and inflate to prevent aspiration; apply compression via tracheostome; establish endotracheal intubation if possible; immediate surgical exploration, ideally with cardiothoracic assistance. Mortality remains very high.

**Granulation:** The tracheostomy tube as a foreign body and persistent bacterial flora are an ongoing stimulus for granulation tissue. Granulation forms at three sites:

1. **Peristomal granulation** (skin edge) — controlled with topical steroid/antibiotic preparations; when severe, removed with bipolar diathermy. Avoid silver nitrate cautery near the stoma as solution can enter the trachea causing mucosal injury.
2. **Suprastomal granulation** (anterior tracheal wall above tube) — almost universal (80% at bronchoscopy). Most are left unless causing obstruction or hindering tube changes. At pre-decannulation MLB, all granulation should be removed. Removal methods: microlaryngeal instruments, microdebrider (Skimmer or Tru-Cut blade), KTP laser (advantage: flexible fibre delivery in the limited confines of the subglottis), CO2 laser. Large granulation may be removed using sinus surgery backbiting forceps inserted through the tracheostome under endoscopic guidance. Alternatively, if the child can be intubated normally, the ET tube pushes the granulation externally out of the stoma where it can be excised by sharp dissection.
3. **Tube-tip granulation** (distal trachea) — from suction catheter trauma or direct tube trauma.

**Suprastomal collapse:** Distinct from suprastomal granulation though the two often coexist. The anterior tracheal wall immediately superior to the stoma softens and prolapses into the lumen of the subglottic trachea. This significantly reduces the available airway, increasing the risks of accidental decannulation and leading to decannulation failure. Treatment options depend on degree:

- Minor collapse — may be left, tends to improve after decannulation
- Moderate — neck exploration with suture through the cartilage and around strap muscles to elevate the collapsed segment, combined with excision and transfixion of the tracheostome skin
- KTP laser ablation of the collapsed segment
- Severe (>50% collapse) — supporting the collapsed segment with a cartilage graft reconstruction
- Simplest: excision and transfixion of tract followed by endotracheal intubation for 2–3 days to support the trachea as the stoma heals

**Speech development:** Tracheostomy adversely affects speech development. Normal phonation is impaired as insufficient subglottic pressure is generated. Small infants tend not to tolerate speaking valves well. A significant proportion of tracheostomized children have coexisting developmental abnormalities. If decannulation occurs within the first 12–18 months (before normal speech patterns develop), the long-term outcome is favourable. Longer-term tracheostomy may lead to longer-term speech impairment.

**Effects on caregivers and family:** Caring for a tracheostomized child puts significant strain on carers and families. Demands on caregiver time may be exhausting and prevent engagement in employment. Mental health status and socioeconomic status are adversely affected. Community nursing support in the UK is very variable and dependent on local healthcare policy.

**Tracheo-oesophageal fistula (adult):** Results from intra-operative damage to the posterior tracheal wall or persistent rubbing of the tube tip on the posterior wall. Presents with aspiration despite an inflated cuff.

**Tracheal stenosis:** Almost always from damage to the cricoid or first tracheal ring at surgery, or from mucosal damage by a poorly positioned tube.

## PART G: Prolonged Tracheostomy — Causes, Sequelae, Investigations

### Causes of Prolonged Tracheostomy in Children

- Unresolved or persistent upper airway obstruction (SGS, bilateral VCP, tracheomalacia)
- Craniofacial syndromes (Treacher Collins, Pierre Robin, Goldenhar)
- Neuromuscular disease requiring long-term ventilation (congenital myopathy, congenital central hypoventilation syndrome)
- Chronic lung disease (bronchopulmonary dysplasia)
- Failed decannulation attempts (suprastomal collapse, residual obstruction)
- Ventilator dependency (spinal injury)

### Sequelae of Prolonged Tracheostomy

- **Speech and language delay** — impaired phonation; if decannulation within 12–18 months, long-term outcome favourable
- **Suprastomal granulation and collapse** — almost universal; increases with duration
- **Tracheal stenosis** — from cuff pressure or tube erosion
- **Tracheocutaneous fistula** — 19–42% after decannulation; risk factors include younger age and longer duration
- **Recurrent lower respiratory tract infections** — tube predisposes to aspiration and bacterial contamination
- **Psychological impact** on child and family — "decannulation panic," anxiety, social isolation
- **Adverse effects on carers** — mental health deterioration, socioeconomic disadvantage, exhaustion, inability to engage in employment
- **Skin complications** — neck skin ulceration from tapes, peristomal breakdown
- **Need for regular upsizing** — a child outgrows tube sizes

### Investigations for Prolonged Tracheostomy

- **MLB (essential):** Assess suprastomal granulation/collapse, subglottic airway patency, vocal cord mobility, tracheal pathology. All granulation should be removed. Suprastomal collapse >50% requires cartilage graft reconstruction.
- **Flexible endoscopy through tube lumen:** Inspect tube lumen patency, tube tip position, carina, and tracheal mucosa. Identifies displacement, obstruction from crusting/granulation, or poor tube tip placement against tracheal wall.
- **Chest X-ray:** Tube position confirmation (tip 1–2 cm above carina) and lung pathology assessment.
- **Sleep study:** If suspected upper airway obstruction above the tracheostomy or to assess readiness for decannulation.
- **Assessment of comorbidities:** Pulmonary function, neurological status, need for further surgery (e.g. mandibular advancement, cleft palate repair) — if procedures that may temporarily compromise the airway are planned, decannulation should be deferred.

## PART H: Difficult Decannulation — Evaluation and Management

### Decision to Decannulate

Decannulation may be considered when the original condition requiring tracheostomy has improved. The child must be able to maintain an adequate airway without the tracheostomy. The majority of paediatric tracheostomies are short term — the natural airway tends to improve with overall growth of the child or as a result of corrective surgery such as laryngotracheal reconstruction. The decision is a complicated one requiring a senior clinician after careful discussion with parents and relevant healthcare professionals.

### Pre-Decannulation Assessment

Endoscopic assessment of the airway is **essential** before definitive decannulation. Prescott found suprastomal granulation in 50 and significant suprastomal collapse in 52 of 300 tracheostomies — these are the most common causes of decannulation failure.

**MLB should assess:**

- Suprastomal granulation — remove at endoscopy using punch forceps or laser ablation
- Suprastomal collapse — if >50%, requires KTP laser ablation or reconstructive surgery using cartilage grafting
- Subglottic airway patency
- Vocal cord mobility
- Tracheal pathology

**Additionally consider:**
- Comorbidities (pulmonary, neurological disease)
- Need for further surgery that may compromise the airway (mandibular advancement, cleft palate repair) — decannulate only after these treatments are complete
- If the subglottic airway is satisfactory at endoscopy, proceed to formal decannulation in the next few days

### Causes of Difficult Decannulation

- Suprastomal collapse (most common cause)
- Suprastomal granulation
- Residual subglottic or tracheal stenosis
- Bilateral vocal cord palsy
- Tracheomalacia
- Psychological dependency ("decannulation panic") — with long-standing tracheostomy, the child may have no memory of mouth/nose breathing and the new sensation may be distressing

### Staged Decannulation Protocol (Great Ormond Street)

| Day | Procedure |
|---|---|
| 1 | Admission, downsize to 3.0 tube (2.5 if <13 months) |
| 2 | Block tube for 12 hours from 8 am; if successful, continue overnight for a further 12 hours |
| 3 | Decannulate, occlude stoma with adhesive tape and dressing. Observe on ward |
| 4 | Observe off the ward |
| 5 | Discharge |

**Rationale:** The tracheostomy tube occupies a significant fraction of the tracheal lumen — up to half in an infant. To assess whether the child can breathe through the normal anatomical airway, the tube is capped (with a button, tape, or obturator). To reduce the effect of the tube occupying the lumen, the tube size is reduced (downsizing). Leaving the small tube in situ allows some respiration if required and also prevents the tract from closing down should decannulation fail. Removal of the tracheostomy leads to a significant change in physiology — the dead space is doubled and airway resistance is trebled.

### Immediate Decannulation

If a child can tolerate the tube occupying half the tracheal lumen (i.e. tolerate a capped tube), the airway after decannulation is likely to be more than sufficient. However, some children cannot tolerate this degree of obstruction. In this case, the tube may simply be removed and the stoma occluded with a dressing. **This must be done in a controlled setting (ICU) with facilities for intubation available.** Contraindicated if oral intubation is not possible (e.g. Treacher Collins syndrome) — decannulation should be delayed until the child is large enough for staged decannulation with capping.

### Post-Decannulation Care

Once the tube is removed, an airtight dressing must be applied to occlude the stoma. Several gauze squares covered with an occlusive dressing are usually sufficient. The dressing should be changed whenever an air leak appears to improve the chance of full closure.

### Tracheocutaneous Fistula

Incidence: 19–42% after decannulation. Risk factors:

- Lower age at initial tracheostomy
- Longer duration of tracheostomy
- Persistent obstruction above the level of the stoma (e.g. inadequate reconstruction of SGS)

Allow 6–12 months for spontaneous closure before considering surgery. It is essential to reassess the upper airway prior to closure — some children still use the fistula as an accessory airway if the original obstruction is unresolved. The persistence of squamous epithelium lining the tract increases likelihood of persistent fistula.

**Closure options:**

- **Secondary closure (closure by secondary intention):** Surgically removing the skin lining the tract, or cauterizing using diathermy, coblation or trichloroacetic acid, may lead to scarring and satisfactory closure
- **Primary closure (more common):** The tract of the stoma is dissected down to the level of the trachea and closed with transfixion sutures. The strap muscles are reapposed (fills the cosmetic defect). The scarred skin surrounding the tracheostome is excised in a fusiform horizontal incision with wide undermining of surrounding skin and horizontal skin closure. Deep dermal/platysmal sutures support the wound. A drain is left for 24 hours in case of air leak (which might otherwise cause surgical emphysema and pneumomediastinum). Flexing the neck aids closure of large skin defects.

A recent systematic review found no difference in outcome or complication between primary and secondary closure techniques.

### Revision of Tracheostomy Scar

Fusiform horizontal excision of scarred skin, wide undermining of surrounding skin, identification and reapposition of strap muscles in midline to eliminate neck contour defect, deep dermal/platysmal sutures, meticulous skin closure.

## PART I: Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Anatomy — Relevance to Tracheostomy

### Left RLN

The left RLN arises from the vagus nerve in the thorax. It loops under the aortic arch (ligamentum arteriosum) and ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove on the left side. It enters the larynx posterior to the cricothyroid joint, deep to the inferior constrictor muscle. Its longer intrathoracic course makes it more vulnerable to injury from aortic arch pathology, mediastinal lymphadenopathy and cardiothoracic surgery. In relation to the inferior thyroid artery on the left, the nerve more commonly lies posterior to the artery.

### Right RLN

The right RLN arises from the vagus at the level of the right subclavian artery, loops under the subclavian artery and ascends in the tracheo-oesophageal groove on the right side. Its shorter course and more lateral trajectory make it slightly less vulnerable than the left, but it is more at risk during thyroid surgery due to its close relationship with the inferior thyroid artery — the nerve may pass anterior, posterior, or through the branches of the artery. On the right, there is an equal chance of any of these three relationships. Berry's ligament (the posterior suspensory ligament of the thyroid gland) is a key danger point. A non-recurrent right laryngeal nerve occurs when the proximal portion of the right fourth arch artery disappears — associated with an aberrant right subclavian artery (arteria lusoria).

### Relevance to Tracheostomy

The RLN runs in the tracheo-oesophageal groove — lateral to the trachea. During tracheostomy, straying from the midline risks injury to the RLN and also to the great vessels within the carotid sheath. In obese patients and small children, anatomical landmarks may be lost and lateral straying is more likely. An inexperienced surgeon may inadvertently stray from the midline and cause damage to the contents of the carotid sheath, oesophagus or recurrent laryngeal nerve. The thyroid isthmus crosses the front of the trachea covering a variable number of cartilaginous rings — it is a highly vascular structure and inadvertent damage during dissection or friction from the tube may result in post-operative haemorrhage.

## PART J: Tracheostomy Safety, Discharge and Home Care

### Safety Initiatives

The majority of life-threatening tracheostomy-specific complications (tube displacement and blockage) should be avoidable with correct care and, if recognized quickly, should be rapidly treatable. The UK National Tracheostomy Safety Project (multidisciplinary collaboration) and the Global Tracheostomy Collaborative (formed 2012) have developed recommendations:

1. **Emergency equipment box:** Must accompany the tracheostomy patient at all times — spare tubes (same size and one size smaller), suction catheters, dressings
2. **Bedhead documentation:** Displayed at all times providing tube size, length, existing upper airway abnormalities, suction catheter size and depth
3. **Emergency treatment algorithms:** Provided for attending resuscitation teams — stepwise approach: suction to assess patency → emergency tube change (same size → smaller size → smaller tube over suction catheter) → remove tube if unsuccessful → assess breathing → rescue breaths → CPR

### Discharge and Home Care

Getting home with a tracheostomy is a complex and time-consuming process. Not all families have sufficient support or resources. While in hospital, caregivers must be educated in tube changing, recognition and initial treatment of complications, and basic life-support training. Generally two responsible adults are required for tube change.

**Requirements include:** Appropriate-sized tubes (same and one smaller), neck ties, scissors, lubricant, sterile saline, dressings, gauze, suction catheters, HMEs/Swedish noses, speaking valves, gloves/aprons, stethoscope, two suction machines (one portable), saturation monitor, ambu bag, oxygen (concentrator or cylinders). Additional for home ventilation: two ventilators/CPAP, disposable circuits, humidifier, CO2 monitor, uninterrupted power supply.

With sufficient support and education, tracheostomized children without other significant disabilities can attend mainstream schooling, though certain activities must be avoided (swimming, water-based sports, contact sports).

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Current paediatric tracheostomy indications have shifted from acute infections to cardiopulmonary disease (34%), neurological impairment (32%) and airway obstruction (19%) — virtually no tracheostomies are now performed for croup or epiglottitis alone
> 2. Paediatric technique differs from adult: vertical skin incision, maturation sutures (skin to tracheal wall with 4/0 Vicryl Rapide), stay sutures (4/0 Prolene taped to chest), vertical tracheal incision at rings 3–4, no cartilage removal, thyroid isthmus divided with bipolar diathermy (not clamped/transfixed)
> 3. Maturation sutures create a true stoma that stays open even without the tube — this is arguably the single most important paediatric modification, dramatically reducing the risk of a false passage at emergency tube change
> 4. Tracheostomy complications are more common in children than adults and in those under 2 years — overall rates of 25–77% with tracheostomy-related mortality of 0–3.6%; non-tracheostomy-related mortality 7–36%
> 5. Tube obstruction and accidental decannulation are the leading causes of tracheostomy-related death — prevention requires regular suction, humidification, secure tube fixation and meticulous nursing care
> 6. Suprastomal collapse and granulation are the most common causes of decannulation failure — MLB is essential before decannulation; suprastomal granulation found in 80% at bronchoscopy; collapse >50% requires cartilage graft reconstruction
> 7. The GOS decannulation protocol: downsize to 3.0 tube (2.5 if <13 months), 12-hour capping trial, overnight trial, decannulation day 3, observation and discharge day 5 — staged approach prevents "decannulation panic" and allows safe fallback
> 8. Tracheocutaneous fistula occurs in 19–42% after decannulation — risk factors include younger age and longer duration; allow 6–12 months for spontaneous closure; primary closure involves tract excision to tracheal wall, strap muscle reapposition and fusiform skin excision
> 9. Percutaneous tracheostomy (Ciaglia technique) is the commonest ICU procedure — it is not standard in children and is inappropriate after major HN surgery because pre-tracheal tissues fit tightly around the tube and can collapse inward if displaced
> 10. Tracheo-innominate fistula is rare but lethal — presents with sudden massive haemorrhage; if a high innominate artery is found at surgery, either use a higher tracheal incision (accepting SGS risk) or abandon tracheostomy; management: cuffed tube inflation, compression via tracheostome, immediate surgical exploration
> 11. Cuffed tubes are rarely indicated in children — uncuffed tubes achieve adequate seal until adolescence; cuffs increase risk of mucosal ischaemia and stenosis; two indications: significant aspiration risk and temporary need for raised ventilation pressures
> 12. The RLN runs in the tracheo-oesophageal groove lateral to the trachea — the left loops under the aortic arch, the right under the subclavian artery; straying from the midline during tracheostomy risks injury to the RLN and carotid sheath contents

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Maturation sutures are the single most important paediatric-specific modification — they create a true stoma that stays open even without the tube, dramatically reducing the risk of a fatal false passage during emergency tube reinsertion; larger series have disproven the concern that maturation sutures increase the rate of tracheocutaneous fistula formation
> - Never suture tube flanges to the skin in children — infant skin is too thin and flexible for reliable anchorage; linen tapes tied with the neck flexed are the safest initial fixation; if the neck is extended when tapes are fastened, tapes will be too long and the tube may be coughed out
> - "If you can hear a tracheostomy tube, you should change it" — an audible whistle indicates partial obstruction from dried secretions narrowing the lumen; if the suction catheter does not pass freely after changing, seek otolaryngology review
> - The first tube change at day 7 should be performed by an otolaryngologist — in children who cannot be orally intubated, it should be done in the operating theatre with full surgical backup; in easily intubated children, ITU with intubation trolley is acceptable
> - Overzealous suctioning is as dangerous as under-suctioning — inserting the catheter beyond the tube tip causes mucosal trauma and promotes granulation formation that can itself lead to tube obstruction
> - Tracheo-innominate fistula presents with sudden massive haemorrhage without warning — any bleeding from the tracheostomy tube that appears to arise from the anterior tracheal wall (rather than tube-tip granulation) must be investigated by endoscopy
> - The dead space doubles and airway resistance trebles after tracheostomy removal — in long-standing cases, the child may have no memory of mouth/nose breathing, and the new sensation can cause "decannulation panic" requiring a gradual staged approach
> - Tracheostomy-sparing interventions (CO2 laser debulking, microdebrider) for advanced upper aerodigestive tract cancers avoid the complications of tracheostomy and reduce hospital stay — avoidance of tracheostomy also prevents stomal seeding of carcinoma and simplifies incision planning for subsequent laryngectomy
