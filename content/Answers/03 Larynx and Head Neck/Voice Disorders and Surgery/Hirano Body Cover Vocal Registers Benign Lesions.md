# Hirano's Body-cover Theory, Vocal Registers and Benign Vocal Fold Lesions

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Describe the Hirano's body-cover theory of phonation. Discuss vocal registers and their clinical importance. Describe the various benign lesions of the vocal fold and management of vocal nodules.
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 58 (Anatomy of the Larynx), Chapter 59 (Physiology of the Larynx), Chapter 63 (Structural Disorders of the Vocal Cords), Chapter 67 (Phonosurgery); Volume 2, Chapter 33 (Paediatric Voice)

## INTRODUCTION

Normal voice production depends on the layered structure of the vocal fold as described by Hirano's body-cover theory. The mucosal wave — undulations of the thin cover over the firm body — is the fundamental mechanism of phonation. Understanding this theory is essential for diagnosing and managing all structural voice disorders, from benign lesions to sulcus vocalis and scarring.

---

## PART A: HIRANO'S BODY-COVER THEORY

### Layered Structure of the Vocal Fold

The vocal fold consists of five distinct layers:

| Layer | Structure | Role in Phonation |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1. Epithelium | Non-keratinizing stratified squamous | Protective covering |
| 2. Superficial lamina propria (SLP) | Loose areolar tissue = Reinke's space | KEY vibrating layer; allows mucosal wave |
| 3. Intermediate lamina propria | Elastic fibres | Part of vocal LIGAMENT |
| 4. Deep lamina propria | Collagen fibres | Part of vocal LIGAMENT |
| 5. Vocalis muscle | Thyroarytenoid muscle | BODY of the vocal fold |

### Body-Cover Concept

```
╔══════════════════════════════════════════╗
║         HIRANO'S BODY-COVER MODEL       ║
╠══════════════════════════════════════════╣
║                                          ║
║  COVER ──► Epithelium + SLP (Reinke's)  ║
║            (vibrates freely)             ║
║                                          ║
║  TRANSITION ──► Vocal Ligament          ║
║  (LIGAMENT)    (intermediate + deep LP)  ║
║                                          ║
║  BODY ──► Vocalis (TA) Muscle           ║
║           (provides firm support)        ║
║                                          ║
╚══════════════════════════════════════════╝
```

- The vocalis muscle provides the firm **BODY** over which the mucous membrane **COVER** is blown by the expiratory air stream
- The loose areolar tissue of Reinke's space allows lax movement of overlying mucosa → **mucosal wave**
- Mucosal wave abnormalities can only be observed using **laryngostroboscopy** or high-speed photography
- Anterior and posterior macula flava serve as cushions to protect vocal fold ends from mechanical damage

### Vibratory Cycle (Mucosal Wave)

Each cycle has four phases:

1. **Closing phase:** Vocal folds close rapidly from lower margin upward
2. **Closed phase:** Medial edges in full contact
3. **Opening phase:** Folds separate from lower margin, peel apart superiorly
4. **Open phase:** Folds separated (longest part of normal cycle)

The vocal folds are set into vibration by the **Bernoulli effect** — as subglottic pressure overcomes resistance, folds peel apart; resulting negative pressure causes rapid closure. This self-sustained oscillation cycle repeats at the fundamental frequency.

### Developmental Note

The five-layer laminar structure is **NOT present at birth**. It starts to differentiate over the first few months of life and becomes easily recognizable by puberty. This explains why microflap dissection is more difficult in early childhood due to a less well-developed plane in the SLP.

## PART B: VOCAL REGISTERS

### Definition

Vocal registers are defined in terms of **laryngeal behaviour** (degree of contraction of vocalis muscle) rather than acoustic terms.

| Register | Other Names | F0 Range | Vocal Fold Configuration |
|----------|------------|----------|-------------------------|
| Pulse | Vocal fry, glottal fry, creaky voice | 20-60 Hz | Long closed phase; thick, lax folds |
| Modal | Chest, head, middle, heavy voice | 100-300 Hz | Complete adduction; full vibratory pattern |
| Loft | Falsetto | 275-1100 Hz | Thin, tense, lengthened; minimal vibration |

### Pulse Register (Vocal Fry)

- Lowest vocal frequencies with pulsatile laryngeal sound
- Long closed phase in each vibratory cycle
- Feature of normal speech (sentence endings)
- Folds are thick, relaxed, with slack edges

### Modal Register

- Encompasses frequencies used in normal speech and singing
- Membranous portions fully adducted with complete closure in each cycle
- In cross-section, folds are triangular
- In low notes: intrinsic muscles relax, folds increase in bulk, lower surfaces make contact and separate in a rolling figure-of-eight motion
- Suprahyoid muscles raise larynx for higher pitches

### Loft Register (Falsetto)

- Covers highest frequencies
- Vocal folds lengthened, extremely tense and thinned with minimal vibration
- Knife-thin free edges almost adducted
- High subglottic pressure; larynx raised; pharynx shortened
- **Incomplete glottal closure is NORMAL** in falsetto

### Clinical Importance of Registers

| Clinical Application | Relevance |
|---------------------|-----------|
| Puberphonia (mutational falsetto) | Persistent loft register after puberty; treatment: voice therapy ± Type III [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|thyroplasty]] |
| [[Spasmodic Dysphonia Botulinum Toxin|Spasmodic dysphonia]] | Singing/falsetto bypass dystonia — diagnostic and therapeutic value |
| Vocal fatigue | Prolonged modal register use without rest causes muscle fatigue |
| Voice therapy | Register transitions used in rehabilitation exercises |
| Stroboscopy interpretation | Mucosal wave characteristics differ by register |
| Post-surgical voice | Altered register capability indicates structural damage to layers |

## PART C: BENIGN LESIONS OF THE VOCAL FOLD

### Classification

| Lesion | Characteristics | Gender/Age |
|--------|----------------|------------|
| Vocal fold polyp | Benign swelling >3 mm; usually solitary | Males > females; 30-50 years |
| Vocal fold nodules | Small bilateral swellings <3 mm at midmembranous point | Children: boys; adults: women <30 |
| Intracordal cyst (mucus retention) | Blocked minor salivary gland | Either gender |
| Intracordal cyst (epidermoid) | Lined by squamous epithelium, keratin-filled | Associated with "voice abuse" |
| Reinke's oedema | Chronic irreversible SLP swelling | Smokers; age 40-60; bilateral |
| Sulcus vocalis | Mucosal invagination | Variable |
| Contact granuloma | Posterior vocal fold/vocal process | Males; reflux/intubation |
| Laryngeal papilloma | HPV 6/11 associated | Bimodal: children/adults |

### Vocal Fold Polyps

- Most common structural cause of hoarseness
- Usually solitary; arises from free edge of vocal fold
- Aetiology: phonotrauma, acute inflammation, smoking (hyaline degeneration), vascular disruption with capillary proliferation, thrombosis, haemorrhage
- May be haemorrhagic (red) or gelatinous/grey
- Associated sulcus/mucosal bridge or intracordal cyst may be found on opposite fold
- **Treatment:** Voice therapy unlikely to resolve polyp (coping strategies only). Most need surgical removal — laser or cold steel suspension microlaryngoscopy. Aim: restore smooth vocal fold edge.

### Vocal Fold Nodules

- Small bilateral swellings at midmembranous portion (junction of anterior and middle thirds)
- Characterized by epithelial thickening with variable inflammation
- Cause hoarseness in ~25% of children, 6% of adults with voice problems
- Aetiology: "voice abuse" — forced voice, shouting, singing above range; phonotrauma; infections, allergies, reflux contribute
- **Hourglass appearance** on laryngoscopy during phonation
- Nodules disappear spontaneously in boys at puberty

### Intracordal Cysts

- **Mucus retention cyst:** From blocked minor salivary gland; lined by cuboidal epithelium; associated with Reinke's space oedema
- **Epidermoid cyst:** Lined by squamous epithelium, filled with keratin/cholesterol; various theories — metaplasia, micro-inclusion, congenital
- Diagnosis: asymmetrical nodular swellings; stroboscopy shows reduced mucosal wave; definitive at microlaryngoscopy and cordotomy
- Surgery: precise dissection preserving overlying mucosa; mini-microflap technique (Sataloff)

## PART D: MANAGEMENT OF VOCAL NODULES

### Conservative Management (Mainstay)

| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Voice therapy | Mainstay of treatment; addresses causative vocal behaviours |
| Vocal hygiene | Adequate hydration, avoid throat clearing, reduce shouting |
| Identify triggers | Occupational voice demands, singing technique |
| Treat contributing factors | Reflux (PPI), allergies, infections |
| Psychological support | Address psychogenic contributors |

- Many nodules resolve spontaneously (especially in children — disappear at puberty)
- No good evidence base for specific voice therapy protocols — individualized approach

### Surgical Management

**Indications:**
- Failed voice therapy (minimum 6 weeks trial)
- Persistent symptomatic nodules causing significant vocal impairment
- Professional voice users with persistent lesions

**Technique:**
- Suspension microlaryngoscopy under GA
- Precise excision of nodule ONLY
- Must NOT damage underlying vocal ligament
- Cold steel preferred; CO2 laser can be used with precision
- Bilateral excision in same session is acceptable

**Post-operative:**
- Voice rest (typically 5-7 days)
- Post-operative voice therapy is ESSENTIAL
- Significant recurrence rate if surgery performed without voice therapy
- Improvement may continue for up to 9 months

### Differential Diagnosis of Bilateral Vocal Fold Swellings

| Condition | Differentiating Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| True nodules | <3 mm, symmetric, midmembranous, bilateral |
| Pseudonodules | Reactive swelling opposite a polyp or cyst |
| Intracordal cysts | Asymmetric, unilateral, reduced mucosal wave |
| Fibrous masses | Larger, harder, post-traumatic |

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Hirano's body-cover model: COVER (epithelium + SLP), LIGAMENT (intermediate + deep LP), BODY (vocalis muscle)
> 2. Reinke's space (SLP) is the key vibrating layer — its integrity determines mucosal wave quality
> 3. Five-layer laminar structure is NOT present at birth — develops through childhood, recognizable by puberty
> 4. Three vocal registers: pulse (20-60 Hz), modal (100-300 Hz), loft/falsetto (275-1100 Hz) — defined by laryngeal behaviour
> 5. Incomplete glottal closure is NORMAL in falsetto register — not pathological
> 6. Vocal fold polyps are the most common structural cause of hoarseness; usually solitary; require surgical removal
> 7. Vocal nodules are bilateral midmembranous swellings — mainstay of treatment is voice therapy; surgery only for failures
> 8. Nodules disappear spontaneously in boys at puberty; in adults more common in women <30 years
> 9. Intracordal cysts (mucus retention vs epidermoid) require microlaryngoscopy + cordotomy for definitive diagnosis
> 10. Significant nodule recurrence if surgery performed without concurrent voice therapy — post-op speech therapy is essential


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - "Pseudonodule" on one fold opposite a polyp or cyst on the other is a common finding — treat the primary lesion; the pseudonodule resolves
> - Stroboscopy is the key investigation — reduced mucosal wave suggests deeper pathology (cyst, sulcus) rather than surface nodule
> - Hourglass glottic closure pattern is classic for vocal nodules — pathognomonic on laryngoscopy
> - Epidermoid cysts may be congenital — look for bilateral cysts or associated sulcus vocalis in cases of persistent childhood dysphonia
> - Bernoulli effect drives the self-sustained oscillation cycle — it is the fundamental physical principle of phonation, not muscular contraction
