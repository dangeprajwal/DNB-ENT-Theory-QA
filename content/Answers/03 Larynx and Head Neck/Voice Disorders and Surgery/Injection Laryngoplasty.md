# Injection Laryngoplasty — Indications, Techniques and Complications

> [!question] Questions Covered
> - What are the indications, surgical techniques and complications of injection laryngoplasty?
*Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 67 (Phonosurgery), Chapter 78 (Paralysis of the Larynx), Chapter 63 (Structural Disorders of the Vocal Cords)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Injection laryngoplasty is a phonosurgical procedure involving injection of a biocompatible material into the vocal fold to augment its volume and improve glottic closure. First described by Brunings in 1911 using paraffin, and popularized by Arnold in 1962 with Teflon, the technique has evolved significantly with newer materials and office-based approaches under local anaesthesia. It is currently the most commonly performed initial intervention for unilateral [[Vocal Fold Paralysis|vocal fold paralysis]] and other causes of glottic insufficiency.

---

## PART A: INDICATIONS

### Primary Indications

| Indication | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Unilateral vocal fold paralysis | Most common indication; bridges gap during recovery or as definitive treatment |
| Vocal fold atrophy | Age-related bowing (presbylaryngis) |
| Vocal fold scar | Post-surgical or post-intubation scarring |
| Sulcus vocalis | Augmentation of deficient fold |
| Soft tissue defect | Post-cordectomy or post-excision |
| Glottic insufficiency | Any cause of incomplete glottic closure |

### Selection Criteria (from Chapter 78)

Injection laryngoplasty is preferred when:
- High degree of vocal disability or high vocal demand
- Good or indeterminate functional prognosis (recovery expected)
- Small glottic gap (2-3 mm)
- No posterior glottic gap (arytenoid repositioning not possible)
- Short life expectancy (less invasive than framework surgery)
- Dysphagia from aspiration due to incomplete glottic closure
- As a diagnostic/therapeutic trial before committing to framework surgery

### Temporary vs Permanent Injection

| Purpose | Material | Duration |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Diagnostic trial | Glycerine | 2-6 weeks |
| Bridging during recovery | Fat, HA, CaHA | 3-12 months |
| Definitive augmentation | CaHA, fat (repeat), medialization thyroplasty | Long-term |

## PART B: INJECTABLE MATERIALS

| Material | Properties | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|-----------|------------|---------------|
| Teflon (historical) | 50% glycerine paste | — | Unpredictable volume; 36% granuloma rate; NO current indication |
| Autologous fat | Harvested from abdomen/thigh | No foreign body reaction; biocompatible | 30-50% absorbed in first month; requires overcorrection; deep injection |
| Glycerine | Clear injectable | Completely reversible; temporary (2-6 weeks) | Very short duration; diagnostic use only |
| Collagen (bovine/human) | Natural lamina propria constituent | Superficial injection into vocal ligament | <1% hypersensitivity; challenging technique |
| CaHA (Radiesse Voice) | Aqueous gel; 25G/27G needles | Widely used; ~1 year duration | Must inject DEEP; superficial injection causes stiffness |
| Silicone (Bioplastique) | 150-600 micron particles in hydrogel | Deep injection | Few long-term results |
| Hyaluronic acid | Various preparations | Biocompatible; variable duration | Variable absorption rates |
| Carboxymethylcellulose | Gel preparation | Office-based use | Limited data |

### Key Principles of Material Selection

- **No substance is ideally suited for lamina propria** — all stiffen tissue to some degree
- Most injectates require overcorrection to account for absorption
- Deep injection (lateral to vocal ligament, into TA muscle) for most permanent materials
- Superficial injection only for collagen (into vocal ligament itself)
- Fat requires the most overcorrection (30-50% absorption)

## PART C: SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

### Approaches Under Local Anaesthesia (Office-Based)

**1. Transcutaneous through thyroid cartilage**
- Most straightforward approach
- 25G needle passed through thyroid lamina into vocal fold
- Difficulty if cartilage is ossified (common in elderly males)
- Needle tip visualized with flexible nasendoscope

**2. Transcutaneous through cricothyroid membrane**
- Same approach used for botox injections — familiar technique
- Needle advanced in midline, angled cranially and laterally
- Can pass into cord directly, or enter lumen first then penetrate fold under nasendoscopic guidance
- Most versatile office-based approach

**3. Transcutaneous through thyrohyoid membrane**
- Requires topical anaesthesia of glottis
- Needle enters at petiole of epiglottis in midline, then guided laterally to vocal fold
- Used for anterior vocal fold augmentation

**4. Transoral approach**
- Using rigid curved cannula (Abraham cannula)
- Under indirect laryngoscopic visualization
- Patient seated with tongue protruded
- Limited by gag reflex

### Under General Anaesthesia

- Performed via suspension microlaryngoscopy
- Drawbacks: visualization may be suboptimal, abnormal anatomical position (supine), lack of real-time phonation feedback
- Injection point: lateral to vocal ligament into TA muscle body
- Used when office-based approach fails or for bilateral procedures

### Injection Technique — Key Steps

```
INJECTION LARYNGOPLASTY — STEPWISE TECHNIQUE

1. Position: Seated (LA) or supine (GA)
2. Anaesthesia: Topical lidocaine to larynx (LA) or general
3. Visualization: Flexible nasendoscope (LA) or rigid scope (GA)
4. Needle placement: Through chosen approach
5. Confirm position: Visualize needle tip in vocal fold
6. Inject material: Lateral to vocal ligament, into TA muscle
   - Inject slowly in small aliquots
   - Monitor glottic closure in real-time
7. Assess: Ask patient to phonate (LA) — auditory feedback
8. Overcorrect: Slightly beyond desired endpoint
   (especially for fat — 30-50% absorption expected)
9. Reassess: Voice quality, glottic closure on stroboscopy
```

## PART D: COMPLICATIONS

### Early Complications

| Complication | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Airway compromise | Rare; from overinjection or oedema; more risk with bilateral injection |
| Haematoma | Local bleeding at injection site |
| Breathy voice | Under-injection — inadequate augmentation |
| Over-injection | Excess material causing obstructive dysphonia |
| Aspiration of material | If needle enters lumen instead of fold |
| Pain/discomfort | At injection site; usually transient |
| Vasovagal reaction | During office-based procedure |

### Late Complications

| Complication | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Granuloma formation | Teflon: 36% incidence (historical); rare with modern materials |
| Material extrusion | Migration of injected material through mucosa |
| Stiffening of mucosa | All materials stiffen lamina propria to some degree |
| Absorption/resorption | Fat: 30-50% in first month; requires repeat injection |
| Foreign body reaction | Rare with modern biocompatible materials |
| Worsening dysphonia | If material injected superficially (impairs mucosal wave) |

### Limitations of Injection Laryngoplasty

- Cannot reposition the arytenoid cartilage
- Cannot close a posterior glottic gap effectively
- Not ideal for large gaps (>3 mm) — medialization thyroplasty preferred
- Cannot restore vocal fold height difference
- Repeated injections may be needed as materials absorb

---

## PART E: INJECTION LARYNGOPLASTY vs MEDIALIZATION THYROPLASTY

| Feature | Injection Laryngoplasty | Medialization Thyroplasty |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Anaesthesia | LA (office) or GA | LA (with auditory feedback) |
| Reversibility | Temporary materials reversible | Implant removable/adjustable |
| Posterior gap | Cannot close | Arytenoid adduction added |
| Large gaps | Limited (<3 mm) | Effective for larger gaps |
| Precision | Variable | More precise and predictable |
| Durability | Material-dependent (months-years) | Durable long-term |
| Invasiveness | Minimally invasive | Open neck surgery |
| Revision rate | Higher (repeat injections) | 5.4-14% |
| Recovery time | Immediate | Days |

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Injection laryngoplasty was first described by Brunings (1911) using paraffin; Arnold (1962) popularized Teflon
> 2. Teflon has NO current clinical indication — 36% granuloma incidence; only historical interest
> 3. Calcium hydroxyapatite (Radiesse Voice) is widely used — lasts ~1 year; MUST inject deep (lateral to vocal ligament)
> 4. Autologous fat requires 30-50% overcorrection due to first-month absorption; no foreign body reaction
> 5. Four transcutaneous approaches: through thyroid cartilage, cricothyroid membrane, thyrohyoid membrane, and transoral
> 6. Office-based injection under LA with nasendoscopic guidance allows real-time phonation feedback — superior to GA
> 7. No injectable material is ideally suited for the lamina propria — all stiffen tissue to some degree
> 8. Injection cannot close posterior glottic gap or reposition arytenoid — medialization thyroplasty + arytenoid adduction needed
> 9. Glycerine injection (2-6 weeks duration) is used as a diagnostic trial combined with EMG for temporary paralysis assessment
> 10. Small glottic gap (2-3 mm) without posterior gap is ideal for injection; larger gaps need framework surgery


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Office-based injection under LA is now the standard of care for initial management of unilateral VFP — avoids GA risks and allows auditory feedback
> - The cricothyroid membrane approach is the most versatile — same technique used for botox injection in [[Spasmodic Dysphonia Botulinum Toxin|spasmodic dysphonia]]
> - Superficial injection causes mucosal wave stiffening and paradoxically worsens voice — always inject DEEP into the TA muscle body
> - In patients with short life expectancy or poor surgical candidates, injection laryngoplasty is preferred over medialization thyroplasty
> - Fat injection requires a separate harvest site (abdomen/thigh) but has the advantage of being autologous with no foreign body reaction
