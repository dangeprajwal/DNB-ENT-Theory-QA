# Thyroplasty and Laryngeal Framework Surgery

**Question:** *Describe laryngeal framework surgery. Classify thyroplasty and describe the surgical technique of Type I medialization thyroplasty. What are the indications, implant materials, and complications?*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 3, Chapter 67 (Phonosurgery), Chapter 78 (Paralysis of the Larynx), Chapter 63 (Evaluation of Voice); supplemented by: Isshiki N et al. (1974), *Thyroplasty as a new phonosurgical technique*; European Laryngological Society classification (Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol 2001); Type I Thyroplasty and Arytenoid Adduction review (IJHNS); PMC 7585243 (long-term outcomes with titanium implant)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Laryngeal framework surgery (LFS) refers to any surgical procedure performed on the cartilaginous skeleton of the larynx that changes the position or tension of the vocal folds in order to improve voice, airway or swallowing function, without directly violating the mucosal surface of the vocal fold. This is its fundamental advantage over microlaryngoscopic approaches.

The concept was first described by Payr in 1915, but it was Isshiki who, in 1974, systematically classified the procedures into four types and pioneered their performance under local anaesthesia — allowing the patient's own voice to provide intra-operative auditory feedback. Thyroplasty refers specifically to those framework procedures that involve the thyroid lamina. The classification, though subsequently refined by the European Laryngological Society, remains eponymously described as Isshiki Types I–IV and forms the basis of modern phonosurgical practice.

The most frequently performed procedure is **Type I (medialization thyroplasty)**, which is the surgical treatment of choice for permanent unilateral vocal fold paralysis with a large glottic gap.

---

## PART A: ISSHIKI CLASSIFICATION OF THYROPLASTY

### Four Types — Overview

| Type | Name | Mechanism | Primary Indication |
|------|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Type I** | Medialization | Displaces vocal fold medially via implant through thyroid window | Unilateral vocal fold paralysis, glottic insufficiency |
| **Type II** | Lateralization | Splits thyroid cartilage at midline and inserts shim to push folds apart | Adductor spasmodic dysphonia, bilateral paralysis (airway) |
| **Type III** | Relaxation (shortening) | Removes anterior strip of thyroid cartilage to shorten and relax folds | Pitch lowering — puberphonia (mutational falsetto), MTF-to-MtF reversal |
| **Type IV** | Tensioning (cricothyroid approximation) | Approximates cricoid to thyroid cartilage anteriorly, elongating folds | Pitch raising — FtM transgender voice, superior laryngeal nerve palsy |

### European Laryngological Society Functional Classification

| Functional Category | Procedure |
|--------------------|----------|
| Approximation laryngoplasty | Type I (medialization), arytenoid adduction |
| Expansion laryngoplasty | Type II (lateralization) |
| Relaxation laryngoplasty | Type III (shortening) |
| Tensioning laryngoplasty | Type IV (cricothyroid approximation, elongation) |

### Key Principle

All four types are ideally performed under **local anaesthesia** — allowing intra-operative auditory feedback. This is the defining advantage of LFS over other phonosurgical procedures. The patient phonates during surgery so that the surgeon can optimally size and position the implant.

## PART B: TYPE I — MEDIALIZATION THYROPLASTY

### Indications

| Indication | Timing/Notes |
|-----------|-------------|
| Unilateral vocal fold paralysis | Wait 12 months in idiopathic cases for possible spontaneous recovery; use injection laryngoplasty as bridge |
| Bilateral bowed vocal folds (presbylaryngis) | Bilateral thyroplasty may be performed |
| Soft tissue defect after cordectomy | Guarded voice expectations |
| Large posterior glottic gap | May require addition of arytenoid adduction |
| High vocal demand or severe dysphonia | Accelerated pathway; injection laryngoplasty as bridge |

**Selection criteria favouring medialization thyroplasty over injection:**
- Permanent paralysis (poor functional prognosis)
- Large glottic gap (>2–3 mm)
- Posterior glottic gap or vertical level difference
- Need for precise, adjustable, reversible medialization
- High-risk patient where GA is more dangerous than local anaesthesia

### Implant Materials

| Material | Properties | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| **Silastic (silicone)** | Easily carved; most widely used (~2/3 of surgeons) | Isshiki's original material; inert, durable |
| **Gore-Tex (ePTFE)** | Pliable ribbon; layered to achieve desired bulk | McCulloch popularized; easy to insert and remove |
| **Hydroxyapatite** | Biocompatible; osseointegrates with cartilage | Custom-carved; Montgomery implant system |
| **Titanium** | Rigid; precise sizing | Used in Isshiki's Type II; also Type I titanium implant systems |
| **Calcium hydroxylapatite** | Injectable; may be used to augment implant | Now replacing Teflon for augmentation |

> **Note:** Teflon is no longer acceptable — it causes severe granuloma formation and its effects are irreversible. Silastic and Gore-Tex are the most commonly used materials.

### Surgical Technique — Step by Step

**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthesia with sedation (preferred); general anaesthesia if patient cannot tolerate awake procedure (disadvantage: no voice feedback).

**Position:** Supine, neck slightly extended.

| Step | Key Detail |
|------|-----------|
| **1. Skin incision** | Horizontal incision at mid-thyroid level (larynx crease); 3–5 cm long |
| **2. Exposure** | Strap muscles divided in midline; thyroid lamina exposed; perichondrium elevated |
| **3. Identify landmarks** | Lower border of thyroid cartilage is critical; the vocal fold lies at the level of the inferior half of the cartilage |
| **4. Create window** | Window cut in outer cortex of thyroid lamina; dimensions approximately 5 × 10 mm; positioned at the level of the vocal fold |
| **5. Window position** | Lower margin of window = lower border of thyroid cartilage; posterior margin = 5–7 mm from midline; window must NOT be placed too high (false cord displacement) |
| **6. Inner perichondrium** | Preserved as a barrier; pushed medially to displace the vocal fold |
| **7. Implant insertion** | Silastic or Gore-Tex implant carved/layered and inserted through window into paraglottic space to push fold medially |
| **8. Voice testing** | Patient asked to phonate; implant sized and adjusted for optimal closure; best voice = correct implant size |
| **9. Wound closure** | Outer perichondrium sutured over window (essential for Gore-Tex — needs anchor); strap muscles, platysma and skin closed |

### Window Dimensions and Positioning

The most common technical error is placing the window **too high and too anterior**:
- Window must be in the **inferior half** of the thyroid lamina
- Inferior border of window flush with the lower border of the thyroid cartilage
- Too superior → implant displaces false cord, not vocal fold → poor voice result
- Too anterior → anterior commissure distortion

### Arytenoid Adduction — Adjunct to Type I

**Indication:** Large posterior glottic gap or vertical height difference between vocal folds — situations where medialization thyroplasty alone cannot achieve complete glottic closure.

**Technique:**
- Muscular process of the arytenoid cartilage is accessed posteriorly, around the back of the thyroid lamina through the inferior constrictor muscle
- A non-absorbable suture is placed through the muscular process
- Suture secured to the thyroid lamina anterolaterally
- This mimics the action of the lateral cricoarytenoid muscle — rotating the vocal process medially and caudally
- Combined with medialization thyroplasty for optimal result

**Arytenoid adductopexy (alternative):** Cricoarytenoid joint capsule opened; arytenoid sutured directly to cricoid crest.

**Zeitels modification (arytenoid fixation + cricothyroid subluxation):** Extended incision; arytenoid fixed in midline position; cricothyroid subluxation adds tension to paralysed cord — preferred for large posterior gap.

## PART C: TYPE II — LATERALIZATION THYROPLASTY

**Indication:** Adductor spasmodic dysphonia (unresponsive to botulinum toxin); airway improvement in bilateral vocal fold immobility.

**Technique:**
- Anterior neck incision; thyroid cartilage exposed
- Thyroid cartilage divided in the midline (laryngotomy) without entering the airway
- A shim (titanium in Isshiki's description) inserted at anterior commissure level to push folds apart
- Performed under local anaesthesia; patient phonates to determine correct shim size
- Isshiki reported 41 patients with very promising results

---

## PART D: TYPE III — RELAXATION THYROPLASTY

**Indication:** Abnormally high-pitched voice — **puberphonia** (mutational falsetto) resistant to voice therapy; pitch lowering in transgender patients (MtF conversion reversed).

**Mechanism:** Shortening and relaxing the vocal folds by removing a horizontal strip of thyroid cartilage anteriorly, reducing the tension of the thyroarytenoid-vocalis unit.

**Note:** Voice therapy is the treatment of choice for puberphonia; Type III is reserved for cases failing conservative management.

---

## PART E: TYPE IV — CRICOTHYROID APPROXIMATION

**Indication:** Pitch raising — female-to-male (FtM) or transgender (MtF) voice surgery; superior laryngeal nerve (SLN) palsy causing low, breathy voice; combined with Type I for "twisted larynx" deformity.

**Mechanism:** Anterior aspect of the cricoid is sutured to the inner aspect of the thyroid cartilage, effectively elongating and tensioning the vocal folds and raising pitch.

**Note:** Overcorrection can compromise the airway if approximation is too extreme.

## PART F: COMPLICATIONS OF THYROPLASTY

### Overall Complication Profile

| Complication | Rate |
|-------------|------|
| Overall complication rate (systematic review, 2426 patients) | 10.5% |
| Major complications | 4.5% |
| Non-obstructive haematoma | 2.4% (most common) |
| Haemorrhage | 1.5% |
| Implant extrusion | 0.99% |
| Implant displacement | 0.62% |
| Revision rate | 5.4–33% |

### Serious Complications

| Complication | Cause | Management |
|-------------|-------|-----------|
| **Airway obstruction** | Oedema + haematoma narrowing airway | Observation overnight; intubation or tracheotomy rarely required |
| **Mucosal perforation** | Penetration of inner perichondrium | Risk of infection and extrusion |
| **Implant extrusion** | Mucosal perforation; infection | Implant removal; revision |
| **Implant displacement** | Poor fixation; weight of implant | Revision thyroplasty |
| **Haematoma** | Wound bleeding | Evacuation; rare airway risk |
| **Infection** | Wound or laryngeal | Antibiotics; implant removal if severe |

### Voice Outcome Failures

The **most common complication** is a suboptimal voice result, due to:
1. Implant placed too high (displaces false cord, not vocal fold)
2. Implant placed too far anteriorly
3. Persistent posterior gap (needs arytenoid adduction)
4. Under-medialization (implant too small)
5. Implant malposition recognized only on revision

## PART G: COMPARISON — INJECTION LARYNGOPLASTY vs. THYROPLASTY

| Feature | Injection Laryngoplasty | Medialization Thyroplasty |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Invasiveness | Less invasive; office-based | More invasive; operating theatre |
| Reversibility | Temporary (most materials) | Adjustable and reversible |
| Anaesthesia | Local ± sedation; or GA | Local preferred for feedback |
| Voice feedback | Yes (awake) | Yes (awake — key advantage) |
| Precision | Less precise | Precise and predictable |
| Timing | Early (bridge); within recovery window | After 12 months for permanent cases |
| Posterior gap | Cannot reliably close | With arytenoid adduction — yes |
| Duration | 10–30 minutes | 35–75 minutes |
| Best for | Temporary paralysis; bridge therapy | Permanent glottic insufficiency |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Laryngeal framework surgery was first described by Payr (1915) and systematically classified by Isshiki (1974) into four types — all based on altering the position or tension of the vocal folds via the thyroid cartilage, without violating the mucosal surface
> 2. Type I (medialization) is the most common — an alloplastic implant (silastic, Gore-Tex, hydroxyapatite) is inserted through a window in the thyroid lamina into the paraglottic space to displace the paralysed fold medially
> 3. Local anaesthesia is the defining principle of thyroplasty — intra-operative voice feedback from the patient allows the surgeon to size and position the implant correctly
> 4. The critical anatomical error is placing the thyroid window too high — the vocal fold lies at the level of the INFERIOR half of the thyroid lamina; a window placed superiorly displaces the false cord, not the vocal fold
> 5. Arytenoid adduction is added when there is a posterior glottic gap or vertical level difference between the folds — it simulates the lateral cricoarytenoid muscle by pulling the muscular process of the arytenoid anterolaterally
> 6. Type II (lateralization) is used for adductor spasmodic dysphonia — the thyroid cartilage is split at the midline and a shim inserted at the anterior commissure to push the folds apart
> 7. Type III (relaxation/shortening) lowers pitch — indicated for puberphonia resistant to voice therapy; a strip of anterior thyroid cartilage is removed
> 8. Type IV (cricothyroid approximation) raises pitch — the cricoid is approximated to the thyroid anteriorly, tensioning and elongating the vocal folds; used in transgender voice surgery and SLN palsy
> 9. Overall complication rate of thyroplasty is 10.5%; the most common serious complication is haematoma (2.4%); revision rates range from 5.4–33%
> 10. Medialization thyroplasty is preferred over injection laryngoplasty for permanent paralysis — it is precise, predictable, durable, adjustable, and can be performed under local anaesthesia even in high-risk patients


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Inject laryngoplasty bridges the gap while awaiting spontaneous recovery (up to 12 months) — thyroplasty is the definitive procedure once paralysis is declared permanent; both can be performed under local anaesthesia
> - A persistent posterior glottic gap on stroboscopy despite adequate medialization is the most common reason for failed thyroplasty — add arytenoid adduction at the initial operation in patients with large posterior gaps rather than as a second procedure
> - Gore-Tex thyroplasty: the outer perichondrium must be sutured back over the window to anchor the Gore-Tex ribbon — failure to do so results in late extrusion
> - The paralysed fold may be on a different vertical plane from the normal fold — stroboscopic assessment of the vertical level discrepancy before surgery determines whether arytenoid adduction is needed in addition to Type I thyroplasty
> - Type IV (cricothyroid approximation) is the phonosurgical procedure of choice for feminization of voice in MtF transgender patients when Glottoplasty (anterior commissure web) is not preferred
> - Always examine the patient awake with stroboscopy before framework surgery — an apparently paralysed fold may have cricoarytenoid joint fixation (mechanical, not neural), which requires a completely different approach
