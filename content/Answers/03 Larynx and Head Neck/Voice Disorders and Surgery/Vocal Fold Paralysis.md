# Vocal Fold Paralysis — Causes, Evaluation and Management

> [!question] Questions
> Enumerate causes of vocal cord palsy. Discuss the clinical features, diagnosis and management of bilateral vocal fold paralysis.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 78 (Paralysis of the Larynx), Chapter 67 (Phonosurgery), Chapter 63 (Structural Disorders of the Vocal Cords)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Vocal fold paralysis (VFP) results predominantly from peripheral nerve damage affecting the recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) or vagus nerve. The larynx has a strong propensity for re-innervation, but regeneration is problematic due to the mixed adductor/abductor fibre composition of the RLN, leading to dysfunctional synkinesis. Adductor fibre predominance (adductor:abductor ratio 5:1) means re-innervation tends to restore muscle bulk and tone but not coordinated movement. The left vocal fold is affected more than the right (60:40 ratio) due to the longer course of the left recurrent nerve.

---

## PART A: CAUSES OF VOCAL FOLD PARALYSIS

### Aetiological Classification

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| **Surgical/Iatrogenic** | Thyroidectomy, anterior cervical spine surgery, carotid endarterectomy, pneumonectomy, CABG, aortic valve replacement, oesophageal surgery, skull base surgery, mediastinal surgery |
| **Intubation-related** | Endotracheal intubation injury (2-11%); prolonged intubation |
| **Malignant** | Lung cancer (left RLN), thyroid cancer, oesophageal cancer, skull base tumours, [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]], lymphoma |
| **Neurological** | Stroke (Wallenberg syndrome), Arnold-Chiari malformation, multiple sclerosis, Charcot-Marie-Tooth, post-polio syndrome, multisystem atrophy |
| **Idiopathic** | 10-41% of all cases; presumed viral neuritis |
| **Inflammatory** | Sarcoidosis, viral neuritis |
| **Trauma** | Neck trauma, blunt/penetrating injuries |

### Relative Frequency

| Cause | Percentage |
|-------|-----------|
| Tumour | 7-52% |
| Surgery/Trauma | 12-75% |
| Idiopathic | 10-41% |
| Intubation | 2-11% |

### Surgeries Placing RLN at Risk

| Surgery | Nerve at Risk |
|---------|--------------|
| Thyroidectomy | RLN (most common surgical cause) |
| Anterior cervical spine approach | RLN |
| Carotid endarterectomy | Vagus, SLN |
| Pneumonectomy (left) | Left RLN |
| Aortic aneurysm repair | Left RLN |
| CABG/aortic valve replacement | Left RLN |
| Oesophageal surgery | Bilateral RLN |
| Skull base surgery | Vagus (high) |

## PART B: CLINICAL FEATURES

### Unilateral Vocal Fold Paralysis

- **Breathy dysphonia** — most prominent symptom
- Dysphagia, especially to liquids (aspiration)
- Breathlessness during speaking and exertion
- "Wet" vocal quality
- Laryngospasm (episodic)
- High vagal injury adds: hemilaryngeal anaesthesia, cricopharyngeal hyperfunction

### Bilateral Vocal Fold Paralysis

- **Stridor** — predominant symptom (inspiratory or biphasic)
- Airway compromise — may be acute and life-threatening
- Voice may be surprisingly GOOD (folds in paramedian position = adequate glottic closure)
- Dyspnoea on exertion
- Aspiration less common (adequate glottic closure)
- May present acutely post-thyroidectomy or post-extubation

### Glottic Gap Configurations

| Configuration | Cause | Clinical Impact |
|--------------|-------|-----------------|
| Spindle-shaped | Membranous portion gap | Breathy voice; responds well to injection/medialization |
| V-shaped / posterior gap | Vocal process separation | Voice + aspiration; needs arytenoid adduction |
| Paramedian bilateral | Bilateral RLN injury | Good voice but compromised airway |
| Intermediate bilateral | Bilateral vagal injury | Poor voice AND poor airway |

## PART C: INVESTIGATIONS

| Investigation | Purpose |
|--------------|---------|
| Flexible nasendoscopy | Assess fold position, mobility, glottic closure |
| Laryngostroboscopy | Mucosal wave, vibratory pattern, arytenoid movement |
| CT: skull base to aortic arch | Minimum imaging — exclude tumour along nerve course |
| MRI | High vagal paralysis; skull base pathology |
| LEMG (laryngeal EMG) | Definitive diagnosis; prognostic information (fibrillations, polyphasic potentials, recruitment pattern) |
| Modified barium swallow | Aspiration assessment |
| Chest X-ray | Lung pathology, mediastinal mass |
| Blood tests | Thyroid function, ESR, glucose, autoimmune screen |

### LEMG Prognostic Value

- **Fibrillation potentials:** Indicate active denervation
- **Polyphasic motor unit potentials:** Indicate re-innervation in progress
- **Reduced recruitment:** Poor prognosis for recovery
- **Normal interference pattern with synkinesis:** Explains absence of movement despite electrical activity

## PART D: MANAGEMENT OF UNILATERAL VFP

### Observation Period

- Wait 9-12 months for spontaneous recovery (especially idiopathic cases)
- Temporary [[Injection Laryngoplasty|injection laryngoplasty]] can bridge this period (glycerine, HA, fat)
- LEMG at 6 weeks and 6 months guides prognosis

### Surgical Options

| Procedure | Mechanism | Indication |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Injection laryngoplasty | Augments fold volume | Small gap (2-3 mm); temporary or definitive |
| Medialization thyroplasty (Type I) | Silastic/Gore-Tex implant medializes fold | Definitive; larger gaps; done under LA |
| Arytenoid adduction | Repositions arytenoid medially | Posterior gap; height mismatch |
| Adduction arytenopexy | Sutures arytenoid to cricoid | Combined with thyroplasty |
| Reinnervation (ansa cervicalis to RLN) | Restores muscle tone/bulk | Complete denervation; young patients |

### Medialization Thyroplasty (Type I — Isshiki)

- Under LOCAL anaesthesia (intraoperative auditory feedback)
- Window created in inferior half of thyroid cartilage lamina
- Implant (Silastic, Gore-Tex, CaHA) inserted to displace fold medially
- Precise, predictable and durable
- Common errors: prosthesis too small or inserted too high
- Revision rates: 5.4-14%
- Combined with arytenoid adduction for posterior gap

### Arytenoid Adduction (Isshiki 1978)

- For large posterior gap and/or vocal folds at different vertical levels
- Muscular process approached through inferior constrictor around posterior thyroid lamina
- Non-absorbable suture through muscular process, secured to thyroid lamina
- Anterolateral traction rotates vocal process medially and slightly caudally
- Usually combined with medialization thyroplasty
- More technically challenging; higher complication rate (airway oedema)

### Reinnervation

- Ansa cervicalis to RLN anastomosis (Crumley technique)
- Provides efferent fibres (C1-C3) to replace unfavourable synkinesis with more favourable synkinesis
- Results take 2-3 months — temporary injection bridges this gap
- Does NOT restore physiologic movement (complex innervation)
- Improves bulk and tone only
- Best suited when fold is completely denervated (nerve section recognized intra-operatively — immediate re-anastomosis is treatment of choice)

## PART E: MANAGEMENT OF BILATERAL VFP

### Emergency Management

- **Acute airway compromise:** [[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]] or intubation
- Most commonly follows bilateral thyroidectomy
- Assessment: determine if paralysis is temporary (neuropraxia) or permanent

### Definitive Surgical Options

The goal is to balance adequate airway with acceptable voice — widening the airway inevitably worsens voice quality.

| Procedure | Mechanism | Outcome |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Tracheostomy | Bypasses glottic obstruction | Definitive airway; voice preserved; burden of stoma |
| Posterior cordotomy | CO2 laser transection of posterior vocal fold | Adequate airway; some voice loss |
| Arytenoidectomy (total/partial) | Laser or open removal of arytenoid | Wider airway; greater voice loss |
| Medial arytenoidectomy | Partial laser arytenoid removal | Balance of airway and voice |
| Lateralization (suture laterofixation) | Suture-based lateral displacement of fold | Reversible; temporary; good airway |
| Selective reinnervation | Phrenic nerve (C3) to PCA via cable graft | Restores abduction; complex procedure |
| Laryngeal pacing | Electrode in PCA muscle; external pacemaker | Experimental; still in clinical trials |

### Posterior Cordotomy (Most Common Procedure)

- CO2 laser transection at posterior aspect of one vocal fold
- Creates permanent posterior glottic gap
- Unilateral procedure — preserves contralateral fold for voice
- Can be repeated or converted to arytenoidectomy if inadequate
- Risk: granulation tissue, restenosis

### Selective Reinnervation for Bilateral Abductor Paralysis

- C3 root of phrenic nerve identified
- Cable graft (great auricular nerve) anastomosed in Y-shape
- Inserted into both PCA muscles bilaterally
- Descending branch of ansa hypoglossi and RLN identified bilaterally and anastomosed
- Complex procedure via extended anterior neck incision
- Aim: restore abduction — physiologic but technically demanding

### Bilateral VFP After Thyroidectomy — Specific Management

| Timing | Action |
|--------|--------|
| Immediate post-op | Secure airway (reintubation or tracheostomy) |
| First 6 months | Observe — neuropraxia may recover; keep tracheostomy |
| 6-12 months | LEMG assessment; if no recovery → plan definitive procedure |
| Definitive | Posterior cordotomy or arytenoidectomy; consider decannulation |

## PART F: ISSHIKI THYROPLASTY CLASSIFICATION

| Type | Mechanism | Indication |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Type I | Medialization (implant pushes fold medially) | Unilateral VFP |
| Type II | Lateralization (midline thyroid cartilage split + shim) | Adductor [[Spasmodic Dysphonia Botulinum Toxin|spasmodic dysphonia]] |
| Type III | Relaxation (shortening vocal fold) | Puberphonia (mutational falsetto) |
| Type IV | Tension (lengthening vocal fold) | Pitch elevation |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Left vocal fold paralysis is more common (60:40) due to longer course of left RLN around aortic arch
> 2. RLN has mixed adductor:abductor fibres in 5:1 ratio — re-innervation restores tone/bulk but not coordinated movement
> 3. Thyroidectomy is the most common surgical cause of VFP; idiopathic cases account for 10-41%
> 4. CT from skull base to aortic arch is the MINIMUM imaging for unexplained VFP
> 5. LEMG provides definitive diagnosis and prognostic information — fibrillation potentials indicate active denervation
> 6. Unilateral VFP: wait 9-12 months for recovery; temporary injection bridges the gap; definitive = medialization thyroplasty ± arytenoid adduction
> 7. Medialization thyroplasty done under LA with auditory feedback — precise, predictable and durable
> 8. Arytenoid adduction needed for posterior gap or vertical height mismatch — combined with Type I thyroplasty
> 9. Bilateral VFP: voice is often good (paramedian position); airway is the problem — posterior cordotomy is the most common procedure
> 10. Bilateral VFP management = balance between adequate airway and acceptable voice — widening airway worsens voice


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - In bilateral VFP, paradoxically the voice may be surprisingly good because folds are in paramedian position — patients present with stridor, not dysphonia
> - Arnold-Chiari malformation causes bilateral VFP that may be REVERSIBLE with posterior fossa decompression
> - Wallenberg syndrome (lateral medullary stroke) causes ipsilateral VFP with contralateral sensory loss — classic crossed neurological deficit
> - Vocal fold position does NOT reliably indicate the site or type of nerve lesion — this is a common misconception
> - Immediate RLN re-anastomosis during surgery (when nerve section is recognized) is the treatment of choice — gives best long-term outcomes
