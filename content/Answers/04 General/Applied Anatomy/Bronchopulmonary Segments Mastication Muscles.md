# Right Main Bronchus and Bronchopulmonary Segments; Muscles of Mastication

**Question 1:** *Describe right main bronchus and bronchopulmonary segments with clinical importance.*

**Question 2:** *Describe muscles of mastication and their actions.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 34 (Foreign Bodies in ENT), Chapter 28 (Stridor); Volume 3, Chapter 41 (Anatomy and Embryology of the Mouth and Dentition)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Knowledge of the anatomy of the right main bronchus and bronchopulmonary segments is essential for the otolaryngologist, as the right bronchus is the most common site of foreign body lodgement in children and adults, and bronchoscopy is a core otolaryngological skill. The muscles of mastication, innervated by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve, are clinically important in the assessment and management of trismus, [[Deep Neck Space Infections|deep neck space infection]]s, oral and oropharyngeal tumour staging, and temporomandibular joint disorders.

---

## PART A: RIGHT MAIN BRONCHUS — ANATOMY

### Anatomy of the Trachea and Carina

The trachea is a cartilaginous and membranous tube extending from the cricoid cartilage (C6) to the carina (T4-5), measuring approximately 10-12 cm in length in adults and consisting of 16-20 C-shaped cartilaginous rings. The posterior wall (trachealis muscle) is membranous. At the carina, the trachea bifurcates into the right and left main bronchi.

### Right Main Bronchus

| Feature | Right Main Bronchus | Left Main Bronchus |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Length | 2.5 cm | 5 cm |
| Diameter | Wider (larger) | Narrower |
| Angle from trachea | 25 degrees | 45 degrees |
| Relationship | More vertical, direct continuation of trachea | More horizontal, crosses in front of oesophagus |
| Key relation | Azygos vein arches over it | Aortic arch crosses over it |
| Divisions | 3 lobar bronchi (upper, middle, lower) | 2 lobar bronchi (upper, lower) |

### Why Foreign Bodies Preferentially Enter the Right Bronchus

- The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertically oriented than the left
- It forms a more direct continuation of the trachea
- The smaller angle of divergence (25° vs 45°) favours passage of inhaled material
- From meta-analysis: right bronchus > left bronchus > trachea > larynx for foreign body location

### Bronchopulmonary Segments

A bronchopulmonary segment is the basic anatomical and functional unit of the lung, defined as the portion of lung supplied by a segmental bronchus and its accompanying branch of the pulmonary artery, separated from adjacent segments by connective tissue septa.

### Clinical Importance of Bronchopulmonary Segments

1. **Foreign body localization:** Segmental anatomy guides bronchoscopic retrieval — foreign bodies in the right lower lobe are most common; accurate knowledge of segmental bronchial orifices is essential for systematic bronchoscopic examination
2. **Segmental resection:** Each segment is a surgically resectable unit with its own vascular supply and separated by intersegmental planes — allows lung-sparing surgery
3. **Atelectasis/pneumonia:** Segmental collapse or consolidation patterns on imaging indicate the specific segmental bronchus affected
4. **Bronchoscopic anatomy:** Systematic bronchoscopy requires identification of each segmental orifice — the carina, lobar bronchi and segmental openings serve as landmarks
5. **Bronchopulmonary sequestration:** Anomalous segments with aberrant arterial supply from the aorta — intralobar (within visceral pleura) or extralobar (separate pleural covering)
6. **Congenital anomalies:** Porcine bronchus (right upper lobe bronchus arising directly from trachea), tracheal bronchus, and bronchial trifurcation are recognized variants

### Three Anatomical Narrowings of the Oesophagus (Related)

In the context of oesophagoscopy, three anatomical narrowings correspond to where foreign bodies commonly lodge:
1. Cricopharyngeus (C6) — most common site
2. Aortic arch crossing (T4) — 26 cm from incisors
3. Lower oesophageal sphincter/cardia (T10-11)

## PART B: MUSCLES OF MASTICATION

### Overview

The muscles of mastication are four paired muscles that act on the mandible to produce the movements of mastication (chewing). All four muscles are derived from the first pharyngeal (branchial) arch and are innervated by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3) via the motor root.

### The Four Muscles

**1. Masseter**

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Origin | Superficial head: zygomatic process of maxilla and anterior 2/3 of zygomatic arch; Deep head: posterior 1/3 and medial surface of zygomatic arch |
| Insertion | Multiple insertions on lateral surface of ramus and angle of mandible |
| Action | Elevation of mandible (jaw closure); superficial fibres assist in protrusion |
| Nerve supply | Masseteric nerve (V3 — anterior division) |
| Clinical note | Submasseteric spaces lie between masseter insertions and ramus; infection may spread to this space |

**2. Temporalis**

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Origin | Floor of temporal fossa and deep surface of temporal fascia |
| Insertion | Coronoid process and anterior border of ramus of mandible |
| Action | Anterior fibres: elevation of mandible; Posterior fibres: retraction of mandible |
| Nerve supply | Deep temporal nerves (V3 — anterior division) |
| Clinical note | Most powerful elevator; posterior fibres important for retraction (biting position) |

**3. Medial Pterygoid**

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Origin | Superficial head: tuberosity of maxilla; Deep head: medial surface of lateral pterygoid plate of sphenoid |
| Insertion | Medial surface of ramus and angle of mandible (roughened area below mandibular foramen) |
| Action | Elevation of mandible; assists in protrusion and lateral (side-to-side) movement |
| Nerve supply | Nerve to medial pterygoid (V3 — main trunk) |
| Clinical note | With masseter forms a sling around the angle of mandible (pterygomassseteric sling); pterygomandibular space lies between medial pterygoid and ramus |

**4. Lateral Pterygoid**

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Origin | Upper head: infratemporal surface of greater wing of sphenoid; Lower head: lateral surface of lateral pterygoid plate |
| Insertion | Upper head: articular disc and capsule of TMJ; Lower head: pterygoid fovea on neck of mandibular condyle |
| Action | Depression of mandible (jaw opening — when acting with digastric); protrusion of mandible; unilateral action produces lateral deviation to opposite side |
| Nerve supply | Nerve to lateral pterygoid (V3 — anterior division) |
| Clinical note | Only muscle of mastication that opens the jaw (with suprahyoid muscles); attachment to TMJ disc is clinically significant in disc displacement |

### Movements of the Mandible

| Movement | Primary Muscles | Assistants |
|----------|----------------|------------|
| Elevation (closing) | Masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid | — |
| Depression (opening) | Lateral pterygoid (inferior head) | Digastric, mylohyoid, geniohyoid (when hyoid is fixed) |
| Protrusion | Lateral pterygoid (bilateral) | Masseter (superficial), medial pterygoid |
| Retraction | Temporalis (posterior fibres) | Digastric (posterior belly) |
| Lateral movement | Ipsilateral temporalis (posterior fibres) + contralateral lateral pterygoid and medial pterygoid | — |

### Blood Supply

All muscles of mastication are supplied by branches of the maxillary artery (terminal branch of the external carotid artery), specifically:
- Masseteric artery → masseter
- Deep temporal arteries → temporalis
- Pterygoid branches → medial and lateral pterygoids

### Clinical Significance

**Trismus:** Involuntary restriction of mouth opening caused by spasm or inflammation of the muscles of mastication. Causes include:
- [[Peritonsillar Abscess|Peritonsillar abscess]] (spasm of medial pterygoid)
- Dental infections spreading to masticator space
- Oral/oropharyngeal tumours invading pterygoid muscles
- Post-[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] fibrosis
- TMJ pathology

**Tumour staging:**
- T4a oropharyngeal cancer: invasion of medial pterygoid, mandible or hard palate
- T4b oropharyngeal cancer: invasion of lateral pterygoid muscle, pterygoid plates
- [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|Nasopharyngeal carcinoma]] T2: involvement of medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid or prevertebral muscles

**Deep neck space anatomy:**
- Submasseteric space: between masseter and lateral ramus
- Pterygomandibular space: between medial pterygoid and medial ramus
- [[Parapharyngeal Space|Parapharyngeal space]]: bounded by superior constrictor and medial surface of medial pterygoid
- Masticator space: contains all four muscles, ramus and body of mandible

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter (2.5 cm vs 5 cm) and more vertically oriented (25° vs 45°) than the left — making it the preferential site of foreign body lodgement
> 2. The right lung has 10 bronchopulmonary segments in 3 lobes; the left lung has 8-10 segments in 2 lobes — each segment is a surgically resectable unit with independent vascular supply
> 3. Three oesophageal narrowings where FBs lodge: cricopharyngeus (C6, most common), aortic arch (T4), and lower oesophageal sphincter (T10-11)
> 4. All four muscles of mastication are derived from the first branchial arch and innervated by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (V3) via the motor root
> 5. Masseter, temporalis and medial pterygoid are elevators (jaw closure); lateral pterygoid is the only muscle of mastication that depresses the mandible (jaw opening)
> 6. Lateral pterygoid attaches to the TMJ articular disc via its upper head — this is clinically significant in disc displacement disorders
> 7. Trismus from peritonsillar abscess is caused by spasm of the medial pterygoid muscle — the muscle lies immediately lateral to the tonsillar fossa
> 8. In oropharyngeal cancer staging, invasion of medial pterygoid = T4a; invasion of lateral pterygoid = T4b — this distinction has critical implications for resectability
> 9. The pterygomandibular space lies between the medial pterygoid and the ramus of the mandible — infections can spread from this space to the parapharyngeal space
> 10. Porcine bronchus (right upper lobe bronchus arising directly from trachea) is a recognized anatomical variant that must be identified during bronchoscopy to avoid missing pathology


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - During rigid bronchoscopy for foreign body removal, systematic examination must include all segmental bronchial orifices — after FB removal, an immediate second look is mandatory to ensure no retained fragments in other segments
> - The submasseteric spaces are formed because the masseter muscle has multiple insertions on the lateral surface of the ramus — odontogenic infections can track into these spaces causing swelling over the angle of the mandible mimicking parotid pathology
> - Trismus in the presence of a neck mass should always raise suspicion of malignancy invading the masticator space — MRI is the best imaging modality to delineate tumour extension into the pterygoid muscles
> - In NPC staging (AJCC 8th), involvement of the medial and lateral pterygoid muscles or prevertebral muscles upgrades the tumour to T2 — this is different from oropharyngeal staging where pterygoid involvement indicates T4
> - Foreign bodies in the right lower lobe are the most difficult to retrieve bronchoscopically because of the acute angle required — optical forceps with a rigid telescope of corresponding length improve visualization of segmental bronchi
> - The lateral pterygoid is the only muscle of mastication to produce opening movement — however, it cannot open the jaw alone; the suprahyoid muscles (digastric, mylohyoid, geniohyoid) must contract simultaneously with the hyoid bone fixed by the infrahyoid muscles
