## Introduction

Anticancer drugs have been used since the 1940s, following the observation that nitrogen mustard gas interfered with haematopoiesis. Cancer is defined as the uncontrolled growth of cells coupled with malignant behaviour — invasion and metastasis — arising through a complex interaction between genetic and environmental factors causing mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes. Chemotherapy aims to exploit the resulting differences in biological and proliferative characteristics between normal and cancer cells. Most cytotoxic drugs preferentially affect dividing cells in tumours. Chemotherapy alone cannot cure head and neck cancer but is used in conjunction with surgery and [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] for its direct tumouricidal effect, radio-sensitizing properties, organ preservation potential and control of subclinical micrometastatic spread.

## Part A — Cell Cycle Phases and Chemotherapeutic Targets

### Phases of the Cell Cycle

The cell cycle is divided into phases governed by an elaborate set of molecular switches:

| Phase | Name | Key Events | Duration |
|-------|------|-----------|----------|
| G0 | Quiescence | Resting phase; non-dividing cells | Variable (days to years) |
| G1 | Gap 1 / Growth | Cell enlarges; makes new proteins; prepares to copy DNA | 8-10 hours |
| S | Synthesis | DNA replication; chromosomal material doubles | 6-8 hours |
| G2 | Gap 2 | Further growth; cell prepares to divide | 4-6 hours |
| M | Mitosis | Chromosomes separate; cell divides | ~1 hour |

**Mitosis sub-phases:** Prophase (chromatin condenses into chromosomes) -> Metaphase (spindle forms from microtubules; chromosomes align at equatorial plane) -> Anaphase (sister chromatids separate) -> Telophase (cell division)

At the end of a cycle, daughter cells either continue through the cycle, leave and enter G0, or become terminally differentiated. Most anticancer agents do NOT cause cell death during G0.

### Classification by Cell Cycle Specificity

| Category | Drugs | Characteristics |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| Phase-specific | Methotrexate (S), vinca alkaloids (M), taxanes (M), antimetabolites (S) | Kill proliferating cells during specific phase; dose-related plateau in cell killing; increase duration of exposure to increase kill |
| Cell-cycle-specific | Most chemo agents (alkylating agents, platinum derivatives) | Act on dividing cells regardless of phase; linear dose-response curve — higher dose = greater fractional cell kill |
| Cell-cycle-non-specific | Few agents (e.g., some alkylating agents, mitomycin C) | Kill both resting (G0) and dividing cells |

## Part B — Classification of Chemotherapeutic Agents by Mechanism

### Comprehensive Classification Table

| Class | Subclass | Key Agents | Mechanism of Action | Phase Specificity |
|-------|----------|-----------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Alkylating Agents | Nitrogen mustards | Melphalan, chlorambucil | Covalently link alkyl groups (R-CH2) to DNA; form inter/intra-strand cross-links; interfere with DNA replication enzymes | Most active in S phase |
| | Oxazaphosphorines | Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide | Same alkylating mechanism | S phase |
| Heavy Metals | Platinum agents | Cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin | Form DNA cross-links (see Part C for detail) | Cell-cycle-specific |
| Antimetabolites | Folic acid antagonists | Methotrexate | Inhibits dihydrofolate reductase; blocks tetrahydrofolate synthesis; inhibits thymidylate synthetase | S phase |
| | Pyrimidine analogues | 5-Fluorouracil, cytarabine, gemcitabine | Inhibit nucleic acid synthesis; incorporated into DNA/RNA | S phase |
| | Purine analogues | 6-Mercaptopurine, thioguanine | Inhibit nucleotide biosynthesis by incorporation into DNA | S phase |
| Cytotoxic Antibiotics | Anthracyclines | Doxorubicin, daunorubicin, epirubicin | Intercalate with DNA; inhibit topoisomerase II; stabilize DNA-topo II complex; prevent strand reconnection | Non-phase-specific |
| | Glycopeptides | Bleomycin | Causes DNA fragmentation | G2 phase |
| | Others | Actinomycin D | Intercalates between G-C base pairs; blocks RNA synthesis | Non-specific |
| | | Mitomycin C | Cross-links DNA (alkylating-like mechanism) | Non-specific |
| Spindle Poisons | Vinca alkaloids | Vincristine, vinblastine, vindesine, vinorelbine | Bind tubulin; inhibit microtubule assembly; block metaphase | M phase |
| | Taxanes (taxoids) | Paclitaxel, docetaxel | Promote microtubule assembly; inhibit disassembly; activate apoptotic pathways | M phase |
| Topoisomerase Inhibitors | Topo I inhibitors | Irinotecan, topotecan | Bind enzyme-DNA complex; prevent DNA replication | G2 phase |
| | Topo II inhibitors | Etoposide | Stabilize topo II-DNA complex; cause strand breaks | G2 phase |

### Single Agents Active in H&N Cancer

The following agents are active as single agents, with response rates between 15% and 40%: methotrexate, cisplatin, carboplatin, fluorouracil, ifosfamide, bleomycin, paclitaxel and docetaxel. Cisplatin is one of the most active drugs against squamous head and neck cancer. Combination chemotherapy in H&N usually includes cisplatin.

## Part C — Platinum-Based Chemotherapeutic Agents

### Enumeration and Mechanisms

| Agent | Structure | Mechanism of Action | Key Feature |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|-------------|
| Cisplatin | Organic heavy metal complex with chloride ions | Chloride ions lost after diffusion into cell; compound cross-links with DNA strands (mostly to guanine groups); forms intra- and inter-strand DNA cross-links; inhibits DNA, RNA and protein synthesis | Most active platinum agent in H&N SCC |
| Carboplatin | Same platinum moiety as cisplatin; bonded to organic carboxylate group | Same cross-linking mechanism as cisplatin but bonded to a cyclobutane dicarboxylate leaving group; slower aquation and DNA binding | Less nephrotoxic, ototoxic; more myelosuppressive |
| Oxaliplatin | Platinum atom complexed with oxalate and bulky DACH group | Forms interstrand and intrastrand DNA cross-links via reactive platinum complexes; inhibits DNA synthesis | Bulky DACH group may overcome cisplatin resistance |

### Dosage Regimens in H&N Cancer

| Setting | Cisplatin Dose | Schedule | Notes |
|---------|---------------|----------|-------|
| Concurrent CRT (high-dose) | 100 mg/m2 | Days 1, 22, 43 of RT | Gold standard; may require inpatient admission for pre-hydration |
| Concurrent CRT (weekly) | 40 mg/m2 (capped at 70 mg) | Weekly during RT | More flexibility; can adjust based on tolerability; suitable for outpatient/day ward |
| Concurrent CRT (daily) | 6 mg/m2 | Daily during RT | Occasionally used; outpatient delivery |
| Induction (PF) | 100 mg/m2 | + 5-FU 1 g/m2/day x 4-5 days; 3-weekly x 2-3 cycles | Former standard IC regimen |
| Induction (TPF) | Part of TPF | + Docetaxel + 5-FU; 3-weekly x 3 cycles | Current preferred IC regimen (higher toxicity) |
| Palliative/metastatic | Cisplatin or carboplatin | + 5-FU; various schedules | Expected response rate ~40% |

- **Carboplatin** is used when cisplatin is contraindicated (renal dysfunction, neuropathy, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]]/deafness, fluid overload risk). It is less tumouricidal than cisplatin and has been largely overtaken by cetuximab when platinum is contraindicated.
- A significant proportion of patients do not receive all three cycles of high-dose cisplatin due to toxicity, but one study showed no survival difference between two and three cycles.

### Adverse Effects of Platinum Agents

| Adverse Effect | Cisplatin | Carboplatin | Oxaliplatin |
|---------------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Nephrotoxicity | +++ (dose-limiting) | + (mild) | + |
| Ototoxicity | +++ (high-frequency SNHL) | + | + |
| Peripheral neuropathy | ++ (sensory) | + | +++ (cold-triggered) |
| Nausea/vomiting | +++ (highly emetogenic) | ++ | ++ |
| Myelosuppression | ++ | +++ (dose-limiting) | ++ |
| Electrolyte disturbance | +++ (hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia) | + | + |
| Anaphylaxis | + | ++ (more common) | + |

## Part D — Complications of Chemotherapy in Head and Neck Cancer

### Systemic Complications

| System | Complications |
|--------|--------------|
| Haematological | Myelosuppression (anaemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia); febrile neutropenia (medical emergency); myelodysplasia (long-term) |
| Gastrointestinal | Severe nausea/vomiting (cisplatin — highly emetogenic); mucositis (exacerbated when combined with RT); stomatitis; diarrhoea; anorexia |
| Renal | Nephrotoxicity (cisplatin — requires pre-hydration and monitoring; dose-limiting); acute kidney injury; chronic renal impairment |
| Neurological | Peripheral sensory neuropathy (cisplatin, oxaliplatin); ototoxicity/SNHL (cisplatin — irreversible); tinnitus |
| Dermatological | Alopecia; acneiform rash (cetuximab); hand-foot syndrome (5-FU); nail changes; radiation recall dermatitis |
| Hepatic | Transaminitis; hepatotoxicity (methotrexate — chronic use) |
| Cardiovascular | Cardiotoxicity (anthracyclines — cumulative dose-limiting); thromboembolic events |
| Immunological | Immunosuppression; opportunistic infections; reactivation of latent infections |
| Reproductive | Infertility; teratogenicity |

### Complications Specific to Concurrent Chemoradiation in H&N

- **Enhanced mucositis** — the most significant acute toxicity; Grade 3-4 mucositis significantly higher with CRT than RT alone; impacts nutrition, speech and quality of life
- **Enhanced dysphagia** — may require feeding tube (prophylactic PEG/RIG or reactive nasogastric tube)
- **Nephrotoxicity from cisplatin** — requires pre-hydration protocols (typically 1-2 L normal saline before each dose)
- **Myelodysplasia and neutropenia** — may necessitate dose reduction or treatment breaks
- **Treatment breaks** — chemotherapy toxicity can cause detrimental breaks in radiotherapy delivery, associated with poorer outcomes (tumour repopulation)
- **Chemotherapy-related death** — increased with TPF induction regimens compared with PF

## Part E — Oral Chemotherapy Drugs in Head and Neck Cancer

Oral agents offer advantages of convenience, outpatient administration and potential for chronic therapy. Several oral agents have been studied in H&N cancer:

| Agent | Class | Mechanism | Use in H&N Cancer |
|-------|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Capecitabine | Oral prodrug of 5-FU | Converted to 5-FU by thymidine phosphorylase (preferentially in tumour tissue); inhibits thymidylate synthase | Palliative setting for recurrent/metastatic disease; better tolerated than IV 5-FU |
| Methotrexate | Folic acid antagonist | Inhibits dihydrofolate reductase | Oral low-dose for palliation; single-agent in recurrent/metastatic disease |
| Gefitinib (Iressa) | EGFR-TKI | Blocks EGFR signalling cascade; inhibits growth and proliferation | Phase II activity as single agent in recurrent/metastatic HNSCC; acceptable safety |
| Erlotinib (Tarceva) | EGFR-TKI | Blocks EGFR tyrosine kinase; inhibits downstream signal transduction | Single-agent activity in recurrent/metastatic disease; no benefit shown in locally advanced disease |
| Cyclophosphamide | Alkylating agent | Alkylates DNA; forms cross-links | Low-dose oral metronomic therapy; investigational |

- **Gefitinib and erlotinib** are orally active EGFR-TKIs that have activity as single agents in recurrent/metastatic H&N cancer with an acceptable safety profile compared to conventional chemotherapy. However, results in locally advanced disease have not demonstrated additional benefit.
- **Oral metronomic chemotherapy** (continuous low-dose oral agents) is an emerging concept for maintenance or palliation in patients unsuitable for intensive IV regimens.

## Part F — Targeted Therapeutic Agents in Head and Neck Cancer (Overview)

### Classification of Targeted Agents

| Category | Agent | Target | Mechanism |
|----------|-------|--------|-----------|
| Anti-EGFR MAB | Cetuximab (Erbitux) | EGFR extracellular domain | Human-murine chimeric MAB; blocks ligand binding; receptor internalized and inactivated; also anti-angiogenic |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The cell cycle comprises G0 (resting), G1 (growth/protein synthesis), S (DNA synthesis), G2 (pre-mitotic growth) and M (mitosis) phases, governed by cyclins, CDKs and their inhibitors; most anticancer agents do NOT kill cells in G0
> 2. Chemotherapeutic agents are classified by mechanism into alkylating agents, platinum compounds (heavy metals), antimetabolites (folic acid antagonists, pyrimidine/purine analogues), cytotoxic antibiotics, spindle poisons (vinca alkaloids, taxanes) and topoisomerase inhibitors
> 3. Three platinum agents are used in H&N cancer: cisplatin (forms intra/inter-strand DNA cross-links via guanine; most active), carboplatin (same platinum moiety, carboxylate leaving group; less nephrotoxic but more myelosuppressive) and oxaliplatin (bulky DACH group; may overcome cisplatin resistance)
> 4. Cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on days 1, 22 and 43 of RT is the gold standard concurrent CRT regimen; weekly cisplatin 40 mg/m2 offers more flexibility and outpatient delivery; daily cisplatin 6 mg/m2 is occasionally used
> 5. Cisplatin's dose-limiting toxicities are nephrotoxicity (requires pre-hydration) and ototoxicity (irreversible high-frequency SNHL); carboplatin's dose-limiting toxicity is myelosuppression
> 6. Phase-specific drugs (methotrexate S-phase, vinca alkaloids M-phase) have a dose-related plateau in cell killing — the way to increase kill is to increase duration of exposure rather than dose
> 7. Complications of chemotherapy include myelosuppression, mucositis (most significant in CRT), nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, peripheral neuropathy, nausea/vomiting, immunosuppression and treatment-related death (increased with TPF regimens)
> 8. Concurrent CRT causes enhanced mucositis and dysphagia compared to RT alone; chemotherapy-induced treatment breaks detrimentally affect radiotherapy outcomes through tumour repopulation
> 9. Oral chemotherapy agents available for H&N cancer include capecitabine (oral prodrug of 5-FU), methotrexate, gefitinib and erlotinib (oral EGFR-TKIs); these are primarily used in palliative/recurrent settings
> 10. Targeted agents in H&N cancer include anti-EGFR MAB (cetuximab), EGFR-TKI (gefitinib, erlotinib), anti-VEGF (bevacizumab) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, durvalumab, ipilimumab)

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Cisplatin is the most active single agent against H&N SCC** — combination chemotherapy in H&N invariably includes cisplatin; single-agent cisplatin has been shown to be as effective as multi-drug regimens in the concurrent setting (MACH-NC meta-analysis)
> - **Carboplatin does NOT equal cisplatin in efficacy** — it is deemed slightly less tumouricidal and has been largely replaced by cetuximab when cisplatin is contraindicated, rather than being used as a direct substitute
> - **Taxanes promote microtubule assembly (opposite to vinca alkaloids)** — docetaxel and paclitaxel stabilize microtubules and prevent disassembly, while vincristine and vinblastine prevent microtubule assembly; both cause M-phase arrest but through opposing mechanisms
> - **5-FU must be converted to its active form (5-FdUMP) to exert effect** — it inhibits thymidylate synthetase, blocking dTMP production; this action is stabilized by folinic acid (leucovorin), which is why folinic acid is sometimes co-administered
> - **Chemotherapy follows Gompertzian kinetics** — tumours grow most rapidly at small volumes and slow down at larger sizes; chemotherapy is therefore most effective at eradicating small tumour burdens (rationale for adjuvant chemotherapy)
> - **A significant proportion of patients do NOT complete all 3 cycles of high-dose cisplatin** — one study showed no survival difference between 2 and 3 cycles, but the RT delivered was not identical between arms; elderly patients (>70 years) benefit least from concurrent CRT
