## Introduction

Gene therapy is the means of delivering exogenous genetic material for therapeutic purposes into the host cell target using vectors. This strategy can be utilized in patients with genetic defects leading to phenotypic impairment. Gene therapy can be restorative, regenerative or protective in nature by replacing, suppressing or enhancing gene expression. As of 2012, approximately 1800 human clinical gene therapy trials (including phase I to IV) exist worldwide, spanning cancer, monogenetic diseases (cystic fibrosis, haemophilia B), cardiovascular disease and infectious diseases (HIV). Despite initial promise, progress in translating laboratory success into clinically useful applications has been quite limited, with efficiency and targeting remaining formidable barriers.

---

## Part A: Definition and Fundamental Concepts

Gene therapy involves the delivery of exogenous genetic material to target cells using vectors (delivery vehicles) that bypass host defences. The treatment aims to replace or repair the defective gene causing a given disease or to provide a new or altered function in a cell. Vectors are typically replication-deficient viruses, although non-viral vectors such as liposomes are also used. In select circumstances (skin, eye), a gene gun can deliver material directly.

The disease groups targeted in descending order of clinical trials include:

1. **Cancer** (largest number of trials)
2. **Monogenetic diseases** (cystic fibrosis, haemophilia B)
3. **Cardiovascular disease**
4. **Infectious diseases** (predominantly HIV)

**Key milestones in gene therapy:**

- Severe Combined Immune Deficiency (SCID): 18/20 patients alive at 10-year follow-up
- Leber's congenital amaurosis: successful vision restoration
- X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy: halted disease progression
- Beta-thalassemia: transfusion independence achieved
- Alipogene tiparvovec (Glybera): first commercially available gene therapy drug in Europe (2012), AAV vector for familial lipoprotein lipase deficiency
- Gendicine: first gene therapy drug approved for clinical use (China 2004), adenovirus harbouring p53 gene for head and neck cancer

## Part B: Gene Therapy Vectors

The most common vectors are replication-deficient viruses. Each vector type has unique advantages and limitations.

| Feature | Adenovirus | AAV | Retrovirus/Lentivirus | Non-viral |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Family | Adenoviridae | Parvoviridae | Retroviridae | n/a |
| Genome | dsDNA | ssDNA | ssRNA | Unlimited |
| Cell tropism | Dividing + non-dividing | Dividing + non-dividing | Dividing only (retro); both (lenti) | Dividing + non-dividing |
| Integration | Non-integrating | Non-integrating | Integrating | Non-integrating |
| Expression | Transient | Potentially long-lasting | Long-lasting | Transient |
| Capacity | 7.5 kb | 4.5 kb | 8 kb | Unlimited |

### Adenovirus

Adenovirus is a human pathogenic, non-encapsulated DNA virus and the most extensively studied gene therapy vector. It efficiently infects both dividing and non-dividing cells by binding to the cox-adenovirus receptor. Once bound, the virus is internalized, escapes from the endosome and is trafficked to the nucleus very efficiently. Over 50 current open protocols use adenoviral vectors, making it a very versatile means of gene delivery.

**Advantages:** Relatively simple amplification methods for high-titer replication-defective vectors; efficient infection and transgene expression; can be combined with complexes for non-receptor-mediated entry.

**Disadvantages:** Immunogenic (both cell- and humoral-mediated immune responses are activated, especially at doses >10^10 particles), which limits redelivery; transient expression (viral genome does not integrate and is lost with cell division); a subject died in a University of Pennsylvania trial from excessive immune response. "Gutted" vectors, missing almost all viral genes, have been shown to be less immunogenic and express for longer periods in vivo.

### Adeno-associated Virus (AAV)

AAV is a single-stranded encapsulated parvovirus that has emerged as a vector with significant therapeutic promise. AAV has not been associated with any pathologic human disease, making it a safer alternative. It produces very little immunogenicity. In quiescent cells, AAV integrates into the host chromosome; in rapidly dividing cells, it persists as an extrachromosomal episome.

**Advantages:** Minimal immunogenicity; infects dividing and non-dividing cells; potential for long-lasting expression; safe profile.

**Disadvantages:** Limited loading capacity (up to 4.2 kb); inefficient expression and delayed onset of expression. Newer generations of recombinant AAV have shorter turnover of transcription and enhanced gene expression potencies.

### Retrovirus/Lentivirus

Retroviral vectors offer the substantial advantage of persistent gene expression through chromosomal integration. The RNA genome is reverse-transcribed and integrated as a provirus into the host chromosome. Murine retroviruses only infect dividing cells, significantly limiting clinical utility. Lentiviruses (including HIV-derived vectors) infect both dividing and non-dividing cells with greater versatility.

**Advantages:** Persistent gene expression; chromosomal integration; lentivirus infects non-dividing cells.

**Disadvantages:** Risk of insertional mutagenesis (provirus insertion near proto-oncogene may lead to therapy-induced cancer; a patient developed lymphoproliferative disorder in a SCID trial); chromosomal silencing via methylation/acetylation of the promoter may downregulate transgene expression.

### Non-viral Vectors

Non-viral methods include naked DNA (plasmid), liposomes and ballistic gene delivery (gene gun). Liposomes bind to DNA, forming complexes with high affinity for plasma cell membranes, incorporated into cells by endocytosis.

**Advantages:** Simplicity; ease of large-scale production; minimal immune response; non-immunogenic; no potential for insertional mutagenesis; unlimited capacity; safe.

**Disadvantages:** Inefficient gene transfer; not receptor-mediated (poor targeting); transient expression (plasmid lost with cell division). Gene gun is limited to exposed surfaces only.

## Part C: Barriers to Gene Delivery

Several barriers must be overcome for successful gene therapy:

```
BARRIERS TO GENE THERAPY DELIVERY

[1] TARGETING -----> Only affected cells should receive the gene
        |
        v
[2] TRANSFECTION --> Binding and internalization into cells
        |
        v
[3] TRANSDUCTION --> Cellular trafficking to the nucleus
        |              (must escape endosomal degradation)
        v
[4] EXPRESSION ----> Quantity and stability of nuclear expression
```

Each method of gene delivery varies in its ability to overcome these barriers. Additional specific challenges include:

- **Viral receptor location:** In airway epithelium, viral receptors are on the basolateral membrane away from the cell surface; calcium chelators can briefly disrupt tight junctions to allow vector access
- **Immune response:** Humoral and cell-mediated immunity against viral vectors limits efficacy of repeated administration
- **Gene silencing:** Epigenetic mechanisms (methylation, acetylation) may silence transgene expression
- **Targeting specificity:** Achieving cancer cell-specific delivery without affecting normal cells remains difficult in vivo

## Part D: Gene Therapy Strategies in Head & Neck Cancer

In 2000, approximately 130,000 new head and neck cancer cases were diagnosed in developed countries. Despite innovations in surgery, [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] and chemotherapy, survival for advanced-stage squamous cell cancer has not increased over 40 years. Gene therapy approaches in H&N cancer fall into four major categories:

### 1. Chemosensitization (Suicide Gene Therapy)

The transgene is delivered to cancer cells, followed by a second therapy (radiotherapy or chemotherapy) to induce killing in cells expressing the transgene.

- **HSV-TK/Ganciclovir system:** Herpes simplex thymidine kinase (HSV-TK) is delivered to cancer cells. Once expressed, the enzyme converts the prodrug ganciclovir to its toxic nucleoside analogue, inducing cell death. Through the "bystander effect," infected cells spread expressed genes to surrounding cells via cell-cell contacts. Only 50% of cells need to initially express the toxic gene for the desired effect.
- **p53 gene transfer:** Inclusion of p53 sensitizes cancer cells to apoptosis after treatment with radiation or chemotherapy. A phase I trial delivered Ad-p53 intra-lesionally to 33 patients with recurrent HNSCC. In the surgical arm, 4 patients were alive and disease-free at 1 year. Response did not correlate with p53 status.

### 2. Cytokine Gene Transfer (Immune Modulation)

Individuals with H&N cancer lack an effective local immune response even early in disease. The normal immune system fails to recognize tumour cells due to immunological ignorance, downregulation of MHC complexes and loss of costimulatory pathways. Cytokine stimulatory pathways (IL-2, IFN-gamma, IL-12) are depressed in some tumours.

Gene therapy can overexpress downregulated cytokines to break immune dysregulation. Phase I trials using non-viral plasmids expressing IL-2 have been completed, with phase II trials underway. Gene therapy offers the ability to increase local expression while limiting systemic toxicity (systemic IL-2 causes capillary leak syndrome).

### 3. Restorative Gene Therapy (Tumour Suppressor Gene Replacement)

This strategy restores the function of key cellular genes whose dysfunction has caused cancer progression, particularly disrupted apoptotic pathways.

- The most common mutations in HNSCC are **p53** and **p16**
- **Gendicine** (modified adenovirus harbouring p53 gene) was approved in China in 2004 for treatment of H&N cancer -- the first gene therapy approved for clinical use in humans
- The western version (Advexin) was refused FDA approval in the USA in 2008
- Overall efficacy of Ad-p53 therapy continues to be closely evaluated

### 4. Selective Oncolytic Viruses

Infection with wildtype adenovirus results in viral replication and eventual cell lysis. This approach harnesses replication-selective viruses to lyse only cancer cells.

- **ONYX-015:** An adenovirus missing the E1B gene that normally inhibits cellular p53. Since approximately 60% of HNSCC do not express functional p53, ONYX-015 selectively replicates only in cancer cells with p53 mutations.
- Phase I trial showed the virus was safe even at high doses (adverse effects: fever, injection site pain)
- Phase II studies showed combined approach (ONYX-015 + 5-FU + cisplatin) increased response rates from 33% (chemotherapy alone) to 65%
- Potential for intravenous delivery to treat distant metastases; phase I trials confirmed safety of IV delivery

## Part E: Gene Therapy in Cystic Fibrosis -- The Nasal Model

Cystic fibrosis represents a seemingly ideal disease for gene therapy as the most common lethal genetic disease in Caucasians (~30,000 cases in USA, 70,000 worldwide), with carrier rates of 5% and median survival <40 years. CF is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7q, encoding a cAMP-dependent chloride channel. The Delta-F508 mutation accounts for 70% of cases.

The nose and sinuses serve as a model for pulmonary gene therapy due to:

- **Safety:** Lower risk of adverse reaction compared to pulmonary administration
- **Accessibility:** Ease of vector administration and experimental manipulation
- **Measurable endpoint:** The nasal potential difference provides a characteristic "bioelectric phenotype" that differs in CF patients

**Key clinical trials:**

| Vector | Trial | Results |
|---|---|---|
| Adenovirus | Zabner et al. (1993) | Correction of CF bioelectric potential in nasal epithelium |
| Adenovirus | Crystal et al. (1994) | CFTR expression detected; pulmonary toxicity at high dose |
| Adenovirus | Knowles et al. (1995) | Low efficiency gene transfer, no nasal potential correction |
| AAV | Maxillary sinus model | Gene transfer detected at 41 days; no effect on sinusitis |
| Liposome | Caplen et al. | 20% correction of nasal potential peaking at 3 days |
| Liposome | Gill et al. (1997) | Functional CFTR transfer in 6/8 subjects |

Overall, while AAV vectors are safe and stable, the level of CFTR reconstitution has been too low for functional improvement. The challenge remains to translate in vitro success into clinically measurable endpoints.

## Part F: Gene Therapy in Other ENT Applications

Beyond H&N cancer and cystic fibrosis, gene therapy has emerging applications across ENT:

- **Sensorineural hearing loss:** AAV-mediated delivery of neurotrophins via [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]]s to preserve spiral ganglion neurons; gene therapy for Usher syndrome using lentiviral vectors; hair cell regeneration through Atoh1 gene delivery
- **Vestibular disorders:** Gene therapy approaches for vestibular hair cell regeneration using viral vectors
- **Salivary gland dysfunction:** Aquaporin-1 gene delivery via AAV to irradiated parotid glands to restore salivary flow post-radiotherapy
- **[[Recurrent Respiratory Papillomatosis Larynx|Recurrent respiratory papillomatosis]]:** Potential for targeted gene therapy to complement current surgical management

**Current limitations and future directions:**

- Efficiency and targeting remain difficult in vivo despite initial laboratory promise
- Preclinical animal studies show only modest in vivo results
- Methods to increase cancer cell selectivity (redesigning adenovirus binding sites) may improve future human trials
- Self-propagating tumour-selective approaches (ONYX-015 model) offer significant therapeutic promise
- Combined therapies (gene therapy + chemotherapy/radiotherapy) are the most promising clinical strategy
- The next decade's challenge is translation of basic science expertise into safe and effective clinical applications

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The bystander effect in suicide gene therapy means that 100% cancer cell infection is not required -- only 50% of cells need initial gene expression, making this approach more clinically feasible than expected.
> - Gendicine was approved in China but its western counterpart Advexin was refused FDA approval in 2008, reflecting ongoing uncertainty about Ad-p53 efficacy in H&N cancer.
> - A patient death in a University of Pennsylvania adenoviral trial (1999) led to increased scrutiny of human gene therapy trials and highlighted the balance between promise and immunological risk of viral vectors.
> - The nasal model for CF gene therapy has particular relevance to otolaryngologists -- CF sinus disease itself may contribute to life-threatening pulmonary complications, and the nose provides a safer site for vector administration than the lungs.
> - Calcium chelators can disrupt tight epithelial junctions to allow viral vectors access to basolateral receptors in airway epithelium -- a practical solution to the vector access problem.
> - The most promising clinical strategy in H&N cancer gene therapy is combination therapy (gene therapy + conventional chemo/radiotherapy) rather than single-agent gene therapy alone.
