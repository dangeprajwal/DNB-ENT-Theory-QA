## Introduction

Modern otorhinolaryngology relies on advanced imaging technologies that extend beyond conventional radiography to provide functional, vascular, and high-resolution anatomical information. Functional MRI (fMRI) maps brain activity by detecting haemodynamic changes, enabling investigation of auditory cortical function and other neural processes relevant to otology. Doppler ultrasound exploits the frequency shift of reflected sound waves to assess blood flow, with applications spanning thyroid assessment, vascular tumour characterization, neck node evaluation, and intraoperative vessel identification. High-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the temporal bone remains the gold standard for depicting the complex bony architecture of the ear, providing exquisite detail of the external auditory canal, middle ear, ossicular chain, inner ear, and facial nerve canal.

## Part A — Functional MRI (fMRI): Principles and Applications

### Principle of fMRI

Functional MRI detects changes in local blood flow, blood volume, and blood oxygenation that accompany neural activity. The principle relies on the **blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) contrast mechanism**:

1. **Neural activation** — When a brain region is activated, local neuronal metabolism increases
2. **Haemodynamic response** — Increased neural activity triggers increased blood flow to the active region, supplying more oxygenated haemoglobin than is consumed
3. **BOLD signal** — Deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and causes signal loss on T2*-weighted images; oxyhaemoglobin is diamagnetic and does not. The relative increase in oxyhaemoglobin produces a net increase in T2* signal in active brain regions
4. **Statistical mapping** — Repeated task-rest cycles are analysed statistically to identify voxels showing significant signal changes correlated with the stimulus

### Perfusion fMRI

Perfusion fMRI uses **arterial spin labelling (ASL)** to measure cerebral blood flow directly:

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Technique | Magnetically labels arterial water protons as an endogenous tracer |
| Measurement | Quantitative cerebral blood flow (mL/100g/min) |
| Advantage | Provides absolute perfusion values unlike BOLD (relative change only) |
| Limitation | Lower signal-to-noise ratio than BOLD; slower acquisition |
| Application | Assesses baseline perfusion changes in chronic conditions ([[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], hearing loss) |

### Applications of fMRI in Otology

| Application | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Auditory cortex mapping | Maps tonotopic organization; fMRI studies show increasing stimulus intensity produces larger neural response areas |
| Tinnitus | Identifies pathologically active brain areas; electromagnetic therapy (rTMS) directed to these areas |
| [[Cochlear Implant|Cochlear implant]] assessment | Demonstrates neuroanatomical differences in auditory regions between single-sided deaf children and controls |
| Vestibular research | PET and fMRI studies of optokinetic stimulation identify cortical areas for visual motion processing |
| Central auditory processing | Evidence for anterior "what" and posterior "where" processing areas in human auditory cortex |

### Applications of fMRI Beyond Otology

| Application | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Phonation and voice | fMRI shows common volitional control of phonation and exhalation; multiple forebrain systems converge on motor neurones innervating thyroarytenoid |
| Swallowing physiology | EMG recording combined with fMRI studies pharyngeal and oesophageal motor function |
| [[Spasmodic Dysphonia Botulinum Toxin|Spasmodic dysphonia]] | fMRI studies have failed to identify any specific structural defect; aetiology remains idiopathic |
| Olfaction | Presently has limited usefulness in olfactory assessment |

### DWI as "Functional MRI" in Temporal Bone

In temporal bone imaging, the term "functional MRI" is also applied to **diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI)** sequences:

- DWI characterizes tissues based on the mobility of water molecules within them
- Inflammatory disease has good water mobility; [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]] (tightly packed keratin, limited extracellular space) shows reduced water diffusion
- **Non-echo-planar DWI** detects cholesteatoma down to 2–3 mm diameter, replacing routine second-look surgery in many centres
- Cholesteatoma shows high signal on b1000 DWI with matched low signal on ADC map
- Non-EPI DWI confers a positive predictive value of 93–100% for detecting recurrent cholesteatoma

## Part B — Doppler Ultrasound: Principles and ENT Applications

### Principle of Doppler Ultrasound

The Doppler effect describes the change in frequency of a wave reflected from a moving object. In medical ultrasound:

1. **Sound wave transmission** — A transducer emits ultrasound waves that travel into tissue
2. **Reflection from moving blood** — Red blood cells moving within vessels reflect the ultrasound at a shifted frequency
3. **Doppler shift calculation** — The frequency difference between transmitted and received waves is proportional to blood flow velocity
4. **Doppler equation**: Δf = 2f₀ × v × cos θ / c (where f₀ = transmitted frequency, v = blood velocity, θ = angle of insonation, c = speed of sound in tissue)

### Types of Doppler Imaging

| Type | Principle | Application |
|------|-----------|-------------|
| Continuous wave | Continuous transmission/reception; no depth resolution | Detecting flow presence/absence |
| Pulsed wave | Gated transmission allows depth selection | Measuring velocity at specific depth |
| Colour-flow Doppler | Assigns colour to flow direction/velocity overlaid on B-mode image | Visualizing vascular patterns |
| Power Doppler | Displays amplitude (power) of Doppler signal; more sensitive to low flow | Detecting subtle vascularity in tumours |
| Duplex Doppler | Combines B-mode anatomy with Doppler flow data | Comprehensive vessel assessment |

### ENT Applications of Doppler Ultrasound

**Thyroid Assessment:**
- Colour-flow Doppler distinguishes **type 1 (hyperthyroid)** amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (increased flow) from **type 2 (destructive)** form (reduced flow)
- Increased blood flow within a nodule on colour-flow Doppler is suggestive of malignancy
- Papillary carcinoma shows a chaotic vascular pattern on colour-flow Doppler
- Colour-flow Doppler may identify greater vascularity in Graves' disease

**Parathyroid Assessment:**
- 10 MHz or higher transducer for grey-scale and colour Doppler imaging in transverse and longitudinal planes
- Parathyroid adenomas are typically highly vascular; colour Doppler reveals a **peripheral vascular arc** from a vascular pedicle
- Differentiates adenoma (peripheral vascularity) from lymph node (central hilar vascularity)

**Neck Node Assessment:**
- Addition of power Doppler to assess vascular flow improves detection of metastatic cervical lymph nodes
- Benign nodes show low resistive index with high blood flow
- Sonoelastography uses Doppler techniques to image vibration patterns

**Vascular Malformations:**
- AVM staging (Schobinger): Stage I shows AV shunt on Doppler
- Colour Doppler determines flow characteristics and fistulae in vascular malformations

**Salivary Gland Assessment:**
- Doppler evaluates vascularity of salivary gland lesions

### Intraoperative Doppler Applications

| Application | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Pituitary surgery | Endonasal acoustic Doppler sonography identifies vascular structures during endoscopic approach |
| [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|Free flap]] surgery | Percutaneous Doppler of donor perforator vessels for pre-operative planning; implantable flow probes for post-operative flap monitoring |
| Penetrating neck trauma | Colour-flow duplex Doppler combined with CT angiography for vascular injury assessment |
| Nasal blood flow | Laser Doppler flowmetry measures nasal mucosal perfusion |
| Cardiac monitoring | Oesophageal Doppler probe monitors cardiac output during anaesthesia |

## Part C — HRCT Temporal Bone: Principles and Applications

### Scanning Technique

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Modality | Multidetector CT (MDCT) scanning |
| Acquisition | Volume acquisition; rapid scanning time |
| Slice thickness | 0.5–0.6 mm (thin section) |
| Reconstruction | Multiple planes — axial, coronal, sagittal |
| Resolution | Excellent bony detail |
| Limitation | Cannot differentiate between different types of soft tissue opacity (fluid vs cholesteatoma) |
| Radiation concern | More significant in children, adolescents and young adults |

### Anatomical Subdivisions Assessed

The temporal bone is systematically evaluated in five anatomical subdivisions:

| Subdivision | Structures Assessed on HRCT |
|-------------|----------------------------|
| External ear | EAC walls, soft tissue thickening, bony erosions, exostoses |
| Middle ear & mastoid | Ossicular chain integrity, scutum, tegmen tympani, mastoid air cells, aditus |
| Inner ear | Cochlea, vestibule, semicircular canals, cochlear and vestibular aqueducts |
| Internal auditory canal | Width, length, fundus (transverse crest), nerves (MRI preferred) |
| Facial nerve canal | Labyrinthine, tympanic, mastoid segments; geniculate ganglion; dehiscence |

### Anatomical Variants Critical to Report

Pre-operative identification of these variants prevents surgical complications:

- **Low-lying tegmen tympani** — may be breached during mastoid approach
- **Anteriorly placed sigmoid sinus** — thin cortical bone between sinus and mastoid cavity; risk of bleeding
- **High-riding jugular bulb** — projects into hypotympanum; may be dehiscent of bone covering
- **Aberrant internal carotid artery** — traverses middle ear cavity adjacent to cochlear promontory; presents as pulsatile retrotympanic mass
- **Facial nerve canal variants** — nerve may protrude inferiorly into oval window niche, have dehiscent tympanic segment, or follow a truly aberrant course

### HRCT in Specific Temporal Bone Pathology

**Cholesteatoma:**
- CT is the initial imaging method of choice — depicts middle ear opacification and bone erosions of scutum and adjacent ossicles
- Normal CT has extremely high NPV for absence of cholesteatoma
- Limitation: cannot differentiate mucosal disease from cholesteatoma soft tissue mass
- Pars flaccida cholesteatoma: keratin accumulates in Prussak space, causes scutum erosion and medial ossicular displacement
- Pars tensa cholesteatoma: grows medial to ossicular chain, causes long process of incus erosion
- Complications to assess: lateral semicircular canal fistula, cochlear promontory erosion, tegmen breach

**Chronic Otitis Media:**
- Shows middle ear and mastoid effusion with increased sclerosis of adjacent bony structures
- Detects ossicular erosions and chain fixation
- Tympanosclerosis appears as calcifications affecting tympanic membrane or surrounding/fixing ossicles in epitympanum

**[[Otosclerosis|Otosclerosis]]:**
- Thin-section CT shows low-density (demineralized) plaques at fissula antefenestrum (anterior oval window border)
- Plaque may extend to involve stapes footplate, occlude oval window, and extend to cochlear promontory and round window
- Disease is bilateral in up to 85% of cases
- **Retrofenestral disease**: low density extending around otic capsule — the "fourth ring of Valvassori"
- HRCT also used to evaluate alternative causes of CHL and identify anatomical variants before [[Stapes Surgery|stapedectomy]]

**Superior Semicircular Canal Dehiscence:**
- Thin-section CT demonstrates bone thinning and defects of arcuate eminence
- Absent bone over SSC described in up to 13% of asymptomatic individuals
- Images reconstructed in **plane of Pöschl** (parallel to superior canal) and **plane of Stenver** (perpendicular) for accurate demonstration
- 0.5 mm collimated scans with Pöschl plane reformatting advised

**Temporal Bone Fractures:**
- HRCT is imaging method of choice
- **Longitudinal fractures** (90%): more common; associated with CHL from TM involvement, haemotympanum, or ossicular chain disruption; spare inner ear structures
- **Transverse fractures**: pass through otic capsule and IAM; associated with SNHL, facial nerve paralysis, [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]
- Facial nerve paralysis: 50% with transverse fractures (rapid onset, nerve transection) vs 10–20% with longitudinal fractures (delayed onset, contusion/compression)
- Complications: CSF leak, meningitis, meningocoele, vascular injury, labyrinthine ossificans, post-traumatic cholesteatoma

**Necrotising Otitis Externa:**
- Early disease: subtle soft tissue thickening in EAC
- Key finding: subtle cortical bone erosions (osteomyelitis) — comparison with contralateral side is essential
- Spread pathways: petrotympanic fissure → TMJ; medially → carotid canal; inferiorly → subtemporal soft tissues
- MRI better for detecting early central skull base disease (marrow changes on T1 before frank cortical destruction)
- Follow-up: Gallium-67 scans better than 99mTc bone scans (returns to normal more rapidly after infection resolution)

## Part D — CT vs MRI in Temporal Bone Imaging

| Feature | CT (HRCT) | MRI |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| Best for | Bony detail, air cells, ossicles | Soft tissue contrast, cranial nerves |
| Scan time | Very rapid (seconds) | Longer (45–60 minutes) |
| Radiation | Yes (ionizing) | No |
| Inner ear | Excellent bony detail | Visualizes fluid-filled spaces |
| IAC nerves | Limited soft tissue resolution | Excellent (T2 CISS/FIESTA) |
| Cholesteatoma | Depicts bone erosion; cannot differentiate from fluid | DWI detects cholesteatoma (2–3 mm); differentiates from inflammation |
| NOE | Bone erosions visible | Early marrow infiltration visible on T1 before cortical destruction |
| VS | Bony detail of IAC | Investigation of choice (T2 CISS) |
| SCC temporal bone | Bone destruction | Soft tissue extent; highly predictive when combined with CT |
| Contraindication | Pregnancy (relative) | Metal implants, pacemakers, claustrophobia |
| CBCT advantage | Reduced radiation dose (~60% of MDCT), 0.3 mm slices | — |

## Part E — Temporal Bone Projections in Conventional Imaging

Although CT and MRI have largely superseded plain radiography, knowledge of conventional projections remains relevant:

| Projection | Technique | Structures Assessed |
|------------|-----------|---------------------|
| Schüller (lateral oblique) | 30° caudal angulation | Mastoid air cells, tegmen, sigmoid sinus plate, TMJ |
| Stenver (posterior oblique) | 45° rotation, 12° caudal | Petrous pyramid, arcuate eminence, IAC, SSC, vestibule |
| Towne (AP) | 30° caudal to OM line | Petrous apex, arcuate eminence, mastoid, foramen magnum |
| Transorbital (AP modified) | Through orbits | IAC, vestibule, cochlea, SCC |
| Law (lateral) | 15° caudal angulation | Mastoid air cells, EAC, middle ear, aditus |
| Mayer (axial oblique) | 45° rotation, 45° caudal | Middle ear cavity, attic, ossicles, EAC |
| Submentovertex (SMV/basal) | Beam parallel to OM line | Skull base foramina, petrous bones, sphenoid sinus, foramen spinosum |
| Pöschl | Parallel to SSC plane | Superior semicircular canal (SSCD assessment on HRCT) |

**Modern Practice:**
- Conventional radiography has been replaced by HRCT and MRI for almost all temporal bone pathology
- HRCT reconstructions in Pöschl and Stenver planes remain essential for SSCD assessment
- CBCT offers an alternative with reduced radiation dose and resolution comparable to HRCT

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Functional MRI uses BOLD contrast to detect haemodynamic changes accompanying neural activity — deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic (signal loss) while oxyhaemoglobin is diamagnetic, producing signal increase in active regions
> 2. In otology, fMRI maps auditory cortex tonotopic organization, identifies pathologically active areas in tinnitus for rTMS targeting, and demonstrates neuroanatomical differences in cochlear implant candidates
> 3. Perfusion fMRI uses arterial spin labelling to quantify cerebral blood flow absolutely, unlike BOLD which measures only relative signal changes
> 4. Doppler ultrasound measures blood flow velocity from the frequency shift of reflected ultrasound waves; colour-flow Doppler distinguishes type 1 (hyperthyroid) from type 2 (destructive) amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis
> 5. In parathyroid assessment, colour Doppler reveals a characteristic peripheral vascular arc from a pedicle in parathyroid adenomas, differentiating them from lymph nodes (central hilar vascularity)
> 6. Intraoperative Doppler applications include endonasal acoustic Doppler in pituitary surgery, free flap perforator vessel assessment, and vascular injury evaluation in penetrating neck trauma
> 7. HRCT temporal bone uses 0.5–0.6 mm thin-section multidetector CT providing excellent bony detail of five anatomical subdivisions: external ear, middle ear/mastoid, inner ear, IAC, and facial nerve canal
> 8. Key anatomical variants to report pre-operatively include low tegmen, anteriorly placed sigmoid sinus, high-riding jugular bulb, aberrant ICA, and facial nerve canal dehiscence
> 9. HRCT in otosclerosis shows low-density plaques at fissula antefenestrum; retrofenestral disease shows the "fourth ring of Valvassori" around the otic capsule; bilateral in up to 85%
> 10. Non-echo-planar DWI MRI detects cholesteatoma down to 2–3 mm with PPV 93–100%, replacing routine second-look surgery; cholesteatoma shows high signal on b1000 with matched low ADC


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **fMRI has limited direct clinical utility in ENT currently** — its main role is in research (auditory processing, tinnitus, phonation); in temporal bone imaging, "functional MRI" refers to DWI sequences for cholesteatoma
> - **Doppler in thyroid is most useful for amiodarone thyrotoxicosis** — type 1 (increased flow, treat with thionamides) vs type 2 (reduced flow, treat with corticosteroids); this differentiation directly alters management
> - **Compare with contralateral side in NOE** — early cortical bone erosions may be subtle; MRI T1 detects marrow infiltration before CT shows cortical destruction
> - **Pöschl and Stenver planes are essential for SSCD** — standard axial and coronal CT may overestimate dehiscence; 0.5 mm collimation with Pöschl reformats improves specificity
> - **HRCT before stapedectomy is now obligatory in some countries** — identifies anatomical variants (dehiscent facial nerve, aberrant ICA) that could lead to catastrophic intraoperative complications
> - **Longitudinal fractures (90%) cause CHL; transverse fractures cause SNHL** — facial nerve paralysis with transverse fractures has rapid onset (transection) requiring early exploration; delayed onset with longitudinal fractures (contusion) often recovers spontaneously
