# Oropharyngeal Candidiasis, Amphotericin B and HAART

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 21 (Acute Invasive [[AFRS Fungal Ball|Fungal Rhinosinusitis]]), Chapter 22 (Antimicrobials); Volume 3, Chapter 40 (Neck Space Infections), Chapter 60 (HIV and the Upper Aerodigestive Tract)

## INTRODUCTION

Oropharyngeal candidiasis (OPC) is the most common opportunistic fungal infection of the head and neck, with particular significance in immunocompromised patients including those with HIV/AIDS, patients receiving chemotherapy or [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], and those on prolonged antibiotic or corticosteroid therapy. Amphotericin B remains the gold standard antifungal for severe invasive fungal disease, though its significant toxicity has led to the development of lipid formulations including liposomal amphotericin B. The introduction of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) has dramatically reduced the incidence of opportunistic infections in HIV patients, although OPC remains prevalent in those with poor access to treatment or resistant disease.

## PART A: OROPHARYNGEAL CANDIDIASIS

### Aetiology

**Causative organism:** Candida albicans is the most common species (>80%). Non-albicans species include C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. tropicalis and C. parapsilosis — these are increasingly prevalent and often fluconazole-resistant.

**Predisposing factors:**
- **Immunosuppression:** HIV/AIDS (81% of patients with advanced HIV colonized by oral candida), chemotherapy, transplant immunosuppression
- **Local factors:** Inhaled corticosteroids (without mouth rinsing), dentures, xerostomia, irradiation of head and neck
- **Systemic factors:** Diabetes mellitus, extremes of age (neonates, elderly), malnutrition
- **Antibiotics:** Broad-spectrum antibiotics disrupt normal oral flora allowing candidal overgrowth
- **Head and neck cancer:** Radiotherapy and chemotherapy-induced mucosal damage and immunosuppression

### Pathogenesis

Candida is a commensal organism of the oral cavity in approximately 50% of the normal population. Transition from commensal to pathogenic state occurs when host defences are compromised. Candida adheres to oral mucosal epithelium via cell surface adhesins and produces secreted aspartyl proteinases and phospholipases that facilitate tissue invasion.

### Clinical Presentation

**Types of OPC:**
- **Pseudomembranous (thrush):** White plaques on erythematous mucosa that can be scraped off — the classic presentation
- **Erythematous (atrophic):** Red, smooth, atrophic areas on palate and dorsal tongue — often seen with denture use
- **Hyperplastic (candidal leukoplakia):** White plaques that cannot be scraped off — may have premalignant potential
- **Angular cheilitis:** Fissures and erythema at the oral commissures

### Diagnosis

- Clinical appearance is usually sufficient for diagnosis
- KOH preparation or Gram stain of scraping showing pseudohyphae and budding yeast cells
- Culture on Sabouraud's dextrose agar for speciation and sensitivity testing
- Biopsy if hyperplastic form (to exclude dysplasia)

### Management

**Mild disease:**
- Topical nystatin suspension (100,000 units/mL, 1 mL QDS for 7-14 days)
- Nystatin lozenges/pastilles
- Amphotericin B lozenges

**Moderate to severe disease:**
- Oral fluconazole 100-200 mg daily for 7-14 days (recommended first-line for moderate/severe OPC)
- In H&N cancer patients: fluconazole 200 mg daily for 14 days

**Fluconazole-refractory disease:**
- Itraconazole oral suspension
- Posaconazole suspension (400 mg BD for 3 days, then 400 mg daily)
- Voriconazole

**Refractory candidiasis (severe immunosuppression):**
- Intravenous amphotericin B
- Echinocandins (caspofungin, micafungin)

### Prophylaxis

- Fluconazole prophylaxis should be used with caution — emergence of fluconazole-resistant Candida species is becoming a significant problem, especially in the HIV population
- Increasing resistance of Candida species to fluconazole is a growing concern
- Nystatin prophylaxis is preferred for lower-risk patients (less resistance concern)
- For inhaled corticosteroid users: spacer devices and mouth rinsing after inhalation
- Denture hygiene and overnight removal

### Laryngeal Candidiasis

Candida infection of the larynx may complicate inhaled corticosteroid use or immunosuppression. Treatment is with fluconazole for 3-4 weeks (preferred agent for candida infection). Itraconazole is particularly effective for aspergillus infections of the larynx.

## PART B: AMPHOTERICIN B

### Mechanism of Action

Amphotericin B is a polyene antifungal antibiotic. It binds to **ergosterol** in the fungal cell membrane, forming pores (channels) that alter membrane permeability. This causes leakage of intracellular contents (potassium, amino acids, nucleotides) leading to cell death. The selectivity of amphotericin B for fungal cells arises from its greater affinity for ergosterol (fungal membranes) compared to cholesterol (mammalian cell membranes). However, some binding to cholesterol does occur, which accounts for its toxicity to human cells.

### Antifungal Spectrum

Amphotericin B has the broadest spectrum of any antifungal agent:

| Susceptible Organisms | Resistant Organisms |
|-----------------------|---------------------|
| Candida albicans | C. lusitaniae (intrinsic) |
| Most non-albicans Candida | Some C. krusei strains |
| Aspergillus species | Fusarium species (variable) |
| Mucor/Rhizopus ([[Invasive Fungal Rhinosinusitis|Mucormycosis]]) | Pseudallescheria boydii |
| Cryptococcus neoformans | |
| Histoplasma capsulatum | |
| Blastomyces dermatitidis | |
| Coccidioides immitis | |

### Mode of Administration

**Conventional amphotericin B deoxycholate:**
- Intravenous infusion only (poorly absorbed orally)
- Test dose: 1 mg in 20 mL 5% dextrose over 20-30 minutes
- Therapeutic dose: 0.5-1.5 mg/kg/day by slow IV infusion over 2-6 hours
- Duration: weeks to months depending on infection
- Premedication with paracetamol, antihistamine and hydrocortisone to prevent infusion-related reactions
- Oral formulation (lozenges/suspension) available for topical mucosal treatment only

### Toxicity

**Infusion-related reactions (common):**
- Fever, rigors, chills (60-80% of patients)
- Nausea, vomiting, headache
- Hypotension, tachycardia
- Phlebitis at infusion site

**Nephrotoxicity (major dose-limiting toxicity):**
- Occurs in virtually all patients receiving IV therapy
- Mechanism: renal vasoconstriction + direct tubular toxicity
- Manifests as decreased GFR, renal tubular acidosis, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia
- Worsened by concurrent nephrotoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, cyclosporin)
- Partially reversible on cessation; permanent damage with prolonged use

**Haematological:**
- Normochromic normocytic anaemia (suppression of erythropoietin production)

**Hepatotoxicity:**
- Elevated liver enzymes (less common than nephrotoxicity)

## PART C: LIPOSOMAL AMPHOTERICIN B

### Rationale for Development

The significant toxicity of conventional amphotericin B deoxycholate, particularly nephrotoxicity, led to the development of safer lipid formulations that maintain antifungal efficacy while reducing toxicity.

### Available Lipid Formulations

| Formulation | Proprietary Name | Key Feature |
|-------------|-----------------|-------------|
| Liposomal amphotericin B | AmBisome | Drug enclosed in liposomes |
| Amphotericin B lipid complex | Abelcet | Drug complexed with lipids |
| Amphotericin B colloidal dispersion | Amphotec | Drug in disc-shaped particles |

### Liposomal Amphotericin B (AmBisome)

**Dosing:** 3-5 mg/kg/day IV (can be used at up to 5-10 mg/kg/day)

**Advantages over conventional amphotericin B:**
- Less nephrotoxic — can be used at higher concentrations for longer
- Fewer infusion-related reactions
- Safer in cancer patients, particularly with mucormycosis
- May be more effective due to higher achievable doses
- Preferred formulation for immunocompromised patients

**Specific indications:**
- Acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis (AIFR) — first-line along with surgical debridement
- Mucormycosis — liposomal formulation particularly important as higher doses can be given safely
- Empirical antifungal therapy in febrile neutropenia
- Invasive aspergillosis (second-line after voriconazole)
- Visceral leishmaniasis

**Disadvantages:**
- Significantly more expensive than conventional amphotericin B
- Still requires IV administration
- Some nephrotoxicity still occurs at high doses

### Treatment Algorithm for Invasive Fungal Sinusitis

1. **First-line:** Liposomal amphotericin B (5-10 mg/kg/day) + aggressive surgical debridement
2. Once mucormycosis is ruled out → can switch to **voriconazole** (less toxic, more effective against Aspergillus)
3. Voriconazole dose: 6 mg/kg IV for 2 doses, then 4 mg/kg IV every 12 hours
4. **Step-down:** Posaconazole oral when clinical improvement seen, for long-term treatment
5. Other options: itraconazole, caspofungin (echinocandin)

## PART D: HAART — HIGHLY ACTIVE ANTIRETROVIRAL THERAPY

### Definition

HAART (Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy) refers to the use of combinations of three or more antiretroviral drugs from at least two different drug classes to suppress HIV replication and restore immune function. Since the introduction of HAART, the incidence of opportunistic infections including OPC has significantly declined, although it remains a problem in patients with limited access to treatment, poor adherence or drug-resistant HIV.

### Classification of Antiretroviral Drugs

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| NRTIs (Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors) | Incorporate into viral DNA chain → terminate elongation | Zidovudine (AZT), Lamivudine (3TC), Tenofovir, Emtricitabine, Abacavir |
| NNRTIs (Non-Nucleoside RTIs) | Bind directly to and inhibit reverse transcriptase | Efavirenz, Nevirapine, Rilpivirine, Etravirine |
| PIs (Protease Inhibitors) | Block HIV protease → prevent maturation of virions | Lopinavir/ritonavir, Atazanavir, Darunavir |
| INSTIs (Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors) | Block integration of viral DNA into host genome | Dolutegravir, Raltegravir, Elvitegravir |
| Entry Inhibitors | Block viral entry into CD4 cells | Maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist), Enfuvirtide (fusion inhibitor) |

### Standard HAART Regimens

A typical HAART regimen consists of a **backbone** (2 NRTIs) plus a **third agent** (NNRTI, PI or INSTI):

**Preferred first-line regimens:**
- Tenofovir + Emtricitabine + Dolutegravir (INSTI-based — currently recommended first-line)
- Tenofovir + Emtricitabine + Efavirenz (NNRTI-based — widely used in resource-limited settings)

### Merits of HAART

- Dramatic reduction in AIDS-related mortality (>80% reduction since introduction)
- Restoration of immune function (CD4 count recovery)
- Reduced incidence of opportunistic infections including OPC, PCP, CMV, Kaposi's sarcoma
- Reduction of viral load to undetectable levels (<50 copies/mL) in compliant patients
- Prevention of mother-to-child transmission
- Reduced HIV transmission risk (Treatment as Prevention — U=U: Undetectable = Untransmittable)
- Improved quality of life and life expectancy approaching normal

### Demerits of HAART

- **Lifelong therapy required** — no cure; viral rebound on cessation
- **Drug resistance:** Develops with poor adherence; limits future treatment options
- **Side effects:** Lipodystrophy (fat redistribution), metabolic syndrome (dyslipidemia, insulin resistance), hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity (tenofovir), bone mineral density loss, lactic acidosis (NRTIs), CNS effects (efavirenz)
- **Drug interactions:** Protease inhibitors inhibit CYP3A4 (extensive interactions)
- **Cost:** Especially in resource-limited settings; newer agents more expensive
- **Adherence requirement:** >95% adherence needed for sustained viral suppression
- **Does not prevent all OI:** Fluconazole-resistant candidiasis remains problematic; HAART does not fully prevent oropharyngeal candidiasis

### ENT Relevance of HAART

- Reduction in head and neck manifestations of AIDS (OPC, oral hairy leukoplakia, Kaposi's sarcoma, NHL)
- Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) may cause paradoxical worsening of lymphadenopathy after HAART initiation
- Persistent OPC in HAART-treated patients suggests drug resistance or poor adherence
- Parotid lymphoepithelial cysts may partially resolve with HAART

## PART E: ANTIMICROBIAL THERAPY FOR H&N INFECTIONS

### Microbiological Investigation

**Sampling methods:**
- Pus aspiration (preferred — increases isolation of anaerobes, reduces skin contaminant detection compared to swabs)
- Microbial swabs from fistulae or infected dental sockets
- Blood cultures if systemic sepsis suspected
- Routine pharyngeal swabs must NOT be taken (risk of triggering gag reflex and airway crisis in DNSIs)
- Failure to culture causative bacteria occurs in approximately 25% of cases

**Laboratory tests:**
- Gram stain for rapid preliminary identification
- Aerobic and anaerobic culture
- Sensitivity testing (broth dilution/disc diffusion — determines MIC)
- Molecular techniques (CBNAAT for TB)

### Radiological Investigation

- **Contrast-enhanced CT:** Gold standard imaging for [[Deep Neck Space Infections|deep neck space infection]]s; field should include mediastinum (communication of visceral, retropharyngeal and danger spaces)
- Abscesses demonstrate characteristic rim enhancement
- **Ultrasound:** Determines if abscess has sufficiently liquefied for drainage; useful for targeted aspiration
- **MRI with contrast:** Demonstrates sinus tracts; superior soft-tissue detail
- **OPG:** For infections of dental origin or unknown aetiology

### Antimicrobial Agents of Choice

**Common organisms in DNSIs:**
- Peptostreptococcus spp., Viridans Group Streptococci
- Staphylococcus aureus, S. epidermidis
- Klebsiella pneumoniae
- Polymicrobial cultures are common

**First-line antibiotics:**
- Amoxicillin/clavulanate (co-amoxiclav)
- OR 2nd/3rd generation cephalosporins WITH metronidazole

**Second-line agents:**
- Clindamycin
- Vancomycin (for MRSA or drug allergy)

**Resistance patterns:**
- Clindamycin resistance: 11-18%
- Erythromycin resistance: 14%
- Penicillin resistance: 7-8%
- Aminopenicillin resistance: 0-3%

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Oropharyngeal candidiasis is the most common opportunistic fungal infection in the H&N; 81% of patients with advanced HIV are colonized by oral candida; C. albicans is the predominant species
> 2. Oral fluconazole (100-200 mg daily for 7-14 days) is the recommended first-line treatment for moderate/severe OPC; topical nystatin for mild disease; increasing fluconazole resistance is a significant concern
> 3. Amphotericin B binds to ergosterol in the fungal cell membrane forming pores that cause leakage of intracellular contents — it has the broadest antifungal spectrum of any agent including activity against mucormycosis
> 4. Nephrotoxicity is the major dose-limiting toxicity of conventional amphotericin B — occurs in virtually all patients; manifests as decreased GFR, renal tubular acidosis, hypokalemia
> 5. Liposomal amphotericin B (AmBisome, 5-10 mg/kg/day) is less nephrotoxic, can be given at higher doses for longer, and is safer in cancer patients — first-line for acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis
> 6. HAART uses combinations of 3+ antiretroviral drugs from at least 2 drug classes — standard regimen: 2 NRTIs backbone + INSTI/NNRTI/PI as third agent
> 7. HAART has reduced AIDS-related mortality by >80% and restored near-normal life expectancy; main demerits are lifelong requirement, drug resistance with poor adherence, metabolic side effects
> 8. For deep neck space infections, first-line antibiotics are amoxicillin/clavulanate OR cephalosporin + metronidazole; contrast-enhanced CT is the gold-standard imaging
> 9. Pus aspiration is preferred over swab for microbiological sampling of neck abscesses — increases anaerobe isolation and reduces skin contaminant detection
> 10. Voriconazole (6 mg/kg IV x2, then 4 mg/kg q12h) is now recommended as first-line for invasive aspergillosis by IDSA; liposomal amphotericin B remains first-line for mucormycosis


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Fluconazole prophylaxis for OPC should be used with extreme caution because of the emergence of resistant species — C. krusei and C. glabrata are intrinsically fluconazole-resistant and their prevalence is increasing in the HIV and cancer populations
> - When an immunocompromised patient fails to respond to fluconazole for OPC, the differential includes fluconazole-resistant candida species, non-candidal infection (aspergillosis, CMV, HSV) and mucosal malignancy — speciation and sensitivity testing is essential
> - Conventional amphotericin B test dose (1 mg IV over 20-30 minutes) is administered first to detect hypersensitivity before the full therapeutic dose — premedication with paracetamol, antihistamine and hydrocortisone reduces infusion reactions
> - In acute invasive fungal rhinosinusitis, treatment must be started empirically with liposomal amphotericin B before culture results are available (culture sensitivity is only 54%) — frozen section biopsy (sensitivity 84%) provides faster diagnosis
> - Routine pharyngeal swabs must NOT be taken in patients with deep neck space infections — the gag reflex can precipitate airway crisis in patients with compromised airways from parapharyngeal or [[Retropharyngeal Abscess|retropharyngeal abscess]]es
> - IRIS after HAART initiation can mimic lymphoma with rapidly enlarging cervical lymphadenopathy — it is a diagnosis of exclusion; biopsy may be required to differentiate from lymphoma in HIV patients
