# Drugs in Acute Vestibular Crisis and Classification of Ototoxic Agents

**Question 1:** *Describe five main drugs in acute vestibular crisis — dose, side effects and contraindications.*

**Question 2:** *Classify ototoxic agents and discuss mechanism of ototoxicity.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 76 (Medical Management of Vertigo), Chapter 10 (Management of the Hearing Impaired Child), and Volume 1, Chapter 2 (Genetics of Aminoglycoside Toxicity)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Acute vestibular crisis refers to a sudden onset of severe rotatory vertigo, nausea, vomiting and postural instability, most commonly caused by [[Vestibular Neuritis|vestibular neuritis]], [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]] or [[BPPV|benign paroxysmal positional vertigo]]. The immediate management involves vestibular sedatives and antiemetics to control symptoms, while the underlying cause is addressed. Drug-induced ototoxicity is a potentially preventable cause of acquired hearing loss and vestibular dysfunction. The mechanism involves action on the apoptotic pathway of cochleovestibular hair cells where generation of reactive oxygen species is a rate-limiting step. Recognition of ototoxic agents and monitoring protocols are essential to minimize permanent cochlear and vestibular damage.

---

## PART A: DRUGS IN ACUTE VESTIBULAR CRISIS

### Principles of Treatment

Vestibular sedatives and antiemetics provide symptomatic relief during the acute phase of vestibular crisis. Their use should be limited to the acute attack (usually 48-72 hours) rather than for chronic symptoms, as prolonged use delays central vestibular compensation and prolongs recovery.

### Five Main Drug Classes

**1. Prochlorperazine (Stemetil)**

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Class | Phenothiazine — dopamine D2 receptor antagonist |
| Dose | 5-10 mg oral TDS; 12.5 mg IM; 25 mg PR |
| Onset | 30-60 minutes (oral); rapid (IM) |
| Mechanism | Acts on chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) and vomiting centre; vestibular suppressant via D2 blockade |
| Side effects | **Extrapyramidal effects** (dystonia, oculogyric crisis — especially in children), sedation, postural hypotension, dry mouth |
| Contraindications | Children under 10 kg; Parkinson's disease; bone marrow depression; prolactinoma |
| Antidote for EPS | Procyclidine by injection for acute extrapyramidal reactions |

**2. Cinnarizine (Stugeron)**

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Class | Antihistamine (H1 antagonist) + calcium channel blocker |
| Dose | 15-30 mg TDS orally |
| Mechanism | H1 antihistamine action on vestibular nuclei + calcium channel blockade reducing vestibular hair cell excitability |
| Side effects | Drowsiness, weight gain, GI disturbance, dry mouth |
| Contraindications | Parkinson's disease (may worsen), porphyria |
| Notes | Can be used for prolonged attacks; also effective in vestibular migraine |

**3. Betahistine (Serc)**

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Class | Histamine analogue |
| Dose | 8-16 mg TDS orally; high-dose up to 480 mg/day reported |
| Mechanism | Weak agonist at histamine H1 receptors; potent antagonist at histamine H3 receptors; improves microcirculation in stria vascularis |
| Indication | Specifically marketed for Meniere's disease |
| Side effects | GI disturbance, headache, skin rash |
| Contraindications | Phaeochromocytoma; active peptic ulcer disease; asthma (caution) |
| Evidence | Cochrane review: insufficient evidence that betahistine has definitive effect on vertigo control in Meniere's; meta-analysis (with caveats) shows benefit |

**4. Cyclizine (Valoid)**

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Class | Antihistamine (H1 antagonist) — piperazine derivative |
| Dose | 50 mg oral/IM/IV TDS |
| Mechanism | H1 receptor blockade in vomiting centre and vestibular nuclei; also has anticholinergic properties |
| Side effects | Drowsiness, dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention |
| Contraindications | Severe heart failure (IV route); narrow-angle glaucoma; prostatic hypertrophy |
| Notes | Effective antiemetic for acute vestibular attacks |

**5. Ondansetron (Zofran)**

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Class | 5-HT3 (serotonin) receptor antagonist |
| Dose | 4-8 mg oral/IV/IM BD-TDS |
| Mechanism | Blocks serotonin binding at vagal afferents in the gut and in regions of the CNS involved in emesis |
| Primary use | Post-operative nausea and vomiting; chemotherapy-induced emesis |
| Role in vestibular crisis | Powerful antiemetic, especially when vomiting is prominent; used in the vertiginous patient with persistent vomiting |
| Side effects | Constipation, headache, flushing, QT prolongation (rare) |
| Contraindications | Congenital long QT syndrome; caution with other QT-prolonging drugs |

### Additional Agents

**Hyoscine (scopolamine):** Anticholinergic vestibular sedative; available as transdermal patch (1.5 mg/72 hours); effective for motion sickness and acute vertigo; side effects include dry mouth, drowsiness, urinary retention, blurred vision; contraindicated in narrow-angle glaucoma.

**Domperidone:** Peripheral dopamine antagonist; useful for associated nausea/vomiting; does not cross blood-brain barrier (fewer CNS side effects than prochlorperazine); 10-20 mg TDS; QT prolongation risk.

**Diazepam:** Benzodiazepine; 2-5 mg oral/IV; GABAergic vestibular suppressant; potent but causes significant sedation; reserved for severe acute episodes; risk of dependence.

## PART B: CLASSIFICATION OF OTOTOXIC AGENTS

### Definition

Ototoxicity refers to pharmacological agents that can cause a hearing loss with or without vestibular loss, usually at the cochlear level.

### Classification

**1. Aminoglycoside antibiotics:**
- Gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, streptomycin, neomycin
- Gentamicin and streptomycin: predominantly vestibulotoxic
- Neomycin, amikacin, kanamycin: predominantly cochleotoxic
- Tobramycin: both cochleotoxic and vestibulotoxic

**2. Chemotherapeutic agents:**
- Platinum derivatives: **cisplatin** (most ototoxic) and **carboplatin**
- Vinca alkaloids: vincristine, vinblastine
- Cyclophosphamide
- Methotrexate

**3. Loop diuretics:**
- **Frusemide** (furosemide)
- **Ethacrynic acid** (most ototoxic diuretic)
- Usually reversible; potentiated by concurrent aminoglycosides

**4. Anti-malarials:**
- Chloroquine
- Quinine — causes [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] and hearing loss (cinchonism)
- Hydroxychloroquine

**5. Salicylates and NSAIDs:**
- Aspirin — reversible tinnitus and hearing loss at high doses
- Other NSAIDs — usually reversible

**6. Iron chelators:**
- Desferrioxamine — used in thalassaemia; causes SNHL

**7. Other agents:**
- Erythromycin (high IV doses)
- Vancomycin (especially combined with aminoglycosides)
- Interferon-alpha
- Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil — rare)

## PART C: MECHANISMS OF OTOTOXICITY

### Common Pathway — Reactive Oxygen Species

The mechanism of ototoxicity involves action on the apoptotic pathway of cochleovestibular hair cells where **generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a rate-limiting step.** Ototoxic agents promote ROS formation, overwhelming the antioxidant defence systems of inner ear cells, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage and programmed cell death (apoptosis).

### Agent-Specific Mechanisms

**Aminoglycosides:**
- Enter hair cells via mechanotransduction channels at the stereocilia tips
- Accumulate in lysosomes and disrupt mitochondrial function
- Generate reactive oxygen species → apoptosis of outer hair cells (cochlea) and Type I hair cells (vestibule)
- High-frequency hearing loss initially (basal turn), progressing apically
- Damage is dose-dependent and cumulative; may progress after drug cessation
- Vestibulotoxicity: excitotoxicity through NMDA receptor binding

**Cisplatin:**
- Generates ROS via NADPH oxidase and xanthine oxidase pathways
- Produces DNA cross-links in outer hair cells and stria vascularis
- Depletes glutathione (endogenous antioxidant)
- High-frequency SNHL initially; bilateral, symmetrical, irreversible
- Dose-dependent and cumulative; ototoxicity monitoring essential

**Loop diuretics:**
- Disrupt ionic transport in the stria vascularis
- Frusemide inhibits Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC1) in stria vascularis → reduction of endocochlear potential
- Usually reversible when drug is discontinued
- Potentiated by concurrent aminoglycoside use (synergistic toxicity)

**Salicylates:**
- Inhibit prestin (motor protein of outer hair cells) → reversible OHC dysfunction
- Reduce cochlear blood flow via cyclooxygenase inhibition
- Reversible tinnitus and hearing loss

### Genetic Susceptibility to Aminoglycoside Ototoxicity

Genetic susceptibilities are reported with both gentamicin and cisplatin ototoxicity:

- The mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene is a hotspot for mutations associated with aminoglycoside-induced and non-syndromic hearing loss
- The **1555A>G and 1494C>T mutations** at the highly conserved decoding region of the 12S rRNA are associated with ototoxicity worldwide
- These mutations make human mitochondrial ribosomes more bacteria-like, altering aminoglycoside binding sites
- In carriers of the 1555A>G mutation, aminoglycoside exposure is sufficient but not always necessary for hearing loss to occur — additional environmental and genetic risk factors likely exist
- At-risk populations: patients with cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, or anyone in whom aminoglycoside use is being considered

### Ototoxicity Monitoring Protocols

Chemotherapy ototoxicity monitoring protocols with subjective and objective assessment of hearing and vestibular function are required to adjust therapeutic dosage:
- Serial pure tone audiometry (extended high-frequency audiometry >8 kHz is most sensitive for early detection)
- OAEs for outer hair cell function monitoring
- Vestibular function tests (caloric testing, vHIT) for vestibulotoxicity
- Drug level monitoring (aminoglycoside trough levels)
- Pre-treatment baseline audiogram mandatory before cisplatin chemotherapy

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Prochlorperazine (phenothiazine D2 antagonist) is a potent vestibular suppressant but carries risk of extrapyramidal effects — especially dangerous in children under 10 kg; treated with procyclidine injection
> 2. Cinnarizine (H1 antihistamine + calcium channel blocker) is useful for prolonged vestibular attacks; betahistine (H1 agonist/H3 antagonist) is specifically marketed for Meniere's disease but Cochrane evidence is insufficient
> 3. Ondansetron (5-HT3 antagonist) is a powerful antiemetic, useful in vestibular crisis with prominent vomiting — originally for chemotherapy-induced emesis
> 4. Vestibular sedatives should be limited to 48-72 hours of acute use — prolonged use delays central vestibular compensation and prolongs recovery
> 5. Ototoxicity mechanism: action on the apoptotic pathway of cochleovestibular hair cells where generation of reactive oxygen species is the rate-limiting step
> 6. Aminoglycosides: gentamicin/streptomycin are predominantly vestibulotoxic; neomycin/amikacin are predominantly cochleotoxic — damage is dose-dependent, cumulative and may progress after cessation
> 7. Cisplatin is the most ototoxic chemotherapeutic agent — produces bilateral symmetrical high-frequency SNHL that is irreversible and cumulative
> 8. Loop diuretics (frusemide, ethacrynic acid) cause usually reversible ototoxicity by disrupting endocochlear potential via stria vascularis NKCC1 inhibition — synergistic toxicity with aminoglycosides
> 9. Mitochondrial 12S rRNA mutations (1555A>G, 1494C>T) make ribosomes bacteria-like and increase susceptibility to aminoglycoside ototoxicity — genotyping should be considered before aminoglycoside use in at-risk populations
> 10. Ototoxicity monitoring (serial extended high-frequency audiometry, OAEs, drug level monitoring) is mandatory during cisplatin chemotherapy and aminoglycoside therapy to allow dose adjustment


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Prochlorperazine buccal tablets (3 mg Buccastem) are a practical option in acute vestibular crisis when the patient is vomiting and unable to take oral medication — dissolved in the buccal sulcus without swallowing
> - The combination of frusemide and aminoglycosides is synergistically ototoxic and should be avoided whenever possible — if both are required, drug levels must be closely monitored and hearing tested serially
> - Betahistine at standard doses (48 mg/day) has equivocal evidence, but high-dose betahistine (up to 480 mg/day) has been reported to be effective in Meniere's disease in case series — this remains controversial
> - The 1555A>G mitochondrial mutation is particularly relevant in patients with cystic fibrosis who receive frequent aminoglycoside courses — screening before first aminoglycoside exposure is increasingly recommended
> - Cisplatin ototoxicity is irreversible and cumulative, affecting high frequencies first — extended high-frequency audiometry (>8 kHz) detects loss before conventional audiometry and allows dose modification before speech-frequency hearing is affected
> - Hyoscine patches are a useful non-oral route for vestibular sedation but take 6-8 hours to achieve therapeutic levels — they are better suited for prevention (e.g. motion sickness, post-operative vertigo) than for treatment of established acute crisis
