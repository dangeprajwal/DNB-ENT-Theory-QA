# Branchial Anomalies — Aetiopathogenesis and Type 1 Branchial Cleft Anomaly

**Question 1:** *Aetiopathogenesis of various branchial anomalies. Clinical features and management of type 1 branchial cleft anomaly.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 41 (Cysts and Sinuses of the Head and Neck)


> [!question] Questions Covered
> - Aetiopathogenesis of various branchial anomalies. Clinical features and management of type 1 branchial cleft anomaly.

---

## INTRODUCTION

Branchial anomalies arise from incomplete obliteration of the branchial apparatus during embryonic development. The branchial arches, clefts and pharyngeal pouches give rise to a variety of head and neck structures, and persistence of their remnants leads to congenital cysts, sinuses and fistulae. While second branchial cleft anomalies account for up to 90% of all branchial lesions, first branchial cleft anomalies are rare (less than 10%) but clinically important because of their intimate relationship with the facial nerve. Misdiagnosis is common, with many children undergoing multiple incisions and drainages for recurrent periauricular or upper neck abscesses before the correct diagnosis is made.

---

## PART A: EMBRYOLOGY OF THE BRANCHIAL APPARATUS

### Structure of the Branchial Apparatus

The branchial apparatus consists of paired arches, clefts and pouches that develop during weeks 4-8 of gestation:

- **Branchial arches:** Mesodermal structures that form muscle, cartilage and bone
- **Branchial clefts:** Spaces between the arches on the outside of the embryo, lined with ectoderm
- **Pharyngeal pouches:** Spaces between the arches on the inside of the embryo (as arches bulge into the foregut), lined with endoderm

### Derivatives of Each Arch

| Arch | Nerve | Muscles | Skeletal Elements |
|------|-------|---------|-------------------|
| 1st | Trigeminal (V) | Muscles of mastication, mylohyoid, anterior belly digastric, tensor tympani, tensor veli palatini | Meckel's cartilage → malleus, incus, mandible |
| 2nd | Facial (VII) | Muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior belly digastric | Reichert's cartilage → stapes, styloid process, lesser horn of hyoid |
| 3rd | Glossopharyngeal (IX) | Stylopharyngeus | Greater horn of hyoid, body of hyoid |
| 4th | Superior laryngeal (vagus X) | Cricothyroid, constrictors | Thyroid cartilage |
| 6th | Recurrent laryngeal (vagus X) | Intrinsic laryngeal muscles (except cricothyroid) | Cricoid, arytenoids |

### Types of Branchial Anomalies

**Branchial cleft anomalies** (ectodermal origin): Result in a congenital sinus with an opening on a skin surface. Location depends on the cleft of origin:
- First cleft → opening around the angle of mandible or in the EAC
- Second cleft → opening in the neck along the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid

**Pharyngeal pouch anomalies** (endodermal origin): Result in a sinus with an opening on a mucosal surface, typically in the pyriform fossa (third or fourth pouch).

**Fistulae:** Complete tracts connecting a skin opening to a mucosal opening, occurring when both the cleft and pouch persist.

## PART B: FIRST BRANCHIAL CLEFT ANOMALIES

### Epidemiology

First branchial cleft sinuses are rare, accounting for less than 10% of branchial cleft anomalies. They are frequently misdiagnosed, with children undergoing multiple incision and drainage procedures for recurrent periauricular or upper neck abscesses before the correct diagnosis is considered.

### Anatomy

The external opening varies in position but lies on a line between the tragus and the hyoid bone. The opening is often inconspicuous, appearing as a skin-lined pit. The superior attachment is variable — there may be an opening anterior to the tragus, or the tract may form a true fistula running inferior to the floor of the EAC and opening at the osseocartilaginous junction of the external auditory meatus. Rarely, a frenulum-like band forms between the floor of the meatus and the umbo of the tympanic membrane.

### Work and Work Classification

The original classification by Work and Work (1972) divides first branchial cleft anomalies into two types based on histological composition:

**Type I:**
- Ectodermal origin only
- Present medial to the concha
- Usually superficial and simple in nature
- Essentially a duplication anomaly of the ear canal
- Lined with squamous epithelium

**Type II:**
- Both ectodermal AND mesodermal in origin
- Contains cartilage and hair follicles in the wall
- The more inferior opening is usually below the angle of the mandible
- The tract is intimately related to the facial nerve trunk (passes through the parotid gland)
- More common than Type I

### Clinical Features

- **External opening:** Present at birth; may become more obvious by discharge of epithelial or sebaceous debris (lined with skin, does NOT discharge mucus)
- **Recurrent infection:** Acute inflammation progressing to abscess formation — the most common presentation leading to diagnosis
- **Periauricular swelling:** May be mistaken for parotid abscess or infected preauricular cyst
- **Key distinguishing feature:** A sinus found below the level of the tragus is more likely a first branchial cleft anomaly (vs preauricular sinus found at the level of the tragus); checking the EAC for fistula is imperative

### Investigation

**MRI:** Preferred over CT in children — minimizes ionizing radiation and provides excellent visualization of soft-tissue planes; will usually demonstrate the tract but will not clearly show its relationship to the facial nerve.

**CT (helical):** May be preferred in some institutions for cost, availability and speed of image acquisition (avoiding sedation/anaesthesia).

**Radiopaque sinogram:** Not routinely indicated.

### Surgical Management

Surgery should not be performed during acute infection. A period of quiescence should be awaited, followed by elective excision.

**Surgical approach:**
1. A [[Parotid Gland and Salivary Tumours|parotidectomy]]-type incision is modified to include the lower opening of the sinus
2. The sinus tract is followed for a short distance upward to confirm its nature and direction
3. The facial nerve trunk must be identified as it leaves the skull base and enters the parotid gland
4. The mandibular branch is particularly followed so it can be preserved while freeing the tract
5. Sharp dissection is often required to free the nerve from the tract (especially after recurrent infections or previous incision/drainage)
6. Intraoperative facial nerve monitoring is recommended
7. The upper end of the lesion must be followed to ensure no squamous epithelium remains — this may require opening the EAC and use of the otological microscope and ear instruments
8. Wound closure with suction drainage; light packing of the ear canal if necessary

**Critical surgical considerations:**
- Facial nerve exposure and protection is required in nearly every case
- The superficial position of the facial nerve in small children must be considered
- The tract typically runs deep to the facial nerve branches, which may be adherent if there has been recurrent infection
- The relationship of the tract to the EAC cartilages is unpredictable — it may terminate at the canal or form a duplicated canal parallel to the EAC

## PART C: OTHER BRANCHIAL ANOMALIES — OVERVIEW

### Second Branchial Cleft Anomalies (90% of All Branchial Lesions)

**Sinuses:** External opening on the lower neck, anterior to SCM. Tract directed proximally and medially between the internal and external carotid arteries. Proximal end may communicate with the pharynx through the palatine tonsil to form a true fistula.

**Cysts (Bailey's classification):**
- Type I: Deep to platysma, anterior to SCM
- Type II: Abutting ICA, adherent to IJV (most common)
- Type III: Extending between ICA and ECA
- Type IV: Abutting pharyngeal wall, potentially extending to skull base

**Surgery:** Fusiform skin incision around the external opening; tract followed superiorly with stepladder incision if necessary; ligated at the level of the palatine tonsil. Care taken to avoid hypoglossal nerve in the superior dissection.

**Association:** Branchio-oto-renal syndrome (autosomal dominant, EYA1 gene on 8q13) — bilateral branchial sinuses, preauricular pits, hearing loss and renal abnormalities.

### Third and Fourth Pharyngeal Pouch Sinuses

- Rare (1-2% of branchial lesions)
- Almost always on the left side
- Present in early childhood with recurrent thyroid abscess or neonatal airway obstruction
- Both originate at the pyriform sinus
- Third arch: related to glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX)
- Fourth arch: related to superior laryngeal branch of vagus (CN X)
- Modern management: endoscopic cautery of the pyriform fossa sinus as first-line treatment (described by Bailey, 2004)
- Higher recurrence rate in neonates — open resection recommended in this younger group

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Branchial clefts (ectodermal) produce sinuses opening onto skin; pharyngeal pouches (endodermal) produce sinuses opening onto mucosa; a fistula connects both surfaces when cleft and pouch persist
> 2. First branchial cleft anomalies account for <10% of branchial lesions; frequently misdiagnosed as recurrent parotid/periauricular abscesses
> 3. Work and Work classification: Type I = ectodermal only, superficial, medial to concha (EAC duplication); Type II = ectodermal AND mesodermal, contains cartilage, intimately related to facial nerve trunk
> 4. Facial nerve identification and protection is required in nearly every case of first branchial cleft surgery — intraoperative nerve monitoring is recommended
> 5. The superficial position of the facial nerve in small children must be considered during dissection of first branchial cleft anomalies
> 6. Second branchial cleft anomalies are the most common (90%); tract passes between ICA and ECA; Bailey's classification Types I-IV describes cyst location
> 7. Third/fourth pharyngeal pouch sinuses almost always occur on the left side; present as recurrent thyroid abscess; endoscopic cautery of the pyriform fossa opening is now first-line treatment
> 8. Branchio-oto-renal syndrome (EYA1 gene, 8q13): bilateral preauricular pits, bilateral branchial sinuses, hearing loss and renal abnormalities — autosomal dominant with variable penetrance
> 9. A sinus below the level of the tragus is more likely a first branchial cleft anomaly than a preauricular sinus — always check the EAC for fistula
> 10. All branchial anomaly surgery should be performed electively during a quiescent phase — surgery during acute infection increases the risk of nerve injury and incomplete excision


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The classic presentation of a first branchial cleft anomaly is a child with multiple episodes of periauricular or upper neck abscess that have been repeatedly incised and drained — an experienced otolaryngologist should consider this diagnosis
> - Type II first branchial cleft anomalies pass through the parotid gland and are intimately related to the facial nerve trunk — previous infections and surgical drainage make subsequent definitive excision more hazardous
> - The first branchial cleft anomaly tract does not extend medial to the cartilaginous canal, but a strand may cross the lumen from the osseocartilaginous junction to the umbo
> - Unlike branchial cleft sinuses (which discharge epithelial/sebaceous debris), pharyngeal pouch sinuses discharge mucus from the internal mucosal opening
> - Renal ultrasound should be performed in all children with bilateral preauricular sinuses or bilateral branchial anomalies to exclude branchio-oto-renal syndrome
> - Garabedian found higher recurrence rates of endoscopic cautery for pyriform fossa sinuses in neonates — open resection is recommended in this younger age group
