## INTRODUCTION

Vascular anomalies of the head and neck in children are broadly classified into vascular tumours (of which infantile haemangioma is the most common) and vascular malformations (including lymphatic, venous, capillary and arteriovenous malformations). This distinction, established by the Mulliken and Glowacki classification and refined by the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA), is fundamental to appropriate management. Haemangiomas are true neoplasms with endothelial proliferation that undergo involution, while vascular malformations are errors of morphogenesis that grow proportionally with the child and do not involute. Lymphatic malformations (formerly termed cystic hygromas or lymphangiomas) represent a distinct subset of low-flow vascular malformations that predominantly affect the head and neck.

## PART A: ISSVA CLASSIFICATION OF VASCULAR ANOMALIES

The ISSVA classification (1996, updated) provides the framework for accurate diagnosis and management:

| Category | Types |
|----------|-------|
| **Vascular Tumours** | Infantile haemangioma, Congenital haemangioma (RICH, NICH), Tufted angioma, Kaposiform haemangioendothelioma, Pyogenic granuloma |
| **Slow-Flow Malformations** | Capillary (CM), Venous (VM), Lymphatic (LM) |
| **Fast-Flow Malformations** | Arterial (AM), Arteriovenous fistula (AVF), Arteriovenous malformation (AVM) |
| **Complex Combined** | CVM, CLM, LVM, CLVM, AVM-LM, CM-AVM |

### Key Distinguishing Features

| Feature | Haemangioma | Vascular Malformation |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| Onset | Usually postnatal (2-3 months) | Present at birth |
| Growth | Proliferates then involutes | Grows with child, no involution |
| Gender | Female:Male = 3-5:1 | No gender preponderance |
| Histology | Endothelial hyperplasia | Normal endothelial activity |
| GLUT-1 | Positive (97%) | Negative |
| VEGF/FGF | Elevated | Not elevated |

## PART B: CYSTIC NECK MASSES IN CHILDREN

### Enumeration

Cystic neck masses in children include:

**Congenital:**
- Thyroglossal duct cyst (midline, most common)
- Branchial cleft cyst (second branchial most common; lateral)
- Lymphatic malformation (cystic hygroma)
- Dermoid cyst
- Thymic cyst
- Ranula (plunging type extends into neck)
- Laryngocoele (rarely palpable externally)

**Acquired:**
- Suppurative lymphadenitis with cystic degeneration
- Cystic metastatic lymph node (rare in children)
- Abscess (from branchial remnant, dental, salivary)

### Investigation Approach

Ultrasound is the first-line investigation for all paediatric cystic neck masses. MRI provides superior soft-tissue delineation for deeper or complex lesions and avoids ionizing radiation. CT with contrast may be used for rapid assessment or bony detail. The anatomical location, relationship to midline, and flow characteristics on Doppler guide the differential diagnosis.

## PART C: INFANTILE HAEMANGIOMA — CLINICAL FEATURES AND MANAGEMENT

### Epidemiology and Natural History

Infantile haemangiomas are the commonest tumours in white infants, present in up to 10-12%. Two-thirds are found in the head and neck. Extremely premature infants (birth weight <1000 g) are at particular risk (approximately 30% affected). They are less common in non-Caucasians.

**Natural history follows a predictable pattern:**
- **Birth to 2 months:** Pink macules appear; proliferation begins
- **2-8 months:** Rapid growth — tense, minimally compressible mass; plateau in size by 4-8 months
- **1 year onward:** Involution begins
- **5 years:** 50% completely involuted
- **7 years:** 70% involuted
- **12 years:** Further involution in remainder
- **Permanent skin changes** (loose skin, altered colouration, telangiectasia, scarring) occur in over 50% even after complete involution

### Subtypes

**Infantile haemangiomas** are subdivided by depth as superficial, deep or visceral. Congenital haemangiomas (present fully formed at birth) are categorized as rapidly involuting (RICH), non-involuting (NICH) or non-progressive.

### Associated Syndromes

**PHACES syndrome** — associated with large cervicofacial haemangiomas in approximately 10% of cases:
- **P**osterior cranial fossa malformation (Dandy-Walker)
- **H**aemangiomas
- **A**rterial anomalies (absent carotid/vertebral)
- **C**ardiac anomalies
- **E**ye anomalies
- **S**ternal anomalies (bifid/cleft)

90% of PHACES patients are girls, with particular risk of thromboembolic stroke.

### Complications

- **Periorbital haemangiomas:** Risk of permanent amblyopia within 1 week of visual axis obstruction — urgent ophthalmology referral mandatory
- **Subglottic haemangiomas:** Progressive stridor from 2-3 months; 50% have no cutaneous manifestation; beard distribution facial haemangioma increases risk
- **Ulceration:** Affects <5% of cases; most common perioral and perineal
- **Ear canal obstruction:** Parotid haemangiomas may cause conductive hearing loss
- **Congestive cardiac failure:** Rare; from AV fistulation in large visceral haemangiomas

### Management

**Active monitoring:** For the majority of smaller haemangiomas — reassurance regarding natural involution is sufficient.

**Propranolol (first-line medical treatment):**
Propranolol has revolutionized haemangioma management. Its efficacy was first described by Leauté-Labrèze (Bordeaux, France).

Mechanisms of action:
- Vasoconstriction (immediate — softening and pallor within 24 hours)
- Decreased expression of FGF and VEGF
- Promotion of apoptosis via cAMP and tyrosine kinase pathways

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Target dose | 1-3 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses |
| Starting dose | 1 mg/kg/day |
| Dose escalation | Increase to 2 mg/kg/day after 1st week |
| Monitoring | Pulse and BP every 30 min for 2 hours at initiation/dose increase |
| Response rate | 97% of infantile haemangiomas |
| Weaning | Staged over approximately 4 weeks |

Pre-treatment workup: FBC, blood glucose, renal/hepatic/thyroid function, ECG, echocardiography. Contraindicated in asthmatics and with selective beta-2 agonists.

Side effects: minor (weakness, fatigue, altered sleep) or major (bronchospasm, hypotension, bradycardia, hypoglycaemia — more common in neonates).

**GLUT-1 staining:** If minimal response to propranolol, consider alternative diagnosis — biopsy for GLUT-1 (positive in 97% of infantile haemangiomas, negative in vascular malformations).

**Topical timolol:** For smaller, superficial lesions.

**Corticosteroids:** Previously first-line, now second-line. Prednisolone 2-3 mg/kg/day achieves resolution in up to 90%, but side effects (Cushingoid facies, growth retardation, gastritis) limit use. May be combined with propranolol for large/complicated lesions.

**Surgical excision:** Reserved for poor propranolol responders, residual lesions after involution, or subglottic haemangiomas requiring definitive treatment. Laryngofissure excision for subglottic lesions is second-line after propranolol.

**Laser therapy:** Pulsed dye, CO2, Nd:YAG — for cutaneous lesions and periorbital disease. Caution against laser for subglottic haemangiomas due to risk of circumferential scarring and stenosis.

### Kasabach-Merritt Syndrome

Associated with kaposiform haemangioendotheliomas and tufted angiomas (NOT simple haemangiomas). Consumptive thrombocytopaenia from platelet trapping leads to secondary coagulopathy and DIC. Mortality risk 30-50%. Heparins must NOT be used (increase FGF release and tumour proliferation).

## PART D: LYMPHATIC MALFORMATIONS (CYSTIC HYGROMA)

### Pathogenesis

Lymphatic malformations (LMs) are low-flow vascular malformations resulting from abnormal embryological development of regions of the lymphatic system. The overall result is a collection of varying-sized lymphatic spaces and channels instead of the normal regular network of fine channels with one-way valves. The precise mechanisms are uncertain.

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence: approximately 1:5000
- Accounts for 6% of all paediatric soft-tissue masses
- Head and neck involvement in 75% of cases, from where they may extend into the thorax
- 50% diagnosed at birth; 90% by 2 years of age

### Classification

**By cyst size:**
- **Macrocystic:** Few large cysts (volume >2 cm³) — better response to sclerotherapy
- **Microcystic:** Smaller cysts — poorer response to sclerotherapy
- **Mixed:** Most lesions contain a mixture of macro- and microcystic components

**By location (Type classification):**

| Type | Location | Cyst Size | Prognosis |
|------|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Type 1 | Below mylohyoid — anterior and posterior triangles | Predominantly macrocystic | Better |
| Type 2 | Above mylohyoid — oral cavity, cheek, parotid | Predominantly microcystic | Worse |

**De Serres staging:**

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Unilateral infrahyoid |
| II | Unilateral suprahyoid |
| III | Unilateral supra- and infrahyoid |
| IV | Bilateral suprahyoid |

Higher stages correspond to smaller cyst size, greater functional deficits and greater treatment complexity.

### Clinical Features

- Soft, fluctuant, transilluminant mass (macrocystic component)
- Grows proportionally with the child; does NOT involute
- Becomes firm and lumpy after respiratory infections (intercurrent infections may also lead to partial sclerosis)
- Macroglossia from sublingual and tongue base involvement
- Progressive airway obstruction from pharyngeal/laryngeal extension
- Mandibular distortion from chronic mass effect

### Extensive Cervicothoracic Disease

Cystic hygroma involving the neck, chest and mediastinum represents the most challenging clinical scenario. These large LMs may cause:
- Neonatal airway obstruction — may require EXIT procedure or immediate [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]]
- Thoracic extension compromising respiratory function
- Mediastinal involvement affecting great vessels
- Combination of macro- and microcystic disease requiring multimodality treatment

### Investigation

- **Ultrasound:** First-line; characterizes cyst size and extent
- **MRI (T2-weighted):** Preferred for deeper lesions; differentiates macro- from microcystic disease; demonstrates relationships to surrounding structures; guides treatment planning
- **CT:** For rapid assessment; useful for airway evaluation
- **Fetal MRI/ultrasound:** Prenatal diagnosis allows planning of EXIT procedure or immediate airway management

### Management

Management requires a multidisciplinary approach (ENT, dermatology, paediatric surgery, interventional radiology).

**Observation:** Some smaller, asymptomatic LMs may require no treatment initially.

**Sclerotherapy:**
- Performed by interventional radiologists under general anaesthesia with fluoroscopic control
- Agent of choice: 3% sodium tetradecyl sulphate (STS) injected as foam
- Injected directly into larger cysts after aspiration of fluid, causing inflammatory response with subsequent fibrosis and sclerosis
- Most effective for macrocystic disease
- NOT effective for microcystic disease (poor penetration)
- Multiple treatments usually required at intervals of weeks to months
- **OK-432 (Picibanil):** Previously commonly used but discontinued (loss of supply and withdrawal of USFDA approval)
- Side effects: post-treatment swelling (risk of airway obstruction), soft tissue and nerve injury, secondary infection

**Surgical excision:**
- Only means of complete elimination of disease and cure
- Most straightforward for localized macrocystic disease (Type 1)
- Extremely challenging for extensive mixed disease
- Neonatal debulking may clear anterior cervical portion in first days of life, potentially avoiding tracheostomy
- Facial/parotid component addressed when child is older (reduced risk to facial nerve)
- Transoral approaches for tongue/sublingual disease (preserving submandibular ducts and lingual nerves)
- Coblation (radiofrequency ablation) now preferred for tongue base and mucosal lesions — avoids lateral thermal injury

**Multimodality approach:** For extensive mixed disease — surgery for microcystic areas combined with sclerotherapy for macrocystic components, delivered in multiple stages.

**Emergency interventions:** Tracheostomy and debulking surgery may be required urgently for neonatal airway obstruction.

## PART E: ROLE OF CBNAAT IN EXTRAPULMONARY TB OF HEAD AND NECK

### Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis in H&N

Tuberculosis can affect cervical lymph nodes (scrofula), salivary glands, thyroid and other head and neck structures. Cervical tuberculous lymphadenitis is the most common form of extrapulmonary TB in the head and neck.

### CBNAAT (Cartridge-Based Nucleic Acid Amplification Test)

CBNAAT (GeneXpert MTB/RIF) is a rapid molecular diagnostic test that simultaneously detects Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and rifampicin resistance:

- **Principle:** Automated real-time PCR-based platform using specific primers for M. tuberculosis rpoB gene
- **Turnaround time:** Results within 2 hours (vs weeks for culture)
- **Sensitivity:** High sensitivity for smear-positive specimens; moderate sensitivity for smear-negative specimens
- **Specificity:** Very high (>98%)
- **Rifampicin resistance:** Detected simultaneously, identifying potential MDR-TB

### Role in Extrapulmonary TB of H&N

- Performed on FNAC aspirates, tissue biopsies or pus from cervical lymph nodes
- Superior to conventional AFB smear microscopy (higher sensitivity)
- Faster than mycobacterial culture (2 hours vs 6-8 weeks)
- Particularly valuable in paucibacillary extrapulmonary disease where smear microscopy often yields false negatives
- Recommended by WHO as initial diagnostic test for extrapulmonary TB specimens
- Enables early initiation of appropriate anti-tubercular therapy and identification of drug resistance

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. ISSVA classification divides vascular anomalies into vascular tumours (proliferative, involute) and vascular malformations (morphogenesis errors, grow with child, no involution) — this distinction is fundamental to management
> 2. Infantile haemangiomas affect 10-12% of white infants; two-thirds are in H&N; proliferate from birth to 4-8 months then involute — 50% by age 5, 70% by age 7
> 3. Propranolol has revolutionized haemangioma management — target dose 1-3 mg/kg/day; 97% response rate in infantile type; contraindicated in asthmatics; side effects include hypoglycaemia and bradycardia
> 4. GLUT-1 is positive in 97% of infantile haemangiomas and negative in vascular malformations — useful diagnostic marker when response to propranolol is poor
> 5. PHACES syndrome: posterior fossa malformation, haemangiomas, arterial anomalies, cardiac defects, eye anomalies, sternal anomalies — 90% girls, risk of thromboembolic stroke
> 6. Kasabach-Merritt syndrome (consumptive thrombocytopaenia) is associated with kaposiform haemangioendotheliomas, NOT simple haemangiomas — mortality 30-50%; heparins are contraindicated
> 7. Lymphatic malformations are classified as macrocystic (>2 cm³, better prognosis, responsive to sclerotherapy) or microcystic (poorer prognosis, poor sclerotherapy response) — most are mixed
> 8. Type 1 LM (below mylohyoid, macrocystic) has better prognosis than Type 2 (above mylohyoid, microcystic involving oral cavity/parotid)
> 9. 3% sodium tetradecyl sulphate (STS) foam is the current sclerotherapy agent of choice for macrocystic LM — OK-432 has been discontinued
> 10. CBNAAT (GeneXpert) detects M. tuberculosis DNA and rifampicin resistance within 2 hours on FNAC/tissue from cervical lymph nodes — WHO-recommended initial test for extrapulmonary TB

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Subglottic haemangioma should be suspected in any infant with progressive stridor from 2-3 months of age — 50% have no cutaneous manifestation; beard distribution facial haemangioma is a red flag
> - Permanent skin changes (loose skin, telangiectasia, scarring) persist in >50% of haemangiomas even after complete involution — parents should be counselled about this realistic outcome
> - Large cervicothoracic lymphatic malformations may require prenatal planning (EXIT procedure) — fetal MRI is essential for airway assessment and delivery planning
> - Intercurrent respiratory infections can cause sudden enlargement of lymphatic malformations — however, these infections may also promote partial sclerosis and size reduction over time
> - Surgery for LM of the parotid region should ideally be deferred until the child is older to reduce risk of facial nerve injury — neonatal debulking focuses on the anterior cervical component
> - Pyogenic granulomas (acquired vascular tumours) resemble haemangiomas clinically but arise suddenly in older children without a proliferative-involution lifecycle — treatment is excision, laser or cautery
