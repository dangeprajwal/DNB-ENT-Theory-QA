## INTRODUCTION

Thyroglossal duct cysts are the most common congenital abnormality in the head and neck region. They arise from the embryological remnant of the thyroglossal duct — the track of descent of the thyroid gland from the foramen caecum to its final cervical position. An understanding of the embryology of thyroid descent is essential for correct surgical management, as incomplete excision leads to unacceptable recurrence rates. Midline neck masses in children and adults have a specific differential diagnosis that varies by age group, with thyroglossal duct cyst, dermoid cyst and submental lymph node being the most common benign causes.

---

## PART A: EMBRYOLOGY AND DEVELOPMENT

### Thyroid Descent

The thyroid gland develops from the foramen caecum, which lies in the midline of the tongue at the junction of the anterior two-thirds and posterior one-third. In normal development, the thyroid descends through the tongue, passing caudally with an intimate relationship to the hyoid bone, and finishes in the anterior neck overlying the trachea and laryngeal cartilage.

**Key embryological points:**
- In approximately one-third of cases, the duct has been found to pass posterior to the hyoid bone — this has important implications in surgical treatment
- The thyroglossal duct normally involutes during weeks 8-10 of gestation
- Failure of involution results in persistence of the duct, from which a cyst may arise at any point along the tract
- The cyst may contain thyroid tissue

### Thyroglossal Duct Tract

The thyroglossal tract extends from the foramen caecum of the tongue, passes through or around the hyoid bone (posterior in one-third), continues through the infrahyoid strap muscle region, and terminates at the thyroid isthmus. An abnormal cyst may arise anywhere along this tract. Understanding this course is critical for the Sistrunk procedure.

## PART B: CLINICAL FEATURES AND PRESENTATION

### Typical Presentation

Despite being a congenital lesion, thyroglossal duct cysts often present later in childhood or early adulthood. The clinical presentation includes:

- **Midline neck swelling** — the most common complaint; although it can occur laterally (usually left-sided) in approximately 10% of cases
- **Movement with swallowing and tongue protrusion** — elevates on both manoeuvres due to attachment to the hyoid bone and tongue base via the thyroglossal tract (this is the pathognomonic clinical sign)
- **Infection** — may present with acute inflammation, tenderness and erythema
- **Fistula formation** — may develop if the cyst is surgically drained or discharges spontaneously through the skin

### Clinical Scenario: 8-Year-Old with Midline Neck Swelling

An 8-year-old child with a midline neck swelling measuring 3 x 1 cm:
- **Presumptive diagnosis:** Thyroglossal duct cyst
- **Supportive features:** Midline location, smooth cystic swelling, movement with swallowing and tongue protrusion
- **Differential diagnosis:** Dermoid cyst, submental lymph node, ectopic thyroid tissue
- **Management:** Ultrasound to confirm cyst and verify normal thyroid in cervical position, followed by Sistrunk procedure

---

## PART C: INVESTIGATION

### Essential Investigations

**Ultrasound** is the primary investigation and serves two critical purposes:
1. Confirms the diagnosis — demonstrates a midline cystic lesion
2. Confirms the presence of a normal thyroid gland in the usual cervical position

This second point is vital because it is possible for the thyroglossal cyst to contain the only functioning thyroid tissue, and therefore removal would result in hypothyroidism.

**Cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI):** Advised before revision surgery to ascertain an intact hyoid bone or localize recurrent cyst. MRI is preferred in children to avoid ionizing radiation.

**Technetium-99m thyroid scan:** Indicated if there is concern about ectopic thyroid tissue, particularly if no thyroid tissue is identified in the cervical position on ultrasound. Will identify metabolically active thyroid tissue in the tongue base or elsewhere.

**Thyroid function tests:** Should be performed pre-operatively.

## PART D: SURGICAL MANAGEMENT — SISTRUNK PROCEDURE

### Standard Sistrunk Procedure

The definitive operation was described by Sistrunk from the Mayo Clinic in 1920. It involves en bloc removal of:

1. The thyroglossal duct cyst
2. The thyroglossal duct tract
3. A central portion of the hyoid bone
4. A portion of the raphe joining the mylohyoid muscles
5. A portion of each genioglossus muscle
6. Tissue up to the foramen caecum

**Recurrence rates:**
- With complete Sistrunk procedure: **<5%** (confirmed in a 2013 systematic review of 750 patients having primary surgery)
- With incomplete excision (deviation from Sistrunk's procedure): **27%**

### Extended/Modified Sistrunk Procedure

The operation has been developed further to include a wider block dissection incorporating the infrahyoid region to the thyroid isthmus. This was initially used for recurrent disease but is now applied to all patients by some surgeons to minimize complications.

### Surgical Technique

1. Transverse skin incision over the cyst
2. Mobilize the cyst with surrounding soft tissue
3. Identify the tract passing superiorly towards the hyoid bone
4. Excise the central portion of the hyoid bone (approximately 1 cm)
5. Follow the tract superiorly through the mylohyoid and genioglossus muscles
6. Continue dissection to the foramen caecum
7. If a skin fistula is present, excise it with the underlying cyst and tract

### Complications of Surgery

- Haematoma
- Seroma
- Cyst recurrence (due to incomplete excision)
- Salivary fistula
- Hypoglossal nerve injury

### Management of Recurrence

Recurrence is most often due to incomplete primary excision. Management includes:
- Cross-sectional imaging to localize recurrent cyst and assess hyoid bone status
- Extended Sistrunk procedure with wider block dissection
- Ensuring complete excision up to the foramen caecum

## PART E: DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS OF MIDLINE NECK MASSES

### By Age Group

| Age Group | Common Causes | Less Common |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| Children | Thyroglossal cyst, dermoid cyst, reactive lymph node | Ectopic thyroid, lipoma |
| Adolescents | Thyroglossal cyst, dermoid cyst, lymph node | Thyroid nodule |
| Adults | Thyroid nodule/goitre, thyroglossal cyst, dermoid | Lymphoma, metastatic node |

### Detailed Differential Diagnosis

**Thyroglossal duct cyst:** Midline, cystic, moves with swallowing AND tongue protrusion. Most common congenital midline neck mass.

**Dermoid cyst:** Midline, does NOT move with swallowing or tongue protrusion. Contains ectodermal and mesodermal elements. Occurs along lines of embryonic closure. Firmer consistency than thyroglossal cyst.

**Ectopic/lingual thyroid:** Rare (1:100 000). Represents failure of descent of the thyroid anlage. In 70-80% of cases it is the only thyroid tissue present. May present with dysphagia, airway obstruction or hypothyroidism. Technetium scan confirms diagnosis.

**Submental lymph node:** Firm, non-cystic. Reactive or pathological (lymphoma, metastatic). Does not move with tongue protrusion.

**Thyroid swelling:** Goitre, solitary thyroid nodule. Moves with swallowing but NOT tongue protrusion.

**Plunging ranula:** Arises from sublingual gland, extends through mylohyoid to present as submental/submandibular swelling.

**Congenital midline cervical cleft/cord:** Rare. Arises from failed midline fusion of second and third branchial arches. May extend from mentum to sternum.

### Investigation Protocol for a Vascular Neck Mass

Investigation of a suspected vascular neck mass requires a systematic approach:

1. **Ultrasound with colour Doppler:** First-line investigation — characterizes the mass as cystic or solid, determines flow characteristics (high-flow vs low-flow), identifies feeding and draining vessels
2. **MRI:** Preferred imaging for deeper lesions; distinguishes haemangiomas (lobulated mass with flow voids, hypo-intense T1, hyper-intense T2) from vascular malformations; differentiates macro- and microcystic components of lymphatic malformations
3. **CT with contrast:** May be used for bony detail and rapid assessment; helical CT may be preferred in some institutions for speed (avoiding sedation)
4. **Angiography:** Reserved for arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) — delineates vascular anatomy and allows access for embolization
5. **Biopsy:** GLUT-1 staining distinguishes infantile haemangioma (97% positive) from vascular malformations (negative)

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Thyroglossal duct cysts are the most common congenital abnormality in the head and neck; they arise from failure of involution of the thyroglossal duct during weeks 8-10 of gestation
> 2. The pathognomonic clinical sign is elevation of the swelling with both swallowing AND tongue protrusion — distinguishing it from thyroid swellings (swallowing only) and dermoid cysts (neither)
> 3. In one-third of cases the thyroglossal duct passes posterior to the hyoid bone — this has critical surgical implications for the Sistrunk procedure
> 4. Ultrasound must confirm a normal thyroid in the cervical position before surgery — the thyroglossal cyst may contain the only functioning thyroid tissue
> 5. The Sistrunk procedure (1920) involves removal of the cyst, central hyoid bone, mylohyoid raphe, genioglossus muscle portions and tissue to the foramen caecum — recurrence <5% with complete excision vs 27% with incomplete excision
> 6. The extended Sistrunk procedure involves wider block dissection to the thyroid isthmus — now applied to all patients by some surgeons
> 7. Thyroglossal cyst can present laterally (usually left-sided) in approximately 10% of cases — do not exclude the diagnosis based on location alone
> 8. Midline neck masses in children: thyroglossal cyst > dermoid cyst > reactive lymph node > ectopic thyroid; in adults: thyroid pathology predominates
> 9. Ectopic/lingual thyroid is the sole thyroid tissue in 70-80% of cases; up to one-third have hypothyroidism; technetium scan confirms diagnosis
> 10. GLUT-1 is a specific marker for infantile haemangiomas (97% positive) and is otherwise found only in placenta and blood-brain barrier endothelium — negative in vascular malformations


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A child with a midline neck swelling that moves with both swallowing and tongue protrusion has a thyroglossal cyst until proven otherwise — but always check for a normal cervical thyroid on ultrasound before surgery
> - The commonest cause of recurrence after thyroglossal cyst surgery is failure to perform a complete Sistrunk procedure — the central hyoid bone and core of tissue to the foramen caecum must be excised
> - Surgery should not be performed during acute infection — allow a period of quiescence followed by elective excision
> - Cross-sectional imaging before revision surgery is essential to ascertain an intact hyoid bone and localize recurrent cyst
> - Congenital midline cervical cleft arises from failed midline fusion of second and third branchial arches — treated by elective excision using stepladder horizontal incisions or Z-plasty to avoid contracture
> - Malignancy within a thyroglossal cyst (usually papillary thyroid carcinoma) is rare but reported — histological examination of the specimen is mandatory
