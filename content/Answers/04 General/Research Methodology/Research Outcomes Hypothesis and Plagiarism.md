## INTRODUCTION

The practice of evidence-based otorhinolaryngology demands not only sound study design and statistical analysis but also a clear understanding of what constitutes meaningful outcomes, how hypotheses drive scientific inquiry, and how integrity in publication is maintained. Outcome-based research shifts the focus from purely clinician-assessed endpoints (e.g., audiogram values, graft take rates) to patient-centred measures that capture the true impact of disease and treatment on patients' lives. Hypothesis testing provides the formal statistical framework through which clinical questions are answered, while an understanding of plagiarism and publication ethics safeguards the credibility of the medical literature upon which clinical decisions are based.

This document addresses three DNB examination questions spanning these interrelated themes and is structured as Parts A through E for systematic revision.

## PART A: OUTCOME-BASED RESEARCH (Q19)

### Definition

Outcome-based research (also called outcomes research) is the systematic evaluation of the end results of healthcare practices and interventions. It measures the effect of healthcare on the health status of patients and populations, focusing on patient-centred outcomes rather than purely clinical or physiological measures alone.

The fundamental question outcomes research seeks to answer is: **"Does this intervention make a meaningful difference in the patient's life?"**

Unlike traditional clinical research that may focus narrowly on a single physiological parameter (e.g., pure tone audiogram after [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]]), outcomes research takes a broader view encompassing functional status, quality of life, patient satisfaction, and economic impact.

### Why Outcomes Research Matters in ENT

Outcomes research has become indispensable in modern otorhinolaryngology for several reasons. First, it demonstrates the value of interventions to patients, clinicians, policymakers, and funding bodies by providing evidence that treatments produce meaningful real-world benefits. Second, it enables comparison of different treatment strategies using patient-relevant endpoints, which facilitates shared decision-making between clinician and patient. Increasingly, healthcare systems mandate outcome data for resource allocation and commissioning decisions, making this type of research essential for securing funding and justifying clinical services. Outcome-based evidence also forms the backbone of clinical practice guidelines and national audits, ensuring that recommendations are grounded in patient-centred results rather than theoretical efficacy alone.

### Types of Outcomes

Outcome measures in healthcare research can be organized into several broad categories, each capturing a different dimension of the effect of an intervention.

**Clinical outcomes** are objective, clinician-assessed endpoints that have traditionally formed the cornerstone of surgical and medical research. These include mortality, morbidity, complication rates, disease recurrence, graft take rate, and audiometric gain. While they are essential for measuring biological effectiveness, they may not fully reflect how the patient experiences the result of treatment.

**Patient-reported outcomes** are subjective measures reported directly by the patient, without clinician interpretation. They capture quality of life, functional status, symptom burden, and patient satisfaction. These measures have gained prominence because a procedure may be technically successful (e.g., intact tympanic membrane graft) yet fail to produce a meaningful improvement in the patient's perceived hearing or comfort.

**Economic outcomes** address resource utilisation and cost considerations. Common analytic frameworks include cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-utility analysis (CUA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), and healthcare utilisation rates. These are critical for informing health policy and resource allocation.

**Process outcomes** measure how care is delivered rather than what effect it has. Examples include waiting times, adherence to clinical guidelines, appropriate prescribing, and surgical throughput. They serve as surrogate markers for the quality and efficiency of healthcare delivery.

**Composite outcomes** combine elements from multiple categories to produce a single summary metric. The most widely used composite measures are the disability-adjusted life year (DALY), which quantifies disease burden, and the quality-adjusted life year (QALY), which integrates both the length and the quality of life gained from an intervention.

### Hierarchy of Evidence for Outcomes Research

The strength of evidence for outcome claims varies according to study design. Understanding this hierarchy is critical for appraising the medical literature. At the highest level (Level I) sit systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials, which synthesise evidence from multiple studies and carry the greatest weight in clinical decision-making. Level II comprises well-designed randomised controlled trials (RCTs) themselves, which minimise bias through randomisation and blinding. Level III includes cohort studies (both prospective and retrospective), which follow groups over time but lack randomisation. Level IV encompasses case-control studies, which compare patients with a condition to those without. Level V contains case series and case reports, which describe individual or small-group clinical experiences. Finally, Level VI encompasses expert opinion and narrative reviews, which, while useful for hypothesis generation, represent the weakest form of evidence.

### Outcome Domains Relevant to Otorhinolaryngology

Each subspecialty within otorhinolaryngology has its own relevant outcome domains shaped by the conditions treated and the endpoints most meaningful to patients.

In **otology**, key outcome domains include hearing thresholds (measured by PTA and SRT), speech discrimination scores, [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]] severity, dizziness and balance function, graft take rate, and quality of life assessments specific to ear disease.

In **rhinology**, the principal outcome domains are nasal obstruction scores (measured by instruments such as NOSE), olfactory function, sinonasal symptom burden (SNOT-22), and disease-specific quality of life.

In **head and neck surgery**, outcomes of interest encompass swallowing function, voice quality (measured by VHI), survival rates, cosmetic outcome, and quality of life instruments developed specifically for head and neck cancer patients (UW-QoL, EORTC QLQ-H&N35).

In **paediatric ENT**, the focus is on hearing and speech development, sleep quality as assessed by disease-specific tools ([[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|OSA]]-18), and adenotonsillar symptom burden, reflecting the developmental priorities unique to the paediatric population.

## PART B: PATIENT-REPORTED OUTCOME MEASURES (PROMs) -- Assessment of Patient-Based Measures (Q19)

### Definition

Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (PROMs) are standardized, validated questionnaires completed by patients themselves (without interpretation by a clinician) to measure their perception of health status, functional ability, symptom burden, and quality of life. PROMs capture the patient's own perspective and are now considered essential endpoints in clinical trials and audit. They complement traditional clinical measures (e.g., audiometry) to provide a holistic assessment of treatment effect.

### Types of PROMs

PROMs can be classified into several categories, each suited to different research purposes.

**Generic PROMs** are applicable across all diseases and populations, enabling comparison of health status between different conditions. They provide a broad overview of health but may lack sensitivity to detect changes within a specific disease. Widely used generic instruments include the SF-36 (Short Form 36), EQ-5D (EuroQol 5 Dimensions), and WHO-QOL (WHO Quality of Life).

**Disease-specific PROMs** are designed for a particular condition and are therefore more sensitive to clinically meaningful change within that disease. In ENT practice, important examples include the COMQ-12 (Chronic Otitis Media Questionnaire-12), GBI (Glasgow Benefit Inventory), SNOT-22 (Sino-Nasal Outcome Test), VHI (Voice Handicap Index), and UW-QoL (University of Washington Quality of Life).

**Symptom-specific PROMs** measure a single symptom in depth. Examples relevant to otorhinolaryngology include the THI (Tinnitus Handicap Inventory), DHI (Dizziness Handicap Index), NOSE (Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation), and visual analogue scales (VAS) for pain.

**Domain-specific PROMs** assess a particular dimension of health function. In audiology, the APHAB (Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit) evaluates hearing function across four listening situations, while the SSQ (Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing) provides a detailed assessment of auditory processing.

**Utility measures** generate a single index value anchored between 0 (death) and 1 (perfect health), which can be used in health economic analyses to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). The EQ-5D and HUI-3 (Health Utilities Index Mark 3) are the most commonly used utility instruments.

### Properties of a Good PROM (Psychometric Properties)

A validated PROM must demonstrate a set of essential psychometric properties before it can be relied upon for clinical research or audit. These properties ensure that the instrument accurately and consistently measures what it claims to measure, and that it is practical for use in the intended population.

**Validity** addresses whether the instrument measures what it claims to measure. Face validity is the simplest form: on initial inspection, the questionnaire appears to assess the intended construct. Content validity goes deeper, confirming that the items comprehensively cover all relevant aspects of the construct being measured. Construct validity examines whether scores correlate as expected with other measures of the same construct (convergent validity) and do not correlate with unrelated constructs (discriminant validity). Criterion validity tests whether scores correlate with an established gold-standard measure (concurrent validity) or predict future outcomes (predictive validity).

**Reliability** concerns whether the instrument gives consistent results. Test-retest reliability evaluates whether the same patient produces similar scores at two time points when the condition is stable, and is assessed by the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Inter-rater reliability examines whether different assessors obtain similar results when scoring the same responses. Internal consistency measures the degree to which items within the questionnaire correlate with each other and is quantified by Cronbach's alpha, with a threshold of 0.7 or above considered acceptable.

**Responsiveness** is the ability of the instrument to detect clinically meaningful change over time following an intervention. It is assessed using the effect size, standardised response mean (SRM), or, most usefully, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). The MCID represents the smallest change in score that the patient perceives as beneficial, and it is essential for interpreting whether a statistically significant change also represents a clinically meaningful improvement.

**Feasibility and acceptability** encompass practical considerations. The instrument should be short enough for patients to complete without undue burden, written in accessible language, and culturally appropriate for the target population. It should exhibit low ceiling and floor effects (ensuring it can detect change even at extremes of the scale) and ideally be available in validated translations for use in multinational studies.

### Key PROMs Used in Otology and ENT

| PROM | Full Name | Application | Key Features |
|------|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| **GBI** | Glasgow Benefit Inventory | Post-intervention benefit in any ENT intervention | 18-item; measures change in health status after intervention; total score and 3 subscales (general, social, physical); unique as it specifically measures change rather than current status |
| **COMQ-12** | Chronic Otitis Media Questionnaire-12 | Chronic otitis media (COM) | 12-item; disease-specific; measures symptom severity and impact on quality of life; validated for COM |
| **APHAB** | Abbreviated Profile of Hearing Aid Benefit | Hearing aid outcomes | 24-item; 4 subscales (ease of communication, reverberation, background noise, aversiveness); completed with and without hearing aid |
| **SSQ** | Speech, Spatial and Qualities of Hearing | Hearing function | 49-item; 3 domains (speech perception, spatial hearing, quality of sound); useful for [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] and hearing aid assessment |
| **THI** | Tinnitus Handicap Inventory | Tinnitus severity | 25-item; 3 subscales (functional, emotional, catastrophic); categorizes tinnitus as slight, mild, moderate, severe, or catastrophic |
| **DHI** | Dizziness Handicap Index | Vestibular disorders | 25-item; 3 subscales (physical, emotional, functional); self-assessment of dizziness impact |

### Framework for Selecting a PROM

When selecting a PROM for a research study, a systematic approach is essential. The first step is to define the research question clearly -- what outcome are you trying to measure? From there, one should choose between generic and disease-specific instruments; in practice, both are often used together, with a generic measure enabling comparison across diseases and a disease-specific measure providing sensitivity to the condition under study. The psychometric properties of the candidate PROM must be evaluated by reviewing published evidence of its validity, reliability, and responsiveness. The target population should be considered carefully, including factors such as age, literacy level, language, and cultural appropriateness. Practical considerations include instrument length, mode of administration (paper, electronic, telephone), and licensing costs. Finally, one should check whether a published minimal clinically important difference (MCID) exists for the instrument, as this is critical for interpreting whether observed changes are meaningful to patients.

## PART C: HYPOTHESIS TESTING (Q40)

### Definition of Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a testable statement or prediction about the relationship between two or more variables. In clinical research, it is a proposed explanation for an observation that can be tested through experimentation or systematic observation. The hypothesis translates the research question into a statement that can be statistically evaluated.

A good hypothesis must be **specific** (clearly defines the variables and expected relationship), **testable** (can be investigated empirically), **falsifiable** (can be proven wrong), and **based on existing knowledge** (grounded in prior literature or observations).

### Types of Hypotheses

Hypotheses in clinical research are classified according to the nature of the prediction they make.

The **null hypothesis (H0)** states that there is no significant difference or relationship between the groups or variables being studied. It assumes that any observed difference is due to chance alone. For example: "There is no significant difference in hearing outcomes between endoscopic and microscopic tympanoplasty." The null hypothesis serves as the default assumption and is the hypothesis that is directly tested statistically.

The **alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha)** states that there is a significant difference or relationship between the groups or variables. It contradicts the null hypothesis and represents the claim the researcher typically hopes to support. For example: "There is a significant difference in hearing outcomes between endoscopic and microscopic tympanoplasty." The alternative hypothesis is accepted only when sufficient evidence exists to reject the null hypothesis.

A **directional (one-tailed) hypothesis** predicts the specific direction of the difference or relationship. For example: "Endoscopic tympanoplasty produces better hearing outcomes than microscopic tympanoplasty." A **non-directional (two-tailed) hypothesis** predicts that a difference exists but does not specify its direction -- the difference could go in either direction.

### Null Hypothesis (H0) vs. Alternative Hypothesis (H1) -- Key Differences

| Feature | Null Hypothesis (H0) | Alternative Hypothesis (H1) |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------------|
| **Statement** | No difference / no effect / no association | There is a difference / effect / association |
| **Default assumption** | Assumed to be true until evidence suggests otherwise | Accepted only when H0 is rejected |
| **Role in testing** | The hypothesis that is directly tested statistically | The hypothesis the researcher typically hopes to support |
| **Statistical decision** | Can be rejected or failed to reject (never "accepted") | Accepted when H0 is rejected |
| **Analogy** | Like "innocent until proven guilty" in a court of law | Like the "guilty" verdict |
| **Errors associated** | Type I error (rejecting H0 when true) | Type II error (failing to reject H0 when H1 is true) |

**Important note:** We never say we "accept" the null hypothesis. We either "reject H0" or "fail to reject H0." This is because failure to find a statistically significant difference does not prove that no difference exists -- it may reflect inadequate sample size or study power.

### How We Test a Hypothesis -- Step-by-Step Process

Hypothesis testing follows a structured, sequential approach that moves from question formulation to statistical conclusion.

**Step 1: Formulate H0 and H1.** The investigator states the null hypothesis (no difference or effect) and the alternative hypothesis (there is a difference or effect). At this stage, the decision is also made whether the test will be one-tailed (directional) or two-tailed (non-directional).

**Step 2: Choose the Significance Level (alpha).** Alpha is the probability threshold for rejecting H0. It is conventionally set at 0.05 (5%), meaning the investigator accepts a 5% risk of a false positive result (Type I error). More stringent thresholds of 0.01 or 0.001 may be used when the consequences of a false positive are particularly serious.

**Step 3: Select the Appropriate Statistical Test.** The choice of test depends on the type of data and the study design. The table below summarises the most commonly used parametric tests and their non-parametric equivalents.

| Data Type / Comparison | Parametric Test | Non-parametric Equivalent |
|------------------------|----------------|---------------------------|
| 2 independent groups, continuous data | Independent (unpaired) t-test | Mann-Whitney U test |
| 2 related/paired groups, continuous data | Paired t-test | Wilcoxon signed-rank test |
| >2 independent groups, continuous data | One-way ANOVA | Kruskal-Wallis test |
| >2 related groups, continuous data | Repeated measures ANOVA | Friedman test |
| 2 categorical variables | Chi-square test | Fisher's exact test (small samples) |
| Correlation (continuous) | Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) | Spearman's rank correlation (rho) |
| Survival data | -- | Log-rank test, Cox regression |

Parametric tests assume that the data are normally distributed, continuous (interval or ratio scale), derived from larger samples (usually n > 30), and exhibit homogeneity of variance. They generally offer greater statistical power. Non-parametric tests make fewer assumptions about the underlying distribution and are appropriate for ordinal data, non-normal continuous data, or smaller sample sizes. While they are more robust to violations of distributional assumptions, they have somewhat less statistical power.

**Step 4: Calculate the Test Statistic and p-value.** The test statistic quantifies how far the observed data deviate from what would be expected under the null hypothesis. The p-value is the probability of obtaining the observed result (or a more extreme result) if H0 were true.

**Step 5: Make a Decision.** If p < alpha (e.g., p < 0.05), the result is statistically significant: H0 is rejected and H1 is accepted. If p >= alpha (e.g., p >= 0.05), the result is not statistically significant: there is insufficient evidence to reject H0, and one fails to reject H0.

**Step 6: Interpret the Result.** Statistical significance does not necessarily imply clinical significance. The investigator should always consider the effect size, confidence intervals, and clinical relevance of the finding. A very large study may detect a statistically significant but clinically trivial difference, while an underpowered study may miss a genuine and important effect.

### Errors in Hypothesis Testing

| Error Type | Definition | Consequence | Probability | How to Minimise |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Type I Error (alpha error)** | Rejecting H0 when it is actually TRUE (false positive) | Concluding a treatment works when it does not | Equal to significance level (usually 0.05 or 5%) | Use a more stringent alpha (e.g., 0.01); Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons |
| **Type II Error (beta error)** | Failing to reject H0 when it is actually FALSE (false negative) | Concluding a treatment does not work when it actually does | Denoted by beta (conventionally set at 0.20 or 20%) | Increase sample size; increase effect size; increase alpha (trade-off) |

### Statistical Power

Statistical power is defined as 1 minus beta and represents the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis -- in other words, the probability of detecting a true effect when one exists. A study should ideally have a power of at least 0.80 (80%), meaning there is at least an 80% chance of detecting a true effect. Power depends on four factors: sample size, effect size, significance level (alpha), and variability in the data. A power analysis should be performed before the study begins (a priori) to determine the minimum sample size needed to detect a clinically meaningful effect with adequate confidence.

### Confidence Intervals (CI)

Confidence intervals provide a range of values within which the true population parameter is likely to fall. A 95% confidence interval means that if the study were repeated 100 times, 95 of those intervals would contain the true value. Confidence intervals are particularly informative because they convey both the magnitude and precision of the estimate. If the 95% CI for the difference between groups does not include zero (or does not include 1 for ratios such as odds ratios or relative risks), the result is statistically significant at p < 0.05. Confidence intervals provide more information than p-values alone, as they show not only whether a difference is likely to exist but also how large that difference might plausibly be.

## PART D: TYPES OF PLAGIARISM IN MEDICAL LITERATURE (Q84)

### Definition

Plagiarism is the appropriation of another person's ideas, processes, results, or words without giving appropriate credit. It is a form of research misconduct and is considered a serious ethical violation in academic and medical publishing. The World Association of Medical Editors (WAME) defines plagiarism as the use of others' published and unpublished ideas or words without attribution or permission, and presenting them as new and original rather than derived from an existing source. Understanding the various forms of plagiarism is essential for maintaining the integrity of the medical literature.

### Types of Plagiarism

**Direct or verbatim plagiarism** is the most straightforward and easily recognized form. It involves copying text word-for-word from another source without quotation marks, proper citation, or attribution. An example would be copying a paragraph from a textbook into a manuscript without quoting or citing. Most plagiarism detection software is designed to identify this form reliably.

**Mosaic or patchwork plagiarism** is more subtle and therefore harder to detect. It involves mixing copied phrases from multiple sources with original text, or paraphrasing with the original sentence structure and key terms retained. For instance, a writer may take sentences from different published papers and stitch them together with minor word changes, creating the appearance of original text while the underlying structure and ideas remain borrowed.

**Self-plagiarism**, also known as duplicate or redundant publication, occurs when an author reuses their own previously published work in a new publication without proper disclosure or citation. This includes submitting the same manuscript to multiple journals simultaneously (dual submission). An example would be publishing the same dataset with minor modifications in two different journals. Although the author is the original creator, this practice is considered unethical because each publication is expected to represent a distinct and original contribution to the literature.

**Idea plagiarism** involves using someone else's original idea, theory, concept, or research design without acknowledging the source. Unlike direct plagiarism, the words may be entirely the author's own, but the intellectual contribution -- the core concept -- belongs to another. For example, adopting a novel surgical technique described by another author and presenting it as one's own innovation without citing the original description constitutes idea plagiarism.

**Data plagiarism or fabrication** encompasses using another researcher's data without permission or proper acknowledgement, as well as fabricating or falsifying data. This may involve presenting another research group's unpublished data as one's own, or altering data points to achieve statistically desired results. Data fabrication and falsification are among the most serious forms of research misconduct and can directly harm patients if false data influences clinical practice.

**Source plagiarism** occurs when an author cites references from a secondary source (such as a review article) while implying that they have directly accessed and read the primary source. For example, an author might read a review article that cites a landmark study, then cite that landmark study in their own paper as if they had read the original, without having done so. This is ethically problematic because the original study may have been misinterpreted or selectively cited in the secondary source.

**Ghost writing** refers to publishing work substantially written by an uncredited author, often a professional medical writer funded by a pharmaceutical company or industry sponsor. In this scenario, a clinician is listed as the author without the writer receiving any credit or acknowledgement. This practice obscures the true origin of the work and may conceal conflicts of interest.

**Gift, guest, or honorary authorship** involves listing individuals as authors who did not make a substantive intellectual contribution to the work. A common example is adding a senior professor's name to a paper solely for prestige or departmental expectations, without that person having met the internationally recognized criteria for authorship. This inflates publication records and dilutes the meaning of authorship.

**Salami slicing** is the practice of inappropriately dividing a single study into multiple smaller publications to inflate the number of publications on a researcher's record. For instance, results from one clinical trial might be split into five separate papers based on different outcome measures, each of which could and should have been reported within a single comprehensive publication. This distorts the literature and makes systematic review more difficult.

**Image or figure manipulation** involves altering, reusing, or fabricating figures, images, or graphs from other publications without permission or proper attribution. Examples include enhancing a microscopy image to show a feature that was not clearly visible in the original, or copying another author's graph into a new manuscript. Advances in digital image analysis have made this form of misconduct easier to detect.

**Translation plagiarism** occurs when an author translates work published in another language and presents it as original work in the new language without any attribution to the original source. For example, translating a Spanish-language paper into English and publishing it without reference to the original constitutes translation plagiarism.

### Consequences of Plagiarism

The consequences of plagiarism extend across multiple levels. For the **author**, consequences include retraction of the paper, loss of professional reputation, disciplinary action, termination of employment, and debarment from future research funding. For the **journal**, plagiarism leads to loss of credibility and necessitates published retraction notices. For the **scientific community**, it erodes trust in the medical literature and can potentially harm patients if fabricated data influences clinical decisions. For the **institution**, consequences include reputational damage, loss of research funding, and regulatory scrutiny.

## PART E: MINIMISING PLAGIARISM IN MEDICAL LITERATURE (Q84)

### Strategies for Authors

Minimising plagiarism begins with the individual researcher adopting disciplined writing and referencing practices. **Proper citation and referencing** is the cornerstone: all sources from which ideas, data, or text are derived must be cited, using a consistent referencing style such as Vancouver, which is standard in medical journals. When using exact words from another source, **quotation marks** must be employed with a citation. **Effective paraphrasing** means rewriting ideas in one's own words with a genuinely different sentence structure, not simply substituting synonyms while retaining the original architecture of the passage.

Authors should routinely run manuscripts through **plagiarism detection software** before submission. The industry standard for academic publishing is iThenticate, while Turnitin is widely used in academic institutions. CrossRef Similarity Check provides journal-level screening, and Grammarly offers a basic plagiarism check facility. Maintaining meticulous **source documentation** throughout the literature review process is essential; reference management software such as EndNote, Mendeley, or Zotero greatly facilitates this.

Authors must follow the **ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) guidelines** for authorship and publication ethics. The ICMJE authorship criteria require that all four of the following conditions be met: (1) substantial contribution to conception or design, or to data acquisition or analysis; (2) drafting or critically revising the manuscript for important intellectual content; (3) final approval of the version to be published; and (4) agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work. Additionally, authors should never submit the same manuscript to multiple journals simultaneously (avoid duplicate submission) and must clearly disclose if any part of the work has been previously published or presented.

### Strategies for Institutions

Institutions play a vital role in fostering a culture of research integrity. **Research integrity education** should be mandatory for all researchers and postgraduates, covering ethics, plagiarism awareness, and responsible conduct of research. Institutions should integrate **plagiarism detection tools** into their thesis and manuscript submission processes so that screening occurs before work leaves the institution. Clear **written policies on research misconduct** must be established, defining plagiarism, outlining procedures for investigation, and specifying consequences. **Mentorship programmes** pairing junior researchers with experienced mentors who model ethical practices are highly effective. Institutional Review Board (IRB) and Ethics Committee oversight ensures that all research undergoes ethical review. Finally, institutions should promote a **culture of integrity** by recognizing and rewarding ethical research practices and creating an environment where misconduct is not tolerated.

### Strategies for Journals and Editors

Journals serve as the final gatekeepers of publication integrity. **Mandatory plagiarism screening** of all submissions using tools such as CrossRef/iThenticate Similarity Check should occur before peer review. Journals should adhere to the **COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics) guidelines** for handling cases of suspected plagiarism and misconduct. Clear, transparent **retraction policies** must be in place, with published retraction notices when plagiarized or fabricated work is identified. **Rigorous peer review** serves as a further line of defence to detect plagiarism, data fabrication, and redundant publication. Journals should require **authorship verification**, asking all authors to sign a declaration of their specific contributions and confirm originality. Finally, **post-publication scrutiny** through monitoring published articles and responding to allegations of misconduct via platforms such as PubPeer is increasingly recognized as an essential component of the integrity framework.

### Key Organizations and Guidelines

| Organization/Guideline | Role |
|------------------------|------|
| **ICMJE** (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) | Defines authorship criteria, recommends uniform requirements for manuscripts |
| **COPE** (Committee on Publication Ethics) | Provides guidelines for editors and publishers on handling publication ethics issues |
| **WAME** (World Association of Medical Editors) | Promotes editorial standards and research integrity |
| **ORI** (Office of Research Integrity, USA) | Investigates research misconduct in federally funded research |
| **EQUATOR Network** | Promotes transparent and accurate reporting of health research (CONSORT, STROBE, PRISMA checklists) |
