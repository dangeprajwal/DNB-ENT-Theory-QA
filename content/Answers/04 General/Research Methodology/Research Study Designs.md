> - ## PART A: TYPES OF RESEARCH STUDIES
*1. Case Reports and Case Series**
- Reports of individual patients or groups of patients
- May be retrospective or prospective (cohort studies)
- Highly valid and authentic
- LOW reliability

**2. Case-Control Studies**
- Compares patients with disease (cases) to patients without disease (controls)
- Identifies exposure to risk factors retrospectively
- Useful for studying rare diseases and harm/aetiology
- Less valid for assessing therapy compared to RCTs
- Prone to recall bias and selection bias

**3. Cohort Studies**
- Follows groups of patients over time
- Can be prospective or retrospective
- Good for studying prognosis and natural history
- Less subject to recall bias than case-control studies

**4. Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)**
- Carefully structured prospective study
- Bias and influence carefully controlled
- HIGHLY specific
- Gold standard for therapeutic questions
- Must have: randomization, concealment, blinding, adequate follow-up

**5. Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses**
- Author undertakes extensive literature search and review
- Systematic analysis of quality of evidence
- Beneficial as they save time
- Must check if review is up-to-date
- Most believable evidence for therapy questions

**6. Qualitative/Humanistic Enquiries**
- Explore psycho-social and attitudinal aspects
- Cannot be measured in prospective RCT
- Important for patient-centered outcomes

### Case-Control Study Design

**Definition:** An observational study comparing patients with a specific disease or outcome (cases) to patients without that disease (controls) to identify risk factors or exposures.

**Key Features:**
- Retrospective in nature
- Cases selected based on outcome/disease presence
- Controls matched for confounding variables
- Exposure history obtained for both groups
- Calculates Odds Ratio (OR)

**Advantages:**
- Useful for rare diseases
- Relatively quick and inexpensive
- Good for studying multiple exposures
- Ideal for harm/aetiology questions

**Disadvantages:**
- Prone to selection bias
- Recall bias (cases remember exposures differently)
- Cannot calculate incidence or relative risk
- Cannot prove causation
- Temporal relationship may be unclear

**When to Use:**
- Rare diseases
- Long latency periods
- Multiple potential risk factors
- Initial exploration before cohort studies

## PART B: RANDOMIZED CONTROLLED TRIALS

### Definition

A prospective experimental study where participants are randomly allocated to intervention or control groups to compare outcomes while minimizing bias.

### Essential Components

**1. Randomization**
- Ensures groups are comparable at baseline
- Eliminates selection bias
- Each participant has equal chance of allocation

**2. Concealed Randomization**
- Allocation sequence hidden from investigators
- Prevents manipulation of group assignment
- CRITICAL: Inadequate concealment may exaggerate treatment efficacy by over 40%

**3. Blinding/Masking**
- Single blind: patient unaware of allocation
- Double blind: patient and investigator unaware
- Triple blind: patient, investigator, and analyst unaware
- Lack of blinding may exaggerate effectiveness by 15%

**4. Adequate Follow-up**
- Less than 80% follow-up makes results meaningless
- Must account for dropouts and losses

### Types of RCT Designs

- Parallel group (most common)
- Crossover design
- Factorial design
- Cluster randomized

### Limitations of RCTs

- Expensive and time-consuming
- Ethical constraints
- Not suitable for rare outcomes
- Cannot study harm ethically
- May not reflect real-world practice

## PART C: LEVELS OF EVIDENCE

### Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Classification

| Level | Therapy/Prevention | Diagnosis |
|-------|-------------------|-----------|
| 1a | SR of homogeneous RCTs | SR of Level 1 diagnostic studies |
| 1b | Individual RCT with narrow CI | Validating cohort with good reference standard |
| 1c | All or none | SpPIn and SnNOut |
| 2a | SR of homogeneous cohort studies | SR of Level 2 diagnostic studies |
| 2b | Individual cohort study or low-quality RCT | Exploratory cohort with good reference standard |
| 2c | Outcomes research | Ecological studies |
| 3a | SR of case-control studies | SR of Level 3 diagnostic studies |
| 3b | Individual case-control study | Non-consecutive study |
| 4 | Case series | Case-control study |
| 5 | Expert opinion | Expert opinion |

### Grades of Recommendation

| Grade | Definition |
|-------|------------|
| A | Consistent Level 1 studies |
| B | Consistent Level 2-3 studies or extrapolations from Level 1 |
| C | Level 4 studies or extrapolations from Level 2-3 |
| D | Level 5 evidence or inconsistent studies |

## PART D: SYSTEMATIC REVIEWS AND META-ANALYSIS

### Systematic Review

**Definition:** A comprehensive review using systematic and reproducible methods to identify, evaluate, and synthesize all relevant studies on a specific topic.

**Key Features:**
- Explicit search strategy
- Pre-defined inclusion/exclusion criteria
- Quality assessment of included studies
- May or may not include meta-analysis
- PRISMA guidelines for reporting

**Steps in Conducting:**
1. Define research question (PICO format)
2. Develop search strategy
3. Screen titles and abstracts
4. Obtain and review full texts
5. Extract data independently
6. Assess risk of bias
7. Synthesize findings

### Meta-Analysis

**Definition:** Statistical technique that combines results from multiple valid studies to produce a pooled estimate of effect as if the collected reports were a single study.

**Requirements:**
- Clinical homogeneity (similar patients, interventions, outcomes)
- Methodological homogeneity (similar study designs)
- Statistical homogeneity (similar effect sizes)

**Output:**
- Pooled effect estimate (OR, RR, SMD)
- Confidence intervals
- Forest plot visualization
- Heterogeneity assessment (I-squared)

### Systematic Review vs Meta-Analysis

| Systematic Review | Meta-Analysis |
|-------------------|---------------|
| Qualitative synthesis | Quantitative synthesis |
| Always required | Optional component |
| Describes studies | Combines results statistically |
| Can be done without stats | Requires statistical analysis |
| Assesses quality | Produces pooled estimate |

## PART E: EVIDENCE-BASED MEDICINE IN PRACTICE

### The EBM Process

1. **Ask** - Formulate answerable clinical question (PICO)
2. **Acquire** - Search for best available evidence
3. **Appraise** - Critically evaluate evidence for validity
4. **Apply** - Apply results to patient care
5. **Assess** - Evaluate performance

### PICO Framework

- **P**atient/Population: Who is the patient?
- **I**ntervention: What is being considered?
- **C**omparison: What is the alternative?
- **O**utcome: What are you trying to achieve?

### Sources of Evidence (Search Order)

1. Clinical Evidence
2. Evidence-Based Medicine journal
3. Cochrane Database
4. PubMed/Medline
5. Hand-search specific journals

### Critical Appraisal Questions

**For any study:**
- Is the study valid? (Internal validity)
- What are the results? (Effect size, precision)
- Can results be applied to my patients? (External validity)

**For RCTs specifically:**
- Was randomization concealed?
- Were groups similar at baseline?
- Was blinding adequate?
- Was follow-up complete (greater than 80%)?
- Were outcomes measured objectively?
- Was intention-to-treat analysis used?

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. EBM is defined as conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of current best evidence in patient care decisions
> 2. Study hierarchy: Expert opinion < Case series < Case-control < Cohort < RCT < Systematic review
> 3. Case-control studies are best for rare diseases and studying harm/aetiology
> 4. RCTs are gold standard for therapeutic questions but not suitable for studying harm
> 5. Inadequate concealment of randomization may exaggerate treatment effect by over 40%
> 6. Lack of blinding may exaggerate treatment effect by 15%
> 7. Follow-up less than 80% makes RCT results uninterpretable
> 8. Systematic reviews should use PRISMA guidelines
> 9. Meta-analysis requires clinical, methodological, and statistical homogeneity
> 10. PICO framework: Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome
> 11. Level 1a evidence = systematic review of homogeneous RCTs
> 12. SpPIn: High Specificity + Positive result = Rules IN diagnosis
> 13. SnNOut: High Sensitivity + Negative result = Rules OUT diagnosis


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. For questions about therapy, look for systematic reviews of RCTs first - this is Level 1a evidence
> 2. Case-control studies calculate Odds Ratio, not Relative Risk - they cannot determine incidence
> 3. Publication bias in meta-analysis can overestimate treatment effects - always check funnel plots
> 4. Even highly cited journals publish methodologically flawed studies - always critically appraise
> 5. A test with high sensitivity is good for ruling OUT disease (SnNOut); high specificity is good for ruling IN disease (SpPIn)
> 6. When searching literature, use PICO to structure your question - this dramatically improves search efficiency
