## INTRODUCTION

Research is the systematic investigation undertaken to establish facts, test hypotheses, and reach new conclusions. In medicine, the quality of patient care depends directly on the quality of available research evidence. Understanding research types, how to evaluate a research paper, and the significance of journal indexing and impact factors are essential competencies for the clinician, researcher, and DNB candidate alike. As defined by Professor David Sackett, evidence-based medical practice is the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients.

## PART A-1: FACTORS FOR CHOOSING A RESEARCH TOPIC (Q10)

### Formulating the Research Question

The research question defines what a study aims to achieve. Many experienced researchers agree that formulating a question that can be translated into a feasible and valid study can be surprisingly difficult. What often happens is that instead of a research question, merely a topic is formulated. Framing the research question is the first step for every new project, the importance of which cannot be overestimated. Conversely, many studies fail not because the study design is flawed or execution is poor, but because the question was not formulated adequately in the first place.

### Factors to Consider When Choosing a Research Topic

**1. Clinical Relevance and Significance:**
- The research question should address a current clinical problem or uncertainty
- It should pass the "so what" test — answering the question should provide a significant contribution to existing knowledge
- It should have potential to influence clinical or public health policies
- The topic should address a gap in the published literature

**2. The PICO Framework:**
A good research question in epidemiological research has four components summarized by the acronym PICO:
- **P** — Target Population or Participants (who will be studied)
- **I** — Intervention or determinant (what is being tested or examined)
- **C** — Comparison or Control group (what is the alternative)
- **O** — Outcome(s) of interest (what are you measuring)

*Example:* A topic such as "rising complication rate after [[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]]" is too vague. A properly formulated PICO question would be: "In patients undergoing tonsillectomy for recurrent tonsillitis (P), does the use of bipolar diathermy forceps (I) increase the occurrence of tonsil bleeds requiring return to theatre within 28 days (O), compared to other dissection types (C)?"

**3. Feasibility:**
- Availability of study subjects and adequate sample size
- Availability of resources (funding, equipment, manpower)
- Time constraints and duration required
- Technical expertise available
- Access to data sources and patient populations

**4. Ethical Considerations:**
- The study must be ethically justifiable
- Institutional ethics committee approval must be obtainable
- Informed consent procedures must be practical
- Risk-benefit ratio must favour the participants

**5. Novelty and Originality:**
- A thorough literature review should confirm the topic adds to what is already known
- The study should resolve current uncertainties
- Replication studies should have valid justification

**6. The Determinant-Occurrence Relationship:**
- The researcher must specify what outcomes to study and what determinants to consider
- The target population (the kind of people for whom the study should provide answers) must be clearly defined
- The concept of determinant-occurrence relationship provides guidance in structuring the research question

**7. Study Design Compatibility:**
- The research question determines the appropriate study design
- Researchers must decide whether to assign determinants themselves (experimental study) or examine events as nature takes its course (observational study)
- Analytical studies require a comparison group; descriptive studies do not

## PART A-2: CORRELATION COEFFICIENT (Q10)

### Definition

A correlation coefficient is a statistical measure that quantifies the strength and direction of the linear association between two variables. It is expressed as a value ranging from -1 to +1.

### Interpretation

| Value | Interpretation |
|-------|---------------|
| +1 | Perfect positive correlation |
| 0 | No correlation |
| -1 | Perfect negative correlation |
| 0.00 to 0.25 | Little or no correlation |
| 0.25 to 0.50 | Fair degree of correlation |
| 0.50 to 0.75 | Moderate to good correlation |
| 0.75 to 1.00 | Good to excellent correlation |

### Types of Correlation Coefficient

**1. Pearson's Product-Moment Correlation (r):**
- Used for continuous, normally distributed (parametric) data
- Measures the linear relationship between two variables
- Most commonly used correlation coefficient in medical research
- Example: Correlation between hearing threshold and age

**2. Spearman's Rank Correlation (rho/rs):**
- Used for ordinal data or non-normally distributed (non-parametric) data
- Based on ranked values rather than actual values
- Example: Correlation between grade of [[Facial Nerve|facial nerve palsy]] (House-Brackmann) and recovery time

### Important Distinction: Correlation vs Agreement

As noted by Bland and Altman, a correlation coefficient measures the strength of association between two measures, but not the extent of agreement. Two measures may be perfectly correlated (r = 1) yet consistently disagree by a fixed amount. For assessing agreement, Bland and Altman recommend plotting the scores from two tests graphically (Bland-Altman plot), which is a simpler method than statistical comparison alone.

### Correlation Coefficient in Clinical Research

**Test-Retest Reliability:**
The reproducibility of an instrument (test-retest reliability) is commonly reported as a correlation coefficient. Repeat measurements should be far enough apart in time for the subject to forget earlier answers, but not so far apart that their health status might have changed.

**Internal Consistency (Cronbach's Alpha):**
Individual questions in a questionnaire domain should correlate highly with each other and with the total score. This internal consistency is measured using Cronbach's alpha:
- Alpha = 1: All questions are identical (redundant)
- Alpha = 0: No relationship between questions
- Alpha between 0.7 and 0.9: Ideal range
- Alpha > 0.9: Questions may be addressing a very narrow aspect; some items may be redundant (reduces content validity)

### Key Points About Correlation Coefficient

- Correlation does not imply causation
- The square of the correlation coefficient (r-squared) indicates the proportion of variance in one variable explained by the other
- A statistically significant correlation may still be clinically insignificant if the sample size is very large
- Scatter plots should always be examined visually before calculating correlation coefficients, as outliers can distort the result

## PART A: TYPES OF RESEARCH

Research can be broadly classified based on purpose, design, and the direction of data collection.

### I. Classification Based on Purpose

**1. Basic (Fundamental) Research:**
- Aims to expand knowledge of fundamental biological processes
- Conducted in a laboratory setting (bench research)
- Examples: molecular biology studies, genetic studies, cell biology experiments

**2. Applied (Clinical) Research:**
- Aims to solve practical clinical problems
- Directly relevant to patient care
- Examples: drug trials, surgical technique comparisons, diagnostic test evaluations

**3. Translational Research:**
- Bridges the gap between basic science discoveries and clinical application ("bench to bedside")

### II. Classification Based on Study Design

A fundamental distinction exists between experimental and observational studies, and between descriptive and analytical studies.

**Table: Taxonomy of Research Study Designs**

| Category | Sub-type | Key Feature |
|----------|----------|-------------|
| **Descriptive** | Case report | Single patient description |
| | Case series | Group of patients, no controls |
| | Surveillance study | Ongoing data collection |
| **Analytical Observational** | Cross-sectional | Determinant and outcome measured simultaneously |
| | Case-control | Looks backward from outcome to determinant |
| | Cohort (prospective/retrospective) | Follows groups over time from determinant to outcome |
| **Experimental** | Randomized controlled trial (RCT) | Investigator assigns determinant randomly |
| **Secondary Research** | Systematic review | Systematic search, critical appraisal, synthesis |
| | Meta-analysis | Statistical combination of results from multiple studies |
| **Qualitative** | Qualitative/humanistic enquiry | Explores psycho-social and attitudinal aspects |

### III. Detailed Description of Key Study Types

#### A. Descriptive Studies

- **Case report/Case series:** The most frequently published study type in surgery. Can describe the distribution of diseases in a population but cannot directly quantify attributable risk. Strength: inexpensive, uses existing data, few ethical limitations. Weakness: danger of over-interpretation.

- **Surveillance study:** Ongoing systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of health data essential to planning, implementation and evaluation of health services.

#### B. Analytical Observational Studies

- **Cross-sectional study:** Determinant and outcome measured at the same time; no follow-up. Well suited for estimating prevalence. Cannot establish causal relationships or temporal order. A special case includes studies of diagnostic test accuracy.

- **Cohort study:** Follows groups over time. Best way to ascertain disease incidence and natural history. Provides temporal order between determinant and outcome. May be prospective or retrospective based on data collection method. Drawback: expensive and inefficient for rare outcomes.

- **Case-control study:** Selects patients by outcome (cases vs controls) and looks backward at determinant exposure. Efficient in time, effort and money. Especially relevant when occurrence of outcome is low. Cannot produce estimates of disease incidence. The relevant effect measure is the odds ratio.

#### C. Experimental Studies

- **Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT):** The defining feature is random allocation of the determinant (intervention). Randomization eliminates the influence of both known and unknown confounders present at the time of randomization. Key design features include:
  - Concealment of randomization (inadequate concealment may exaggerate treatment effect by >40%)
  - Blinding/masking (lack of blinding may exaggerate effectiveness by ~15%)
  - Adequate follow-up (if <80% followed up, results become meaningless)
  - Intention-to-treat analysis

#### D. Secondary Research

- **Systematic Review:** A review of a clearly formulated question using systematic and explicit methods to identify, select and critically appraise relevant research. Three key features: strenuous effort to locate all original reports, critical evaluation of reports, and conclusions based on preset quality criteria. Must follow PRISMA guidelines.

- **Meta-analysis:** The use of statistical techniques in a systematic review to integrate results of included studies into one overall statistic. Uses Forest plots with odds ratios or weighted mean differences.

- **Qualitative research:** Explores questions related to psycho-social and attitudinal aspects that cannot be measured in a prospective RCT.

### IV. Levels of Evidence

**Table: Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Classification**

| Level | Evidence |
|-------|----------|
| 1a | Systematic review of RCTs; meta-analyses |
| 1b | Individual RCT |
| 2a | Systematic review of cohort studies |
| 2b | Individual cohort study |
| 2c | Outcomes research |
| 3a | Systematic review of case-control studies |
| 3b | Individual case-control study |
| 4 | Case series (and poor quality cohort/case-control studies) |
| 5 | Expert opinion, anecdotal evidence, case reports, or based on physiology/bench research |

Caution: A rigid hierarchical approach is not always appropriate. A well-designed cohort or case-control study may provide better evidence than a small or poorly conducted RCT. The GRADE Working Group has developed a system for grading quality of evidence that classifies studies by both design and quality.

## PART B: EVALUATION OF A RESEARCH PAPER (CRITICAL APPRAISAL)

Critical appraisal is the process of systematically assessing the validity, results, and applicability of a research paper. It is a logical way of assessing the likely truth of a piece of research, evaluating the results in terms of individual patient care, and determining whether it is useful for practice.

### Step 1: Read the Abstract

- Gain an overview of the methods and results
- Determine the study type (RCT, cohort, case-control, systematic review, etc.)
- The abstract should clearly describe the population, intervention, comparison, outcomes (PICO)
- If there is a complex description that is confusing, ask why - authors may be hiding an overall negative result

### Step 2: Determine if the Paper Answers Your Question

- Use the PICO format to structure your question:
  - **P** = Patient/Population
  - **I** = Intervention
  - **C** = Comparison
  - **O** = Outcome
- Unless the paper is the highest level of evidence and deals specifically with your question, look for individual RCTs addressing specific relevant outcomes

### Step 3: Assess Validity (Is the Study Valid?)

Check for the three categories of bias:

**A. Selection Bias:**
- Does the sample adequately represent the target population?
- Are the comparison groups similar in all important respects apart from the determinant of interest?
- Confounding by clinical indication is a common form

**B. Information Bias:**
- Was information gathered in the same way for all groups?
- Were outcome assessors blinded to determinant status?
- Were determinant assessors blinded to outcome status?

**C. Confounding:**
- Were known confounders controlled for in design (restriction, matching) or analysis (stratification, multivariate adjustment)?

#### Specific Validity Checks by Study Type:

**For RCTs:**
- Was randomization adequate and concealed?
- Was there blinding (single/double)?
- Was follow-up adequate (>80%)?
- Was analysis by intention-to-treat?
- Were groups treated equally apart from the intervention?

**For Diagnostic Studies:**
- Was the reference (gold) standard appropriate?
- Were the reference standard and diagnostic test interpreted blind and independently?
- Was the reference standard applied to all patients?
- Was the test evaluated on an appropriate spectrum of patients (spectrum bias)?

**For Systematic Reviews:**
- What databases and sources were searched? (Standard databases fail to correctly index up to half of published trials)
- Were hand searches and unpublished literature searches included?
- How were studies selected (eligibility criteria)?
- Was there assessment of methodological quality (risk of bias)?
- Was heterogeneity (clinical and statistical) assessed?
- Were data abstracted by two independent reviewers?

### Step 4: Assess the Results (What Are the Results?)

**For Therapy Articles:**

| Measure | Formula | Interpretation |
|---------|---------|----------------|
| Control Event Rate (CER) | Events in control / Total controls | Baseline risk |
| Experimental Event Rate (EER) | Events in treatment / Total treated | Treatment group risk |
| Relative Risk (RR) | EER / CER | Risk in treated vs control |
| Relative Risk Reduction (RRR) | (CER - EER) / CER | Proportional reduction in risk |
| Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) | CER - EER | Actual risk difference |
| Number Needed to Treat (NNT) | 1 / ARR | Patients to treat to prevent one event |

**For Diagnostic Articles:**

| Measure | Meaning | Clinical Use |
|---------|---------|--------------|
| Sensitivity | Proportion of diseased with +ve test | SnNOut: High sensitivity, negative test rules OUT disease |
| Specificity | Proportion of non-diseased with -ve test | SpPIn: High specificity, positive test rules IN disease |
| Positive Predictive Value | Proportion of +ve tests that are true +ve | Varies with prevalence |
| Negative Predictive Value | Proportion of -ve tests that are true -ve | Varies with prevalence |
| Likelihood Ratio (+ve) | Sensitivity / (1 - Specificity) | LR >10 good at ruling IN |
| Likelihood Ratio (-ve) | (1 - Sensitivity) / Specificity | LR <0.1 good at ruling OUT |

**For Systematic Reviews/Meta-analyses:**
- Forest plot: Square box = individual study effect (point estimate); lines = confidence interval; diamond = pooled summary effect
- If confidence interval does not cross the line of no effect (OR = 1), the result is statistically significant
- Assess heterogeneity: chi-square test, I-squared statistic
- Clinical vs statistical heterogeneity must be distinguished

### Step 5: Assess Applicability (Will Results Help My Patient?)

- Is my patient sufficiently similar to those in the trial?
- Was the trial explanatory (ideal conditions, selected cohort) or pragmatic (real-world)?
- Use the f-method: estimate patient's risk compared to control group as a fraction (f), then NNT for patient = trial NNT / f
- Were all clinically relevant outcomes considered?
- Are benefits worth the harms and costs?
- Incorporate patient's values and preferences

## PART C: JOURNAL INDEXING

### Definition

Journal indexing refers to the process by which a scientific journal is evaluated, accepted, and included in a searchable bibliographic database. An indexed journal is one that has been reviewed and listed in a recognized database, making its articles discoverable through structured searches using keywords, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms), and other controlled vocabulary.

### Major Indexing Databases

| Database | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **PubMed/MEDLINE** | The most widely used biomedical database; maintained by the US National Library of Medicine (NLM); uses MeSH terms for structured indexing |
| **Embase** | Published by Elsevier; strong coverage of pharmacology and European literature |
| **Scopus** | Largest abstract and citation database; covers multiple disciplines |
| **Web of Science** | Clarivate Analytics database; basis for Journal Citation Reports (JCR) and Impact Factor calculation |
| **Cochrane Library** | Focused on systematic reviews of healthcare interventions |
| **Google Scholar** | Broad coverage including grey literature (book reviews, web pages); less structured |

### MeSH (Medical Subject Headings)

MeSH is a standard thesaurus for medical indexing used by PubMed/MEDLINE. It provides a controlled vocabulary of terms used to tag articles so that searches can retrieve relevant results even when authors use different terminology. Standard databases may fail to correctly index up to half of published trials, making comprehensive searches essential.

### Process of Indexing

1. A journal submits an application to the indexing body
2. The journal is evaluated on: scientific quality, peer review process, editorial standards, regularity of publication, ethical guidelines adherence, international diversity of contributors
3. If accepted, articles from that journal are indexed with appropriate keywords/MeSH terms
4. Articles become discoverable through database searches

### Significance of Indexing

- **Discoverability:** Indexed articles are searchable by researchers worldwide
- **Credibility:** Indexing implies the journal has met quality standards
- **Citation potential:** Indexed articles are more likely to be read and cited
- **Academic recognition:** Publications in indexed journals carry greater weight for academic promotion and grant applications
- **Inclusion in systematic reviews:** Systematic reviews search indexed databases, so non-indexed articles may be missed (contributing to publication bias)

## PART D: IMPACT FACTOR AND ITS RELATIONSHIP TO INDEXING

### Definition of Impact Factor

The Journal Impact Factor (JIF) is a bibliometric measure calculated annually by Clarivate Analytics (formerly Thomson Reuters) and published in the Journal Citation Reports (JCR). It measures the average number of citations received per article published in that journal during the two preceding years.

### Formula

**Impact Factor (Year X) = Citations in Year X to articles published in Years (X-1) and (X-2) / Total citable articles published in Years (X-1) and (X-2)**

*Example:* If a journal published 100 articles in 2023-2024, and those articles received 300 citations in 2025, the 2025 Impact Factor = 300/100 = 3.0

### How Indexing Affects Impact Factor

| Mechanism | Effect on Impact Factor |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Increased visibility** | Indexed journals are searchable, increasing readership and citation potential |
| **Database inclusion** | Only journals indexed in Web of Science/JCR receive an official Impact Factor |
| **Citation tracking** | Indexing enables systematic tracking of citations across journals |
| **Publication bias** | Positive results are more likely published in high-IF indexed journals; negative trials may go to non-indexed journals |
| **Quality signal** | Indexing attracts better submissions, leading to higher quality papers and more citations |
| **Systematic review inclusion** | Indexed articles are included in systematic reviews, generating additional citations |

### Key Points About Impact Factor

1. **Only journals indexed in Web of Science receive an official Impact Factor** - this is the most direct link between indexing and impact factor
2. Impact Factor reflects journal-level metrics, not the quality of individual articles
3. Self-citation by journals can artificially inflate Impact Factor
4. Review articles tend to receive more citations than original research, inflating IF of review-heavy journals
5. Impact Factor varies significantly across specialties (basic science journals typically have higher IF than clinical journals)
6. Open-access journals in indexed databases may have higher citation rates due to greater accessibility

### Alternative Bibliometric Indices

| Index | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **h-index** | Author-level metric: h papers cited at least h times |
| **CiteScore** | Scopus-based; citations in Year X to documents in Years X-1, X-2, X-3 divided by total documents |
| **SJR (SCImago Journal Rank)** | Weighted by prestige of citing journal |
| **SNIP** | Source Normalized Impact per Paper; accounts for citation practices in different fields |
| **Altmetrics** | Measures online attention (social media, news, policy documents) |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The research question is the most critical step in planning a study — many studies fail because the question was not formulated adequately.
> 2. PICO framework (Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) structures a well-defined research question and literature search.
> 3. A research topic must pass the "so what" test — it should add significantly to existing knowledge and influence clinical policies.
> 4. Correlation coefficient ranges from -1 to +1 and measures the strength and direction of linear association between two variables.
> 5. Pearson's r is used for parametric data; Spearman's rho for non-parametric/ordinal data.
> 6. Correlation measures association, NOT agreement — Bland-Altman plots should be used to assess agreement.
> 7. Cronbach's alpha (ideal 0.7-0.9) measures internal consistency of questionnaire domains.
> 8. Research is broadly classified into descriptive (case report, case series, surveillance) and analytical (cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, RCT) study types.
> 9. The Oxford Centre for EBM hierarchy places systematic reviews of RCTs (Level 1a) at the top and expert opinion (Level 5) at the bottom.
> 10. Inadequate concealment of randomization may exaggerate treatment effect by >40%; lack of blinding by ~15%.
> 11. If <80% of patients are followed up in an RCT, results become meaningless.
> 12. Critical appraisal involves three core questions: Is the study valid? What are the results? Can the results be applied to my patient?
> 13. Journal indexing is the inclusion of a journal in searchable bibliographic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science).
> 14. Only journals indexed in Web of Science/JCR receive an official Impact Factor.
> 15. Impact Factor = Citations in current year to articles in previous 2 years / Total citable articles in previous 2 years.
> 16. Standard databases may fail to correctly index up to half of published trials, making hand-searching essential for systematic reviews.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. A well-designed cohort or case-control study may provide better evidence than a small or poorly conducted RCT - never blindly follow the hierarchy.
> 2. The GRADE system classifies studies by both design AND quality, offering a more nuanced approach than the Oxford levels alone.
> 3. Case-control studies are more efficient than cohort studies when the outcome is rare; cohort studies are more efficient when the determinant is rare.
> 4. Likelihood ratios are superior to sensitivity/specificity because they are unaffected by disease prevalence and can be combined for multiple tests.
> 5. Publication bias (positive results more likely to be published) and language bias (positive results from non-English teams more likely published in English) are major threats to systematic review validity.
> 6. PRISMA guidelines have replaced the QUORUM statement for reporting systematic reviews; the Cochrane risk of bias tool is used for assessing RCT quality.
> 7. Impact Factor should never be used as a surrogate for the quality of individual articles - a landmark study may appear in a low-IF journal and vice versa.
