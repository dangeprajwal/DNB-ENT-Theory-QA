## Introduction

Blood transfusion is an essential component of perioperative management in ENT and head and neck surgery, particularly in major oncological procedures where significant blood loss is anticipated. Understanding blood components, transfusion reactions and hypersensitivity mechanisms is crucial for safe surgical practice. The four principles of safe transfusion include: transfusing the right blood to the right patient at the right time in the right place. This chapter covers the Gell and Coombs classification of hypersensitivity reactions, blood components and fractions, transfusion reactions, and strategies to minimize transfusion including autotransfusion and alternatives to blood.

## Part A: Hypersensitivity Reactions — Gell and Coombs Classification

Hypersensitivity reactions are exaggerated or inappropriate immune responses that cause tissue damage. The Gell and Coombs classification divides hypersensitivity into four types:

### Type I — Immediate (IgE-mediated) Hypersensitivity

Type I hypersensitivity is mediated by IgE antibodies bound to the high-affinity FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. In a previously sensitized individual, re-exposure to the allergen causes cross-linking of surface-bound IgE, triggering mast cell degranulation and release of preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin) within minutes. This is followed by release of newly synthesized mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor) in the late phase.

The effects include smooth muscle contraction, vasodilation, increased vascular permeability and mucus hypersecretion. Up to 40% of the population in industrialized countries have a tendency to atopy (genetic predisposition to Type I reactions). Both host factors (genetic) and environmental factors contribute to the development of allergy.

**Examples in ENT:** [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|Allergic rhinitis]], allergic asthma, anaphylaxis (to drugs, blood products, latex), food allergy.

### Type II — Cytotoxic (Antibody-mediated) Hypersensitivity

Type II reactions involve IgG, IgA or IgM antibodies directed against cell surface antigens or basement membrane antigens. Antibody binding activates the complement cascade, leading to membrane damage and cell lysis. Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by natural killer cells also contributes.

**Examples:** Haemolytic transfusion reactions (ABO incompatibility), pemphigus vulgaris, pemphigoid, Goodpasture's syndrome, myasthenia gravis, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, haemolytic disease of the newborn.

### Type III — Immune Complex-mediated Hypersensitivity

Type III reactions involve IgG, IgA or IgM antibodies forming complexes with soluble antigens. These immune complexes precipitate in tissues, activate complement and attract neutrophils, causing tissue damage through release of lysosomal enzymes and oxygen free radicals.

**Examples:** Extrinsic allergic alveolitis (farmer's lung, bird fancier's lung), serum sickness, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

### Type IV — Delayed (Cell-mediated) Hypersensitivity

Type IV reactions are mediated by sensitized T-lymphocytes rather than antibodies. The response develops 24–72 hours after antigen exposure. Sensitized T cells release cytokines that recruit and activate macrophages, leading to granuloma formation in chronic cases.

**Examples:** Contact dermatitis (nickel allergy relevant to ear piercings), tuberculin skin test (Mantoux), autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's), sarcoidosis, graft rejection.

### Summary Table

| Feature | Type I | Type II | Type III | Type IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mediator | IgE | IgG/IgA/IgM | IgG/IgA/IgM | T cells |
| Effector cells | Mast cells, basophils | Complement, NK cells | Complement, neutrophils | Macrophages, T cells |
| Onset | Minutes | Hours | 6–12 hours | 24–72 hours |
| Mechanism | Mast cell degranulation | Cytotoxicity | Immune complex deposition | Cell-mediated inflammation |
| ENT examples | Allergic rhinitis, anaphylaxis | Haemolytic transfusion, pemphigus | Allergic alveolitis | Contact dermatitis |

## Part B: Mechanism of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially fatal systemic Type I hypersensitivity reaction. The IgE-mediated signalling pathway involves:

1. **Sensitization phase:** First exposure to an allergen leads to IgE production by B cells (class-switched under the influence of IL-4 and IL-13 from Th2 cells). IgE binds to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils.

2. **Activation phase:** Re-exposure to the allergen causes cross-linking of surface-bound IgE on mast cells. This triggers FcεRI signalling through Src family kinases (Lyn initiates signalling; Syk propagates it), leading to ITAM phosphorylation and downstream signalling cascades.

3. **Mediator release:**
   - **Immediate (preformed mediators):** Histamine, tryptase, heparin — cause immediate vasodilation, increased permeability, bronchospasm
   - **Late phase (newly synthesized):** Leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4), prostaglandin D2, PAF — sustain and amplify the inflammatory response

4. **Systemic effects:** Profound vasodilation (distributive shock), increased capillary permeability (fluid extravasation, angioedema), bronchospasm, laryngeal oedema, myocardial depression, and gut smooth muscle contraction.

**Diagnosis of anaphylaxis:** Serum tryptase is the key biomarker. Tryptase is released from mast cell granules during anaphylaxis and has a specificity of 70% and sensitivity of 100%. Levels should be measured at 1 hour and 6 hours post-event. However, normal tryptase levels do not completely exclude anaphylaxis. Post-mortem findings in fatal anaphylaxis include laryngeal oedema, pulmonary hyperinflation, pulmonary oedema and eosinophilic infiltration around bronchi.

## Part C: Blood Groups and Compatibility

### ABO System

The ABO blood group system is the most important in transfusion medicine. The distribution of blood groups in the UK population is:

| Blood Group | Frequency (UK) | Antigens on RBC | Antibodies in Plasma |
|---|---|---|---|
| O | 47% | Neither A nor B | Anti-A and Anti-B (IgM) |
| A | 42% | A antigen | Anti-B |
| B | 8% | B antigen | Anti-A |
| AB | 3% | Both A and B | Neither |

Anti-A and anti-B are naturally occurring IgM antibodies that cause rapid complement-mediated intravascular haemolysis when ABO-incompatible blood is transfused. This is the basis of the most severe immediate haemolytic transfusion reactions. ABO incompatibility is commonly caused by human clerical error and occurs in approximately 1 in 180,000 red cells transfused.

### Rh System

The Rh system is the second most important blood group system. Approximately 85% of the population is RhD positive. Unlike ABO antibodies, Rh antibodies (IgG) are not naturally occurring — they develop after exposure to Rh-positive blood through transfusion or pregnancy. Rh antibodies cause extravascular haemolysis. RhD-negative women of childbearing age must receive only RhD-negative blood and platelets to prevent sensitization and haemolytic disease of the newborn.

### Blood Grouping and Crossmatch

| Test | Time Required | Information Provided |
|---|---|---|
| Rapid ABO/RhD grouping | <15 minutes | ABO and RhD status |
| Antibody screen | 45 minutes | Detects clinically significant antibodies |
| Full crossmatch | 60 minutes | Confirms compatibility of specific units |
| Electronic blood issue | Faster | Computer-matched if two concordant G&S results; no serological crossmatch |

### Red Blood Cells (RBC)

Transfusion of RBC is indicated in massive haemorrhage, symptomatic anaemia, after chemotherapy and in chronic haematological conditions. RBCs are stored at 2–6°C for up to 35 days in SAGM solution. Irradiated blood (for patients at risk of TA-GVHD) has a shelf life of 14 days. Universal leucocyte reduction has been standard since 1999 to reduce the risk of vCJD transmission, febrile transfusion reactions and CMV transmission.

A restrictive transfusion strategy (trigger Hb <70 g/L, target 70–90 g/L) is recommended based on the landmark TRICC trial and subsequent studies. The practice is to transfuse 1 unit followed by reassessment rather than empirical multi-unit transfusion.

### Platelets

Platelets are prepared from whole blood (pooled platelet concentrates) or from a single donor by plateletpheresis. They are stored at 22°C with constant agitation and have a shelf life of 7 days. Platelets should ideally be ABO compatible. Irradiated platelets should be given to patients at risk of TA-GVHD. Some patients with HLA antibodies require HLA-matched platelets.

| Platelet Threshold >50 × 10⁹/L | Platelet Threshold >100 × 10⁹/L |
|---|---|
| Lumbar puncture | Ocular surgery |
| Gastroscopy and biopsy | Neurosurgery |
| Insertion of central lines | |
| Transbronchial biopsy | Spinal anaesthesia: >80 |
| Liver biopsy / laparotomy | |

In ENT patients with thrombocytopenic bleeding, consider tranexamic acid and surgical repair of the bleeding source alongside platelet transfusion. Platelets are relatively contraindicated in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) due to thrombotic risk.

### Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)

FFP is rich in coagulation factors. It must be ABO compatible. Stored at −25°C for up to 36 months; once thawed, must be used within 24 hours. The dose is 12–15 mL/kg. Indications include coagulation factor deficiency, coagulopathy in liver disease, massive haemorrhage and DIC. FFP is no longer recommended for warfarin reversal — prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) is now first-line.

In the UK, children born after 1 January 1996 receive FFP from countries with low vCJD risk, treated with methylene blue for pathogen inactivation.

### Cryoprecipitate

Cryoprecipitate is made from thawing FFP at 4°C. It is rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII and von Willebrand factor. It is used primarily as a source of fibrinogen, especially in massive haemorrhage where fibrinogen concentration falls below 1.5 g/L. The recommended dose is 2 pools of 5 units (1 unit per 5–10 kg body weight). Stored at −25°C for 36 months; once thawed, must be used within 4 hours.

## Part E: Transfusion Reactions

All transfusion reactions must be reported to SHOT (Serious Hazards of Transfusion). In 2012, 62% of errors reported to SHOT were secondary to preventable mistakes in the transfusion process.

### Approach to Acute Transfusion Reactions (Box 24.2)

1. Stop the transfusion
2. Assess patient: Airway, Breathing and Circulation
3. Maintain venous access with physiological saline
4. Check identification of patient and compatibility of blood component
5. Inspect the component for clumps or discolouration
6. Assess for evidence of blood loss
7. Unless mild allergic/febrile reaction — perform FBC, renal/liver screen, blood culture, direct Coombs test, repeat group and screen, urine for haemoglobinuria
8. Inform the transfusion laboratory
9. Return blood components to the transfusion laboratory
10. Report to SHOT

### Classification of Transfusion Reactions

| Reaction | Timing | Mechanism | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate haemolytic | Within 24 hours | IgM anti-A/anti-B destroy donor RBCs; intravascular haemolysis | Pain at infusion site, chest/back pain, hypotension, haemoglobinuria; mortality up to 10% | Stop transfusion; IV fluids; treat DIC/renal failure |
| Bacterial contamination | Within 24 hours | Bacterial growth in stored blood | Acute sepsis, pyrexia, hypotension, septic shock | Stop transfusion; blood cultures; broad-spectrum antibiotics |
| Anaphylaxis | Within 24 hours | Anti-IgA or anti-plasma protein antibodies | Wheeze, stridor, angioedema, shock | IM adrenaline, IV chlorpheniramine, IV hydrocortisone |
| TRALI | Within 6 hours | Anti-leucocyte antibodies in donor plasma | Severe hypoxia, hypotension, bilateral lung infiltrates | Supportive; high-flow O₂; ventilatory support |
| TACO | Within 6 hours | Circulatory overload | Dyspnoea, tachycardia, hypertension, pulmonary oedema | Stop transfusion; O₂; diuretics |
| FNHTR | Within 24 hours | Anti-leucocyte antibodies (reduced since leucocyte reduction) | Pyrexia, rigours, nausea, muscle pain | Slow/stop transfusion; paracetamol |
| Mild allergic | Within 24 hours | Anti-plasma protein antibodies | Urticaria | Slow transfusion; antihistamines |

### Delayed Transfusion Reactions

**Delayed haemolytic transfusion reaction (DHTR):** Occurs days after transfusion in patients with previous transfusion or pregnancy history. Alloantibodies (Rh, Kell, Duffy) that have fallen to undetectable levels are boosted by re-exposure. IgG-mediated extravascular haemolysis causes jaundice, haemoglobinuria, fever and fatigue, with a drop in haemoglobin and positive direct Coombs test.

**Post-transfusion purpura (PTP):** Severe thrombocytopenia (platelets <10 × 10⁹/L) occurring 7–12 days post-transfusion, typically in HPA-1a-negative females with anti-HPA-1a antibodies. Rare since leucocyte reduction. Responds to high-dose IV immunoglobulin.

**Transfusion-associated graft versus host disease (TA-GVHD):** Rare but usually fatal. Donor lymphocytes engraft in the recipient and mount an immune response. Presents with fever, rash, diarrhoea, jaundice and bone marrow failure up to 30 days post-transfusion. Prevented by gamma irradiation of blood for at-risk patients (immunodeficiency, chemotherapy recipients, post-BMT patients).

## Part F: Alternatives to Blood Transfusion and Autotransfusion

### Definition of Autotransfusion

Autotransfusion is the collection and re-infusion of the patient's own blood, either pre-donated before surgery or salvaged during or after surgery. It eliminates the risks of alloimmunization, transfusion-transmitted infections and incompatibility reactions.

### Patient Blood Management Strategies

Modern transfusion practice emphasizes "patient blood management" — a multidisciplinary approach to minimize the need for allogeneic blood transfusion:

**1. Pre-operative anaemia management:**
- Identify and investigate anaemia before surgery
- Treat iron deficiency with oral iron; if not tolerated or no response after 4–6 weeks, use IV iron
- Recombinant erythropoietin for anaemia in chronic renal failure, malignancy or myelodysplasia (limited by cost)
- Withdraw aspirin, clopidogrel and NSAIDs in advance of surgery; avoid NSAIDs post-operatively

**2. Acute normovolaemic haemodilution (ANH):**
Blood is removed immediately pre-operatively (usually post-anaesthesia induction) and replaced with crystalloid or colloid. The collected blood is returned post-operatively or if significant bleeding occurs. Limited evidence for effectiveness; rarely used outside cardiac surgery.

**3. Intra-operative cell salvage (ICS):**
Blood lost during surgery is aspirated into a collection reservoir, filtered, treated with heparin and citrate, centrifuged, washed and re-infused via a leucocyte-reduction filter in a closed automated system within 4 hours. Indicated in surgery where anticipated blood loss exceeds 20% of blood volume (>1 L in an adult). Widely used in major orthopaedic, cardiac, vascular and cancer surgery.

**4. Post-operative cell salvage:**
Blood is collected from wound drains, filtered or washed and re-infused. Has very little role in ENT surgery.

**5. Tissue sealants:**
Biological agents derived from human or animal clotting products (thrombin, fibrinogen, calcium) applied directly to bleeding points. Commonly used in emergency departments for minor cutaneous bleeds.

**6. Tranexamic acid:**
An antifibrinolytic drug that inhibits conversion of plasminogen to plasmin. Can be given orally, topically or intravenously. Recognized role in massive haemorrhage management and reducing post-operative bleeding. A mouthwash preparation is helpful for managing minor oral cavity bleeds.

### Artificial Blood (Haemoglobin-Based Oxygen Carriers — HBOCs)

Artificial blood refers to synthetic or semi-synthetic oxygen-carrying solutions that can serve as temporary substitutes for red blood cells. These include:

**Haemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs):** Solutions of purified haemoglobin (human or bovine) that are chemically modified (cross-linked, polymerized or conjugated to PEG) to improve stability and reduce renal toxicity. They carry and deliver oxygen without the need for crossmatching or blood typing. Advantages include universal compatibility, long shelf life and no risk of infection transmission. Limitations include short intravascular half-life, vasoconstriction from nitric oxide scavenging, oxidative toxicity and lack of regulatory approval in most countries.

**Perfluorocarbon emulsions (PFCs):** Synthetic compounds that dissolve oxygen. They require high inspired oxygen concentrations to function effectively. Clinical applications remain limited.

No artificial blood product is currently approved for routine clinical use in the UK or most Western countries. Research continues to develop safe and effective blood substitutes.

### Methods to Minimize Transfusion Reactions

1. **Correct patient identification:** Verify identity at every stage — sample collection, laboratory testing and bedside administration; the most common cause of severe transfusion reactions is human clerical error
2. **Appropriate crossmatching:** Full crossmatch (60 minutes) for planned procedures; rapid ABO/RhD (<15 minutes) for emergencies
3. **Leucocyte reduction:** Universal since 1999 in the UK; reduces FNHTR, CMV transmission and vCJD risk
4. **Irradiation:** For patients at risk of TA-GVHD (immunocompromised, post-chemotherapy, post-BMT)
5. **Clinical monitoring:** Observations before transfusion, at 15 minutes, and at end; avoid overnight transfusion unless urgent
6. **Restrictive transfusion:** Transfuse 1 unit at a time; reassess; trigger Hb <70 g/L
7. **Washed blood products:** Washing RBCs or platelets in saline removes plasma proteins; indicated for patients with repeated allergic reactions unresponsive to antihistamines
8. **Pre-medication:** Antihistamines and hydrocortisone for patients with history of mild allergic transfusion reactions
9. **Pathogen inactivation:** Methylene blue treatment of FFP; solvent-detergent plasma from pooled donations with standardized coagulation factor concentrations
10. **Donor screening:** Mandatory testing for HBV, HCV, HIV, HTLV, syphilis and travel-related infections (malaria, West Nile virus, T. cruzi)

### Jehovah's Witnesses

Jehovah's Witnesses do not accept red cell transfusions, whether donor blood or their own pre-donated blood. However, most patients will accept cell-salvaged blood provided the blood is returned without a break in the circuit. An individualized approach should be taken, discussing all options for blood avoidance. Most hospitals have a Jehovah's Witness liaison committee.

## Part G: Reversal of Anticoagulation in Surgery

FFP is no longer first-line for warfarin reversal. The current recommendation is:

| Anticoagulant | Reversal Agent | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Warfarin | Vitamin K (oral/IV) + PCC (25–50 units/kg) | PCC contains factors II, VII, IX, X; FFP only if PCC unavailable |
| Dabigatran | Consider PCC or rFVIIa | No licensed antidote at time of publication; consult haematology |
| Rivaroxaban | Consider PCC or rFVIIa | No licensed antidote; consult haematology |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The Gell and Coombs classification divides hypersensitivity into four types: Type I (IgE-mediated, immediate — allergic rhinitis, anaphylaxis), Type II (cytotoxic — haemolytic transfusion reactions, pemphigus), Type III (immune complex — allergic alveolitis, SLE) and Type IV (cell-mediated, delayed — contact dermatitis, thyroiditis).
>
> 2. Anaphylaxis is a severe Type I reaction in which cross-linking of IgE on mast cells triggers FcεRI signalling via Src kinases (Lyn and Syk), causing release of preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase) and newly synthesized mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins), leading to vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm and cardiovascular collapse.
>
> 3. The ABO blood group system is the most important for transfusion; anti-A and anti-B are naturally occurring IgM antibodies that cause intravascular haemolysis; Group O (47% in UK) is the universal donor for red cells; ABO incompatibility occurs in 1 in 180,000 transfusions and is commonly caused by human clerical error.
>
> 4. The four main blood components are: red blood cells (stored at 2–6°C for 35 days in SAGM), platelets (stored at 22°C for 7 days), fresh frozen plasma (stored at −25°C for 36 months, dose 12–15 mL/kg) and cryoprecipitate (rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII and vWF, used when fibrinogen <1.5 g/L).
>
> 5. Restrictive transfusion practice (trigger Hb <70 g/L, target 70–90 g/L) is the current standard based on the TRICC trial; exceptions are patients with acute cardiac ischaemia and those on chronic transfusion programmes who may have a higher trigger.
>
> 6. Transfusion reactions are classified as acute (within 24 hours — immediate haemolytic, bacterial contamination, anaphylaxis, TRALI, TACO, FNHTR) and delayed (DHTR, PTP, TA-GVHD); TRALI has been the commonest cause of severe transfusion-related morbidity and mortality.
>
> 7. Autotransfusion methods include pre-operative autologous donation, acute normovolaemic haemodilution, intra-operative cell salvage (ICS — indicated when anticipated blood loss >20% blood volume) and post-operative cell salvage; ICS has limited role in ENT surgery.
>
> 8. Ten key strategies to minimize transfusion reactions include correct patient identification, appropriate crossmatching, universal leucocyte reduction, irradiation for at-risk patients, clinical monitoring (baseline, 15 minutes and end), restrictive transfusion practice, washed blood products, pre-medication, pathogen inactivation and donor screening.
>
> 9. Artificial blood (HBOCs and perfluorocarbon emulsions) are experimental oxygen-carrying substitutes that offer universal compatibility and no infection risk but are limited by short half-life, vasoconstriction and lack of regulatory approval; no product is currently approved for routine clinical use.
>
> 10. FFP is no longer first-line for warfarin reversal — prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC, containing factors II, VII, IX, X) at 25–50 units/kg with vitamin K is the current standard; no licensed antidotes exist for the newer oral anticoagulants (dabigatran, rivaroxaban) and haematology should be consulted.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The most common cause of severe (immediate haemolytic) transfusion reactions is human clerical error — wrong blood in tube or wrong patient identification — reinforcing that correct patient identification at the bedside is the single most important safety measure in transfusion practice.
>
> - TRALI (transfusion-related acute lung injury) must be differentiated from TACO (transfusion-associated circulatory overload) — both cause acute respiratory distress within hours of transfusion, but TRALI presents with hypotension while TACO presents with hypertension and positive fluid balance; treatment differs significantly (supportive vs diuretics).
>
> - In ENT patients with a coagulopathy or thrombocytopenia requiring surgery, platelet thresholds differ by procedure — neurosurgery and ocular surgery require >100 × 10⁹/L, while most ENT procedures can proceed with >50 × 10⁹/L; spinal anaesthesia requires >80 × 10⁹/L.
>
> - Tranexamic acid is an inexpensive, versatile antifibrinolytic available as IV, oral and mouthwash preparations — the mouthwash is particularly useful for managing minor post-operative oral cavity bleeds and in patients on anticoagulants undergoing dental procedures.
>
> - Cryoprecipitate is the preferred source of fibrinogen in massive haemorrhage — it should be given when fibrinogen falls below 1.5 g/L as part of the massive haemorrhage protocol, alongside the standard MHP packs of RBC and FFP.
>
> - Blood donation in the UK is permitted for ages 17–65 years, with minimum intervals of 12 weeks for males and 16 weeks for females; minimum Hb for donation is 135 g/L for males and 125 g/L for females; all donations are screened for HBV, HCV, HIV, HTLV and syphilis.
