## Introduction

Shock is a life-threatening condition of circulatory failure resulting in inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular hypoxia. If uncorrected, it leads to progressive organ dysfunction and death. The ENT surgeon encounters shock in diverse clinical scenarios including post-[[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]] haemorrhage, major head and neck surgery, anaphylaxis to drugs or blood products, and perioperative sepsis. A thorough understanding of the classification, pathophysiology and management of shock is essential for safe surgical practice.

---

## Part A: Definition and Classification of Shock

### Definition

Shock is a state of acute circulatory failure with inadequate oxygen delivery to meet the metabolic demands of tissues, resulting in cellular hypoxia and progressive organ dysfunction. It is characterized by hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg), tachycardia, altered sensorium and evidence of end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria, rising lactate, metabolic acidosis).

### Classification of Shock

Shock is classified into four main types based on the underlying pathophysiology:

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | Reduced circulating volume | Haemorrhage, dehydration, burns |
| Distributive | Inappropriate vasodilation | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic |
| Cardiogenic | Pump failure | MI, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia |
| Obstructive | Mechanical obstruction | Tension pneumothorax, PE, tamponade |

**1. Hypovolemic shock:** Results from loss of intravascular volume. The commonest cause encountered by the ENT surgeon is haemorrhage (post-tonsillectomy, major head and neck surgery, neck trauma). Other causes include severe dehydration, burns and third-space fluid losses.

**2. Distributive shock:** Results from pathological vasodilation leading to relative hypovolemia despite a normal or increased circulating volume. Subtypes include septic shock (commonest), anaphylactic shock (IgE-mediated vasodilation and capillary leak) and neurogenic shock (loss of sympathetic tone following spinal cord injury, characterized by hypotension with bradycardia and warm peripheries).

**3. Cardiogenic shock:** Results from failure of the heart as a pump due to myocardial infarction, severe arrhythmias, valvular dysfunction or cardiomyopathy. Characterized by low cardiac output, elevated filling pressures and pulmonary oedema.

**4. Obstructive shock:** Results from mechanical obstruction to cardiac filling or output. Causes include tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade and massive pulmonary embolism.

## Part B: Haemorrhagic (Hypovolemic) Shock — Classification and Pathophysiology

### Classification of Haemorrhagic Shock (ATLS)

Haemorrhagic shock is graded into four classes based on estimated blood volume loss (estimated blood volume in an adult = 70 mL/kg, approximately 5 L in a 70 kg adult):

| Parameter | Class I | Class II | Class III | Class IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood loss (mL) | Up to 750 | 750–1500 | 1500–2000 | >2000 |
| Blood loss (% BV) | Up to 15% | 15–30% | 30–40% | >40% |
| Heart rate | <100 | 100–120 | 120–140 | >140 |
| Blood pressure | Normal | Normal | Decreased | Decreased |
| Pulse pressure | Normal | Narrowed | Narrowed | Narrowed |
| Respiratory rate | 14–20 | 20–30 | 30–40 | >35 |
| Urine output (mL/h) | >30 | 20–30 | 5–15 | Negligible |
| Mental status | Slightly anxious | Anxious | Confused | Lethargic |
| Fluid replacement | Crystalloid | Crystalloid | Crystalloid + blood | Crystalloid + blood |

### Pathophysiology of Hypovolemic Shock

The pathophysiology follows a sequence of compensatory mechanisms that ultimately fail:

```
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF HYPOVOLEMIC SHOCK

Blood loss → Decreased venous return → Decreased preload
       ↓
Decreased stroke volume → Decreased cardiac output
       ↓
Decreased MAP → Baroreceptor activation
       ↓
COMPENSATORY PHASE:
• Sympathetic activation → Tachycardia + Vasoconstriction
• RAAS activation → Na+/Water retention
• ADH secretion → Water retention
• Catecholamine surge → Peripheral vasoconstriction
       ↓
If blood loss continues → DECOMPENSATION:
• Tissue hypoperfusion → Anaerobic metabolism
• Lactic acidosis → Cellular injury
• Capillary leak → Third-spacing
• DIC → Coagulopathy
• Multi-organ dysfunction → Death
```

**Compensatory phase:** The initial response to blood loss involves baroreceptor-mediated sympathetic activation causing tachycardia and selective vasoconstriction of cutaneous, splanchnic and renal vascular beds to maintain perfusion to vital organs (brain, heart). The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and antidiuretic hormone promote sodium and water retention. These mechanisms can compensate for up to 15% blood volume loss (Class I) with minimal haemodynamic change.

**Decompensatory phase:** When blood loss exceeds 30% (Class III), compensatory mechanisms are overwhelmed. Progressive tissue hypoperfusion leads to anaerobic metabolism and lactic acidosis. Cellular injury activates the inflammatory cascade, capillary endothelial damage causes fluid extravasation (third-spacing), and activation of the coagulation cascade can lead to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). This vicious cycle of hypoperfusion, acidosis, hypothermia and coagulopathy (the "lethal triad") leads to multi-organ dysfunction and death if uncorrected.

### Massive Haemorrhage

Massive haemorrhage is defined as loss of more than 50% of blood volume within 3 hours, or loss at a rate exceeding 150 mL/min in association with clinical shock. The maximum surgical blood ordering schedule (MSBOS) for common ENT procedures is:

| Procedure | Blood Requirement |
|---|---|
| Tumour of palate | Group and screen only |
| Laryngectomy | 2 units |
| Radical [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] | 2 units |
| Commando procedure | 4 units |

## Part C: Management of Hypovolemic/Haemorrhagic Shock

Management follows the ABCDE approach with simultaneous resuscitation and identification of the bleeding source:

**A — Airway:** Secure the airway with cervical spine protection if trauma is suspected. In ENT emergencies such as post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage, protect the airway from aspiration of blood.

**B — Breathing:** Administer high-flow oxygen. Exclude tension pneumothorax (especially in neck trauma). Assess respiratory rate and oxygen saturation.

**C — Circulation:** Insert two large-bore (14G) intravenous cannulae in the antecubital fossae. Avoid inserting lines on the same side as a vascular injury. Take blood for full blood count, coagulation screen, group and crossmatch, urea and electrolytes, and serum lactate. Begin crystalloid resuscitation (initial bolus of 1–2 litres). If no improvement, proceed to blood transfusion — use O-negative blood in emergency while crossmatch is pending.

**D — Disability:** Assess GCS, pupil reactions and limb power. A shocked patient with warm peripheries and bradycardia may have neurogenic shock secondary to spinal cord injury.

**E — Exposure:** Fully expose the patient to identify all injury sites while maintaining temperature.

### Massive Haemorrhage Protocol (MHP)

The massive haemorrhage protocol is activated when massive haemorrhage is recognized. It requires close coordination with the transfusion laboratory:

**MHP Pack 1:** 4 units red blood cells + 4 units fresh frozen plasma

**MHP Pack 2:** Additional red cells + FFP + 1 adult dose platelets + 2 pools cryoprecipitate

**Pharmacological adjuncts:**
- Tranexamic acid: 1 g IV bolus followed by 1 g infusion over 8 hours (most effective when given within 1 hour of injury, as demonstrated in the CRASH-2 trial)
- Calcium replacement (ionized Ca²⁺ drops due to citrate in stored blood)

**Resuscitation targets during massive haemorrhage:**
- Haemoglobin: 80–100 g/L
- Platelets: >75 × 10⁹/L
- PT/APTT ratio: <1.5
- Fibrinogen: >1.5 g/L
- Ionized calcium: >1 mmol/L
- Temperature: >36°C

**Control of haemorrhage in ENT:**
- Direct pressure or compressive dressings for cervical bleeding
- Foley catheter tamponade for deep cervical vessel bleeding
- Surgical exploration and vessel ligation
- Interventional radiology (embolization) for select cases

## Part D: Pathophysiology and Management of Septic Shock

### Definition

Sepsis is defined as a documented or suspected infection with systemic manifestations of infection. The three states of sepsis, in increasing severity, are:

| State | Definition |
|---|---|
| Sepsis | Documented/suspected infection with systemic manifestations |
| Severe sepsis | Sepsis with organ hypoperfusion (hypotension, raised creatinine, raised bilirubin, prolonged INR, hypoxia, thrombocytopenia, raised lactate) |
| Septic shock | Severe sepsis with hypotension refractory to initial resuscitation — MAP <70 mmHg or systolic <90 mmHg after 30 mL/kg fluid resuscitation and initial vasopressor use |

### Pathophysiology

The underlying pathophysiology of septic shock is that of an uncontrolled physiological response to infection leading to systemic vasodilation produced by a host of cytokines including IL-6, IL-18, TNF-alpha, inducible nitric oxide synthase and arginine. The classic septic patient displays a hyperdynamic circulatory pattern with low filling pressures, maintained or increased cardiac output and microcirculatory dysfunction. However, this textbook presentation is not always seen — patients can range from those with maintained blood pressure but organ dysfunction to those with marked sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction who are far from hyperdynamic.

```
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF SEPTIC SHOCK

Infection (bacterial/fungal) → Immune activation
       ↓
Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines
(IL-6, IL-18, TNF-α, iNOS)
       ↓
Systemic vasodilation → ↓ SVR → Hypotension
       ↓
Capillary leak → Tissue oedema + Relative hypovolemia
       ↓
Microcirculatory dysfunction → Tissue hypoxia
       ↓
Myocardial depression → ↓ Cardiac output
       ↓
Multi-organ dysfunction → DEATH
```

**ENT-specific causes of sepsis:** ENT can be involved in the management of sepsis in critical care either in ENT patients who have developed perioperative sepsis as a primary or secondary infection, or in the investigation of occult sepsis in non-ENT patients. Most critical care patients have indwelling tubes through the nasal passages and are at increased risk of sinusitis that may be a difficult-to-diagnose source of sepsis.

### Management — The Sepsis Six Bundle

Early and aggressive management of sepsis reduces mortality. The Sepsis Six bundle must be implemented within the first hour of diagnosis:

1. Administer high-flow oxygen
2. Take blood cultures
3. Give broad-spectrum antibiotics
4. Give intravenous fluid challenges (30 mL/kg crystalloid)
5. Measure serum lactate
6. Measure hourly urine output

**Ongoing management:** Improving tissue oxygen delivery relies upon restoration of appropriate circulating volume and perfusing pressure. Noradrenaline is the first-line vasopressor alongside fluid resuscitation to restore organ autoregulatory flow. Endpoints for resuscitation include trends in lactate, venous oxygen saturations and urine output. In cases of reduced cardiac output, predominantly inotropic agents like dobutamine or adrenaline are indicated.

## Part E: Anaphylactic Shock — Mechanism and Management

### Mechanism

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening Type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity reaction. In a previously sensitized individual, re-exposure to an allergen causes cross-linking of IgE bound to FcεRI (high-affinity IgE receptors) on the surface of mast cells and basophils. This triggers rapid degranulation and release of preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin) as well as newly synthesized mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, platelet-activating factor). These mediators cause profound systemic vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, bronchospasm and mucosal oedema.

Clinical manifestations include urticaria, angioedema, laryngeal oedema causing stridor, bronchospasm causing wheeze, cardiovascular collapse with hypotension and tachycardia, and gastrointestinal symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea). ECG changes including T-wave inversion, arrhythmias and bundle branch blocks have been reported. Myocardial infarction may occur. Post-mortem findings in fatal anaphylaxis often show laryngeal oedema, pulmonary hyperinflation, pulmonary oedema and eosinophilic infiltration around bronchi. The lifetime prevalence of anaphylaxis is estimated at 0.05–2.0%.

### Severity Grading (Ring and Messmer)

| Grade | Cutaneous | Cardiac | Respiratory | Abdominal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Urticaria, flushing, angioedema | — | — | — |
| 2 | Urticaria, flushing, angioedema | Tachycardia, BP change >20 mmHg | Rhinorrhoea, hoarseness, breathlessness | Nausea |
| 3 | Urticaria, flushing, angioedema | Shock | Laryngeal oedema, wheeze, cyanosis | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea |
| 4 | Urticaria, flushing, angioedema | Cardiac arrest | Respiratory arrest | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea |

### Management of Anaphylaxis

**Immediate management (ABCDE approach):**

1. **Remove the trigger** — stop the offending drug/transfusion; remove the allergen
2. **Call for help** — activate emergency team
3. **Adrenaline (Epinephrine) IM** — the single most important drug:
   - Adult: 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000) intramuscularly into anterolateral thigh
   - Child 6–12 years: 0.3 mg IM
   - Child <6 years: 0.15 mg IM
   - Repeat every 5 minutes if no improvement
   - IV adrenaline (1:10,000) only in cardiac arrest or by experienced clinicians with monitoring
4. **High-flow oxygen** — 15 L/min via non-rebreather mask
5. **IV fluid bolus** — 500–1000 mL crystalloid rapidly (20 mL/kg in children)
6. **Chlorpheniramine IV** — 10 mg (antihistamine)
7. **Hydrocortisone IV** — 200 mg (prevents late-phase reaction; takes hours to work)
8. **Nebulized salbutamol** — for persistent bronchospasm
9. **Airway management** — intubation or surgical airway (cricothyrotomy) if laryngeal oedema progresses

**Post-event management:**
- Serum tryptase levels at 1 hour and 6 hours post-event (specificity 70%, sensitivity 100% for anaphylaxis)
- Observe for at least 6–12 hours (biphasic reactions may occur)
- Referral to allergy/immunology for identification of allergen
- Issue adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen) with training
- Document allergy in medical records and alert systems

### Anaphylaxis During Blood Transfusion

Severe anaphylaxis following transfusion is very rare but can be fatal. Symptoms include wheeze, stridor and angioedema. Immediate resuscitation is imperative with intramuscular adrenaline, intravenous chlorpheniramine and intravenous hydrocortisone. If further transfusions are needed, management should be discussed with the immunologist and haematologist.

## Part F: Contents of Packed Red Blood Cells and FFP

### Packed Red Blood Cells (RBC)

Red blood cells are responsible for the transport of oxygen. They are stored in SAGM (saline-adenine-glucose-mannitol) additive solution at 2–6°C for up to 35 days. Irradiated blood (for immunocompromised patients at risk of TA-GVHD) has a shorter shelf life of 14 days.

**Contents per unit:** Approximately 200–300 mL volume; haematocrit 55–65%; minimal plasma; leucocyte-depleted (<5 × 10⁶ WBC per unit since universal leucocyte reduction in 1999).

**Indications:**
- Massive haemorrhage
- Symptomatic anaemia
- After chemotherapy
- Chronic haematological conditions
- Restrictive transfusion threshold: Hb <70 g/L, target 70–90 g/L (exceptions: acute cardiac ischaemia and chronic transfusion programmes may have trigger Hb of 80 g/L or greater)

### Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)

FFP is derived from whole blood through centrifugation. It is rich in coagulation factors. It must be ABO compatible due to the presence of anti-A and anti-B antibodies. FFP is frozen at −25°C for up to 36 months. Once thawed (takes ~30 minutes), it can be stored at 4°C for 24 hours.

**Contents:** All coagulation factors in physiological concentrations; fibrinogen; albumin; immunoglobulins.

**Indications:**
- Coagulation factor deficiency where specific factor concentrate is unavailable
- Coagulopathy associated with liver disease
- Massive haemorrhage (as part of MHP)
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Dose: 12–15 mL/kg

**Note:** FFP is no longer recommended for reversal of warfarin — prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) containing factors II, VII, IX and X is now first-line.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage is the commonest ENT emergency causing hypovolemic shock — always assume the patient has swallowed significant blood (true blood loss is often underestimated) and secure the airway before attempting haemostasis.
>
> - A shocked patient with warm peripheries and bradycardia in the setting of neck trauma should raise suspicion for neurogenic shock secondary to spinal cord injury, which may mask concurrent haemorrhagic shock from a vascular injury.
>
> - ENT patients in critical care with indwelling nasogastric or nasotracheal tubes are at increased risk of nosocomial sinusitis, which may be an occult source of sepsis that is difficult to diagnose — ENT involvement for investigation and washout may be required.
>
> - Serum tryptase (specificity 70%, sensitivity 100%) is the key biomarker for confirming anaphylaxis — measure at 1 hour and 6 hours post-event; however, normal tryptase levels do not completely exclude anaphylaxis.
>
> - The CRASH-2 trial demonstrated that tranexamic acid given within 1 hour of major haemorrhage significantly reduces mortality — this inexpensive antifibrinolytic drug should be part of every massive haemorrhage protocol and is also available as a mouthwash for minor oral cavity bleeds.
>
> - The "lethal triad" of hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy in haemorrhagic shock creates a self-perpetuating cycle — temperature maintenance (>36°C) and early correction of coagulopathy with component therapy are as important as volume replacement.
