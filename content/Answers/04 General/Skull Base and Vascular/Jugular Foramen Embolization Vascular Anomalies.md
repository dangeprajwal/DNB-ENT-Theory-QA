## INTRODUCTION

The jugular foramen is the second largest opening in the skull base after the foramen magnum, transmitting the sigmoid-jugular venous complex and the lower cranial nerves IX, X and XI. Understanding its anatomy is essential for skull base surgery, particularly for [[Glomus Tumour|paraganglioma]]s and schwannomas. Endovascular embolization has become an indispensable tool in head and neck surgery, used for pre-operative devascularization of tumours, management of [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]], treatment of vascular malformations, and assessment of collateral blood flow before major vessel sacrifice. Anomalous vascular arrangements of the great vessels can cause tracheo-oesophageal compression in infants, presenting with stridor, dyspnoea and dysphagia.

## PART A: ANATOMY OF THE JUGULAR FORAMEN

### General Anatomy

The jugular foramina are the second largest openings in the skull base (after the foramen magnum). The left and right foramina are asymmetrical — the left is usually smaller than the right. The jugular foramen is formed at the junction of the petrous part of the temporal bone and the occipital bone (petrosphenooccipital suture of Gruber).

### Structural Components

The jugular foramen is divided into two compartments by the intrajugular processes, which protrude into the lumen at the caudal aspect:

| Compartment | Contents |
|-------------|----------|
| **Pars venosa (lateral)** | Sigmoid-jugular venous complex |
| **Pars nervosa (medial)** | Inferior petrosal sinus, CN IX (glossopharyngeal), CN X (vagus), CN XI (spinal accessory), superior ganglion of CN IX, jugular ganglion of CN X |

### Walls of the Jugular Foramen

- **Ventromedial wall:** Petrosphenooccipital suture of Gruber (joint between petrous and occipital bones)
- **Dorsolateral wall:** Dorsolateral aspect of the mastoid-occipital suture
- **Dorsomedial wall:** Incisura jugularis (curved indentation in the occipital bone)
- **Ventrolateral wall:** Counterpart of incisura jugularis in the petrous bone

### Important Anatomical Relations

**Venous drainage:**
- Transverse sinus opens into sigmoid sinus, which proceeds through the jugular foramen to become the internal jugular vein
- Several venous pathways drain into the sigmoid-jugular complex: superior and inferior petrosal sinuses, occipital sinus, mastoid and condylar emissary veins
- The inferior petrosal sinus (draining the cavernous sinus and basilar plexus) is the most important tributary

**Critical proximity relationships:**
- The dome of the jugular bulb is in close contact with the floor of the hypotympanum, the vestibule, the posterior semicircular canal, the vestibular aqueduct and the internal auditory canal
- The lateral side of the jugular bulb is close to the mastoid (vertical) segment of the facial nerve
- Only a small osseous spine separates the vertical part of the carotid canal from the jugular foramen — the ICA is immediately adjacent

### Clinical Syndromes

**Vernet's syndrome (jugular foramen syndrome):** Paralysis of cranial nerves IX, X and XI. Caused by tumours, infection or thrombosis affecting the jugular foramen.

**Complications of jugular foramen obstruction:** Obstruction or thrombosis of the sigmoid-jugular complex may cause retrograde thrombosis of the inferior petrosal sinus and cavernous sinus (cavernous sinus syndrome — CN III, IV, VI palsies, proptosis, conjunctival oedema). Blockage can also cause raised intracranial pressure leading to seizures and visual obscurations.

### Common Pathologies of the Jugular Foramen

- Paraganglioma (glomus jugulare) — most common primary tumour
- Schwannoma (CN IX, X or XI)
- Meningioma
- [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]] (petrous apex)
- Metastatic tumour
- Inflammatory (chronic otitis media, cholesteatoma)

## PART B: EMBOLIZATION IN HEAD AND NECK — ROLE, INDICATIONS AND TECHNIQUES

### Principle

Endovascular embolization involves the targeted occlusion of tumour vessels or large arteries using a variety of embolic agents delivered via catheter-based techniques under fluoroscopic guidance. It is performed by interventional neuroradiologists, typically in multidisciplinary consultation with the treating surgeon.

### Indications

| Indication | Purpose |
|------------|---------|
| Pre-operative tumour devascularization | Reduce intra-operative blood loss, facilitate complete resection |
| Palliative treatment | Relieve pain, manage haemorrhage, reduce neurological symptoms |
| Emergency haemorrhage | Control tumour haemorrhage or epistaxis |
| Definitive treatment | Vascular malformations, dural AV fistulae |
| Functional testing | Temporary balloon occlusion before planned vessel sacrifice |
| Drug delivery | Selective intra-arterial chemotherapy |

### Tumours Commonly Treated by Embolization

| Commonly Treated | Less Commonly Treated |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| Meningioma | Schwannoma |
| Paraganglioma | Carcinoid |
| Juvenile angiofibroma | Alveolar sarcoma |
| Haemangiopericytoma | Thyroid carcinoma |
| Esthesioneuroblastoma | Capillary haemangioma |

### Embolic Materials

**Temporary agents:**
- Autologous blood clot — target: afferent arteries
- Gelfoam (gelatin sponge) — target: afferent arteries; biodegradable

**Permanent particulate agents:**
- Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles — target: tumour vessels; available in 40-800 micron sizes
- Trisacryl gelatin particles — target: tumour vessels
- Cellulose porous beads — coated with radiocontrast material

**Permanent mechanical agents:**
- Coils (platinum preferred — inert, no MRI magnetization) — target: large arteries/veins; retrievable coils with controlled delivery now standard
- Balloons (latex or silicone) — target: large arteries; for temporary test occlusion or permanent vessel occlusion

**Liquid embolic agents:**
- Ethanol — sclerosant; for vascular malformations
- Sodium tetradecyl sulphate — sclerosant
- N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (NBCA/Histoacryl/Glubran) — adhesive; sets on contact with blood
- Ethylene-vinyl alcohol copolymer (EVAL/Onyx) — polymer; sets after solvent disperses
- Both NBCA and Onyx risk embedding the delivery catheter within vessels — require considerable expertise

### Techniques

**Transarterial embolization:** Most common approach. PVA particles (sized to occlude pathological circulation) or liquid agents (NBCA/Onyx) delivered via microcatheters. Smaller particles penetrate deeper into tumour vasculature but risk passing through AV shunts to the lungs.

**Percutaneous embolization:** Direct puncture injection of liquid embolic or sclerosant into tumour vessels. Particularly useful for recurrent tumours and juvenile angiofibromas.

**Selective intra-arterial chemotherapy:** Endovascular delivery of chemotherapy agents directly into tumours to maximize local dose. RADPLAT (cisplatin) protocols have shown effectiveness in advanced HNSCC.

### Advantages

- Reduces intra-operative blood loss significantly
- Facilitates complete tumour resection
- Shorter operative times
- Pre-operative functional testing identifies patients at risk of stroke before vessel sacrifice
- Allows palliative symptom control in inoperable cases
- Can be curative for certain vascular malformations and AV fistulae

### Disadvantages and Complications

Serious procedural complications occur in approximately **4%** of patients:
- Cranial nerve palsies (more common with very small particles or NBCA)
- Stroke (from unrecognized spread of emboli through ECA-ICA anastomoses)
- Blindness (ophthalmic artery embolization)
- Induced bleeding from vessel perforation or tissue necrosis
- Post-embolization swelling causing exacerbation of symptoms and pain
- Tissue ischaemia and necrosis

## PART C: ASSESSMENT OF PROGNOSIS FOR ICA LIGATION — TEMPORARY BALLOON OCCLUSION TEST

### Indication

Functional testing by temporary balloon occlusion is indicated before planned ICA sacrifice (endovascular ligation or surgical ligation) and may be useful when surgical resections are likely to require ligation of a major artery.

### Protocol

1. Performed under **local anaesthesia** so the patient is accessible for neurological examination
2. **Anticoagulants** are administered
3. A balloon is inflated in the target artery for **20-30 minutes**
4. The effect on cerebral blood flow is assessed throughout

### Assessment Methods for Collateral Blood Flow

| Method | What It Assesses |
|--------|-----------------|
| Digital subtraction angiography | Visualizes collateral pathways directly |
| Doppler ultrasound | Flow velocity changes |
| Xenon CT | Regional cerebral blood flow |
| SPECT | Cerebral perfusion |
| PET scanning | Metabolic cerebral function |

### Provocative Testing

Additional provocative testing improves reliability by lowering the systemic blood pressure or administering a vasodilator drug (e.g. acetazolamide) simultaneously. This tests whether collateral flow is adequate under stress conditions.

### Complication Rates

| Patient Group | Complication Rate |
|---------------|------------------|
| Aneurysm patients | 3% |
| Cancer patients | 10% |
| Skull base tumour patients | 5-20% |

### Permanent Occlusion

After satisfactory temporary occlusion, the artery is permanently occluded by detaching the balloon or replacing it with embolization coils. Permanent occlusion must be performed at the **same arterial level** as the test occlusion.

**Important caveat:** Despite normal test data, there remains a risk that collateral blood flow may be inadequate — preliminary testing reduces but does not eliminate the risk of delayed stroke.

## PART D: ANOMALOUS VASCULAR MALFORMATIONS CAUSING TRACHEO-OESOPHAGEAL SYMPTOMS

It is estimated that 3% of the population have an anomaly of the great vessels, but only a few have symptomatic airway compression. These anomalies are classified into vascular rings (completely encircle trachea and oesophagus) and vascular slings (non-circumferential pressure).

### Vascular Slings

**Aberrant innominate artery (commonest type):**
- Arises further to the left and more posteriorly than usual
- Crosses the anterior surface of the trachea obliquely just above the carina from left inferiorly to right superiorly
- Presents in first year of life with expiratory stridor, cough, recurrent chest infections, reflex apnoea — less severe than vascular rings
- Bronchoscopy shows characteristic sloping, pulsatile compression 1-2 cm above the carina, most marked on the anterolateral aspect; upward bronchoscope pressure obliterates the right radial pulse
- Treatment: aortopexy (suspend vessel anteriorly from sternum) or reimplantation further right on the aortic arch

**Pulmonary artery sling (anomalous left pulmonary artery):**
- Left pulmonary artery arises from the right, passes between the trachea and oesophagus compressing both
- May be associated with lower-end tracheal stenosis
- Treatment: surgical reanastomosis to relieve compression

### Vascular Rings

**Double aortic arch (commonest ring):**
- Ascending aorta divides into two arches (right and left), reuniting posteriorly to form the descending aorta on the left
- Left arch usually smaller than right
- Results in compression of both trachea and oesophagus — stridor, dyspnoea, dysphagia, brassy cough
- Presents earlier in life with more severe symptoms than slings
- Diagnosis: barium swallow (characteristic double impression); echocardiogram confirms
- Treatment: surgical division of the lesser component of the ring

**Right aortic arch with aberrant left subclavian artery:**
- Less common and less constricting ring
- Ring completed by the ligamentum arteriosum passing to the left of the trachea, connecting the descending aorta to the pulmonary trunk
- Treatment: division of ligamentum arteriosum

**Aberrant subclavian artery (dysphagia lusoria):**
- Aberrant right or (rarely) left subclavian artery passing posterior to the oesophagus
- Compresses the oesophagus alone — produces dysphagia but NO stridor

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The jugular foramen is divided by intrajugular processes into pars venosa (lateral — sigmoid-jugular complex) and pars nervosa (medial — CN IX, X, XI + inferior petrosal sinus)
> 2. Vernet's syndrome (jugular foramen syndrome) comprises paralysis of CN IX, X and XI; may lead to retrograde thrombosis causing cavernous sinus syndrome
> 3. The dome of the jugular bulb is intimately related to the hypotympanum, vestibule, posterior SCC, vestibular aqueduct and IAC — the facial nerve lies laterally
> 4. Embolization complications occur in approximately 4% of cases — cranial nerve palsies (more common with small particles or NBCA), stroke, blindness and induced bleeding
> 5. PVA particles and liquid agents (NBCA, Onyx) are used for tumour devascularization; coils and balloons for large artery occlusion; sclerosants for vascular malformations
> 6. Temporary balloon occlusion test for ICA: performed under local anaesthesia for 20-30 minutes with anticoagulation; collateral flow assessed by DSA, Doppler, xenon CT, SPECT or PET
> 7. Cancer patients have a 10% complication rate with ICA test occlusion vs 3% for aneurysm patients; permanent occlusion must be at the same level as test occlusion
> 8. Aberrant innominate artery is the commonest vascular sling — bronchoscopy shows pulsatile anterolateral tracheal compression above the carina; upward pressure obliterates right radial pulse
> 9. Double aortic arch is the commonest vascular ring — diagnosed by barium swallow (double impression) and echocardiogram; treated by surgical division of the lesser arch
> 10. Aberrant subclavian artery passing posterior to the oesophagus (dysphagia lusoria) causes dysphagia but no stridor — it compresses the oesophagus only

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Pre-operative embolization is best performed immediately prior to surgery because devascularization causes tumour swelling that may exacerbate symptoms
> - Smaller particles penetrate deeper into tumour vasculature but risk passing through AV shunts to the lungs — particle size selection is a critical balance
> - Safe embolization demands knowledge of potential ECA-ICA and vertebral artery anastomoses to avoid cerebral or spinal migration of emboli
> - Despite normal temporary balloon occlusion test data, risk of delayed stroke remains — the test reduces but does not eliminate this risk
> - The jugular foramina are asymmetrical (left usually smaller than right) — this is a normal variant and must not be confused with pathological narrowing
> - Paraganglioma (glomus jugulare) is the most common primary tumour of the jugular foramen — pre-operative embolization is routinely performed to reduce vascularity
