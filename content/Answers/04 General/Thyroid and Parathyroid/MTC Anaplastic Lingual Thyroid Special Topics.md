## INTRODUCTION

Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) originates from parafollicular C-cells and accounts for 5-8% of thyroid malignancies. Unlike DTC, MTC does not concentrate iodine, making RAI therapy ineffective. Approximately 25% of MTC cases are hereditary, linked to RET proto-oncogene mutations. Anaplastic thyroid cancer is the most aggressive thyroid malignancy with a median survival of 3-5 months. Lingual thyroid is the commonest ectopic thyroid and results from arrested embryological descent. Thyroid ophthalmopathy is the most significant extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves' disease. Central neck dissection in DTC remains a topic of active debate.

## PART A: LINGUAL THYROID — EMBRYOLOGY, CLINICAL FEATURES AND MANAGEMENT

### Embryology of Thyroid Descent

The thyroid gland develops from two distinct primordia:

**Median anlage:** Originates as a thickening of the endodermal epithelium in the foregut between the 1st and 2nd branchial arches at the base of the tongue, close to the developing myocardium (future foramen caecum). The cells proliferate to form the thyroid bud and then a diverticulum, which expands and migrates from the base of the tongue to lie anterior to the trachea. The track (thyroglossal duct) between the thyroid gland and base of tongue typically disappears by birth.

**Lateral anlagen (ultimobranchial bodies):** Develop from the caudal aspect of the 4th pharyngeal pouch, supplemented by migratory neural crest elements. These fuse with the median anlage as the thyroid descends and contribute the **C-cells (parafollicular cells)**.

### Lingual Thyroid

Lingual thyroid results from the arrest of migration of the median anlage at the base of the tongue. It is the **most common location of ectopic thyroid tissue**. Other possible ectopic sites include sublingual, high cervical, mediastinal, and (rarely) cardiac endothelium.

**Developmental anomalies of thyroid include:**
- Lingual thyroid (commonest ectopic location)
- Thyroglossal duct cysts (60-80% below hyoid; midline or paramedian)
- Hemiagenesis (left lobe absent more common; 5:1 ratio)
- Ectopic mediastinal thyroid

### Clinical Features

Lingual thyroid typically presents as a smooth, rounded mass at the base of the tongue (foramen caecum region). It may cause:
- Dysphagia or sensation of foreign body
- Dysphonia
- Obstructive symptoms (especially if enlarged during puberty, pregnancy, or hypothyroidism)
- Bleeding (if surface ulcerated)
- Often the only functioning thyroid tissue (70% of cases) — hypothyroidism common

### Investigations

- Thyroid function tests (often hypothyroid)
- Neck ultrasound to confirm absence of thyroid gland in normal pre-tracheal position
- Technetium-99m or I-123 scan (confirms ectopic tissue uptake at tongue base; absence of uptake in neck)
- CT/MRI for extent assessment if large

### Management

- **Asymptomatic and euthyroid:** Observation
- **Hypothyroid:** Thyroid hormone replacement — often sufficient to reduce size of lingual thyroid (reduces TSH stimulation)
- **Symptomatic (obstructive):** TSH suppression therapy first; if unsuccessful — surgical excision via transoral approach or transcervical approach; rarely requires lateral pharyngotomy
- **Note:** If lingual thyroid is the only functioning thyroid tissue, excision results in permanent hypothyroidism requiring lifelong thyroxine replacement

## PART B: MEDULLARY THYROID CARCINOMA — GENETICS AND MANAGEMENT

### Pathology

MTC arises from the parafollicular C-cells (neural crest origin via ultimobranchial body). C-cells are concentrated at the junction between the middle and upper thirds of the lateral lobes. MTC produces **calcitonin** (principal tumour marker), CGRP, and CEA.

### RET Proto-Oncogene Mutations

The RET proto-oncogene is located on chromosome **10q11.2** and encodes a transmembrane tyrosine kinase receptor. Activating germline mutations in specific codons of RET cause hereditary MTC and are the basis for genetic screening and prophylactic thyroidectomy.

**Key molecular points:**
- Sporadic MTC: Somatic RET mutations (e.g., codon 918 — M918T) in 40-60% of tumours
- Hereditary MTC: Germline RET mutations — autosomal dominant inheritance
- Genotype-phenotype correlation is well established — specific codons predict aggressiveness and associated features

### Hereditary MTC Syndromes (25% of all MTC)

| Syndrome | RET Codon | Prevalence | Associated Features |
|----------|-----------|------------|---------------------|
| MEN 2A | 634 (most common) | >50% of hereditary | MTC + Phaeochromocytoma (50%) + Hyperparathyroidism (20-30%) |
| MEN 2B | 918 | ~5% of hereditary | MTC + Phaeochromocytoma + Mucosal ganglioneuromas + Marfanoid habitus |
| FMTC | Various | ~35% of hereditary | MTC only; less aggressive; later onset |

**MEN 2A features (most common hereditary form):**
- MTC is the most common malignancy (virtually 100% penetrance)
- Phaeochromocytoma develops in approximately 50% — bilateral in 50-80%
- Hyperparathyroidism in 20-30% (usually multigland hyperplasia)
- Must exclude phaeochromocytoma BEFORE thyroidectomy (hypertensive crisis risk)

**MEN 2B features (most aggressive form):**
- MTC develops earliest and is most aggressive
- Mucosal ganglioneuromas (lips, tongue, eyelids — characteristic bumpy lips)
- Prominent corneal nerves
- Marfanoid habitus (tall, thin, long limbs)
- Intestinal ganglioneuromatosis (constipation, megacolon)
- Phaeochromocytoma in 50%

### Calcitonin as Tumour Marker

Calcitonin is the principal biochemical marker for MTC. Elevated basal calcitonin levels are diagnostic. A calcium stimulation test (IV calcium gluconate infusion) can distinguish MTC from false-positive calcitonin elevation (e.g., C-cell hyperplasia, proton pump inhibitor use, chronic kidney disease).

CEA is also produced by MTC and serves as an additional marker — rising CEA with stable calcitonin may indicate de-differentiation and poorer prognosis.

### Surgical Management of MTC

Surgery is the only curative treatment as MTC does not respond to RAI therapy or TSH suppression:

- **Minimum surgery:** Total thyroidectomy + bilateral central neck dissection (Level VI)
- **If lateral neck disease present:** Add selective lateral neck dissection (Levels II-V)
- **Biochemical cure rates:** 95-100% without lymph node metastases; only **32-45% with lymph node metastases**
- Pre-operative staging: calcitonin, CEA, neck USS, phaeochromocytoma screen (24-hour urinary catecholamines/metanephrines)
- If calcitonin >400 pg/mL → high likelihood of distant metastases → CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and bone scan

### Prophylactic Thyroidectomy in Familial MTC

Prophylactic thyroidectomy is recommended for all carriers of pathogenic RET germline mutations. Timing is determined by the specific codon mutation and its associated risk:

| Risk Category | Codon | Timing of Surgery |
|--------------|-------|-------------------|
| Highest risk | 918 (MEN 2B), 883 | Within first 6 months of life |
| High risk | 634 (MEN 2A) | By age 5 years |
| Moderate risk | Other codons | Can be deferred; guided by calcitonin levels |

**Key principles of prophylactic thyroidectomy:**
- All first-degree relatives of MTC patients should undergo RET mutation testing
- Central neck dissection is included if calcitonin levels are elevated
- Phaeochromocytoma must be excluded and treated FIRST if present
- Long-term follow-up with calcitonin and CEA monitoring

### Targeted Therapy for Advanced MTC

For patients with unresectable locally advanced or metastatic MTC, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting RET and other kinases are available:
- **Vandetanib:** Targets RET, VEGFR, EGFR — FDA-approved for symptomatic/progressive MTC
- **Cabozantinib:** Targets RET, VEGFR2, MET — FDA-approved for progressive MTC

## PART C: ANAPLASTIC THYROID CANCER — CLINICAL FEATURES AND ADVANCES

### Epidemiology

Anaplastic (undifferentiated) thyroid carcinoma is the most lethal thyroid malignancy:
- Accounts for **<2% of thyroid cancers**
- Median age at diagnosis: **65 years**
- Female:Male ratio: **3:2**
- Median survival: **3-5 months**
- **All cases are classified as Stage IV** regardless of extent (AJCC staging)
- Predilection for iodine-deficient regions; overall incidence declining
- Survival rate of approximately 60% at 5 years for poorly differentiated carcinoma (insular carcinoma)

### Clinical Features

Anaplastic thyroid cancer typically presents with:
- **Rapidly enlarging, firm-to-hard neck mass** — often doubling in weeks
- Compressive symptoms: dysphagia, stridor, hoarseness (RLN invasion)
- Skin involvement (redness, warmth — may mimic thyroiditis or abscess)
- Fixation to surrounding structures
- Distant metastases at presentation in approximately 50% (lung, bone, brain)
- May arise in background of pre-existing DTC (de-differentiation) or longstanding goitre

### Pathology

The tumour consists of undifferentiated cells that have lost thyroid-specific markers (thyroglobulin negative, TTF-1 often negative). Diagnosis requires histological confirmation — FNAC may suggest the diagnosis but tissue biopsy (core or open) is often needed for definitive diagnosis due to the undifferentiated nature.

### Management

**Surgery:**
- Limited to diagnosis, airway maintenance, and R0/R1 resection if achievable
- **No debulking surgery** — no survival benefit from incomplete resection
- **[[Laryngectomy Rehabilitation Voice Prosthesis|Total laryngectomy]] should be avoided** — does not improve survival
- If R0/R1 resection achievable → surgery followed by adjuvant chemoradiation

**[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|Radiotherapy]]:**
- IMRT is preferred technique
- Hyperfractionated regimens (60-70 Gy) used in multimodality protocols
- Palliative radiation for local symptom control

**Chemotherapy:**
- Anaplastic thyroid cancer is **not chemosensitive**
- Agents used: taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel), doxorubicin, cisplatin
- Best results seen with combined chemoradiation

**Multimodality approach:** Surgery (if R0/R1 feasible) + EBRT + chemotherapy offers the best chance of prolonged survival, though outcomes remain poor.

**Molecular targeted agents:**
- Showing some promise in clinical trials
- BRAF inhibitors (e.g., dabrafenib + trametinib) — targeting BRAF V600E mutations found in 20-45% of anaplastic carcinomas
- Anti-angiogenic agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors under investigation

### Thyroid Lymphoma (Differential Diagnosis)

Primary thyroid lymphoma should always be considered in the differential diagnosis of a rapidly enlarging thyroid mass, especially in patients with Hashimoto's thyroiditis:

| Feature | MALT Lymphoma | DLBCL |
|---------|--------------|-------|
| Behaviour | Indolent | Aggressive |
| Prognosis | >90% cure rate | Requires systemic treatment |
| Treatment | Local therapy (surgery ± radiation) | CHOP-R + radiation |
| Association | Strong with Hashimoto's | May arise from MALT transformation |

## PART D: THYROID OPHTHALMOPATHY AND ITS MANAGEMENT

### Pathophysiology

Thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy) is clinically apparent in approximately **50% of patients with Graves' disease**. It is an autoimmune inflammatory disorder affecting the orbital tissues:

- **Extraocular muscle swelling** — lymphocytic infiltration and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) accumulation
- **Periorbital fat proliferation** — increased orbital volume
- **Late fibrosis** — restrictive myopathy and permanent changes
- TSH receptor antibodies cross-react with orbital fibroblasts expressing TSH receptors
- **Smoking is a major exacerbating factor**
- **Hypothyroidism also worsens** ophthalmopathy (maintain euthyroid state)

### Two Phases

1. **Dynamic (active) phase:** Inflammation, swelling, progression — amenable to immunosuppressive treatment
2. **Quiescent (burnt-out) phase:** Fibrosis, stable — rehabilitative surgery only

### Clinical Features

- **Lid retraction** (most common sign)
- **Periorbital oedema**
- **Lid lag** (von Graefe's sign)
- **Exophthalmos/proptosis** (up to one-third of patients)
- Diplopia (restrictive myopathy — inferior rectus most commonly affected)
- Conjunctival injection and chemosis
- Exposure keratopathy
- Optic neuropathy (most serious complication — compressive)

### Urgent Referral Criteria

Urgent ophthalmology referral is required for:
- Rapid deterioration of visual acuity
- Cranial nerve palsies
- Visual acuity <6/18
- Decreased colour vision (optic neuropathy)
- Corneal ulceration

### Management

**Mild disease:**
- Artificial tears and lubricants
- Smoking cessation
- Head-of-bed elevation
- Selenium supplementation

**Moderate-to-severe active disease:**
- High-dose intravenous **glucocorticoids** (methylprednisolone pulse therapy — more effective and fewer side effects than oral steroids)
- **Immunosuppressive agents** (mycophenolate, azathioprine)
- **Orbital radiotherapy** (20 Gy in 10 fractions)
- **Rituximab** (anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody) — emerging evidence of efficacy

**Sight-threatening disease (optic neuropathy):**
- Emergency high-dose IV methylprednisolone
- If no response within 1-2 weeks → urgent **orbital decompression surgery**

**Quiescent phase (rehabilitative surgery — sequential approach):**
1. Orbital decompression (if proptosis)
2. Strabismus surgery (if diplopia)
3. Lid surgery (retraction/lengthening)

### Other Extrathyroidal Manifestations of Graves' Disease

- **Pretibial myxoedema (dermopathy):** Occurs in 1-2% of Graves' patients; localized thickening and induration of skin over anterior tibiae; demonstrates **Koebner phenomenon** (develops at sites of trauma)
- **Thyroid acropachy:** Rarest manifestation (<1/1000); clubbing of fingers and toes with subperiosteal new bone formation on X-ray

## PART E: ROLE OF CENTRAL NECK DISSECTION IN DTC

### Anatomical Definition

Central neck dissection (CND) involves clearance of Level VI (pre-laryngeal/Delphian, pre-tracheal, and paratracheal nodes) and Level VII (superior mediastinal nodes) lymph nodes.

### Therapeutic Central Neck Dissection

- **Mandatory** for clinically evident central compartment nodal disease (cN1a)
- Performed at the time of thyroidectomy
- Improves regional control and facilitates accurate staging
- Not controversial

### Prophylactic Central Neck Dissection — The Debate

The role of prophylactic CND (pCND) in the absence of clinically evident central neck disease (cN0) remains one of the most debated topics in thyroid surgery:

**Arguments FOR prophylactic CND:**
- Micrometastases present in 30-60% of cN0 patients (high occult metastasis rate)
- Improves staging accuracy — may upstage patients and guide RAI decision
- Some studies show improved locoregional recurrence rates
- Reduces need for reoperation in the central compartment (revision surgery in Level VI has higher complication rates)
- Decreases post-operative thyroglobulin levels (improved biochemical surveillance)

**Arguments AGAINST prophylactic CND:**
- No proven survival benefit (DTC has excellent prognosis regardless)
- Increased risk of complications: hypoparathyroidism (temporary 14-30% vs 5-10%; permanent 1-4% vs <1%) and RLN injury
- Conflicting evidence — some studies show no outcome improvement
- Micrometastases may not be clinically significant in low-risk patients

### Current Recommendations

Based on BTA and ATA guidelines:

| Clinical Scenario | Recommendation |
|-------------------|----------------|
| Low-risk DTC, <4 cm, cN0 | Prophylactic CND **NOT recommended** |
| T3/T4 tumours (>4 cm or ETE) | Consider prophylactic CND |
| Clinically evident N1a | Therapeutic CND mandatory |
| Clinically evident N1b | Therapeutic lateral ND + ipsilateral CND |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Lingual thyroid is the commonest ectopic thyroid — results from arrested descent of the median anlage; in 70% it is the only functioning thyroid tissue
> 2. MTC arises from C-cells (neural crest origin via ultimobranchial body); produces calcitonin and CEA as tumour markers
> 3. RET proto-oncogene mutations on chromosome 10q11.2 cause hereditary MTC (25%): MEN 2A (codon 634, commonest), MEN 2B (codon 918, most aggressive), FMTC
> 4. MEN 2A: MTC + phaeochromocytoma (50%) + hyperparathyroidism (20-30%); always exclude phaeochromocytoma BEFORE thyroidectomy
> 5. MEN 2B: mucosal ganglioneuromas, Marfanoid habitus, prominent corneal nerves — prophylactic thyroidectomy within first 6 months of life (codon 918)
> 6. Anaplastic thyroid cancer: <2% of thyroid cancers, median survival 3-5 months, ALL staged as Stage IV; surgery limited to R0/R1 resection — no debulking, no total laryngectomy
> 7. Thyroid lymphoma must be excluded in rapidly enlarging thyroid mass — MALT lymphoma has >90% cure rate with local therapy
> 8. Thyroid ophthalmopathy: clinically apparent in 50% of Graves' disease; glycosaminoglycan accumulation + lymphocytic infiltration of extraocular muscles; smoking is a major exacerbating factor
> 9. Central neck dissection is mandatory for clinically evident N1a disease; prophylactic CND is NOT recommended for low-risk DTC <4 cm (cN0)
> 10. Vandetanib and Cabozantinib are FDA-approved TKIs for progressive/unresectable MTC targeting RET pathway


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Calcium stimulation test differentiates true MTC from reactive C-cell hyperplasia — essential when borderline calcitonin elevation is encountered
> - In MEN 2A/2B, phaeochromocytoma must ALWAYS be excluded and treated before thyroidectomy — undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma during surgery can cause lethal hypertensive crisis
> - Biochemical cure in MTC (undetectable calcitonin) is achieved in 95-100% without lymph node metastases but drops to only 32-45% with nodal disease — emphasizing the importance of early detection
> - Thyroid lymphoma and anaplastic carcinoma both present as rapidly enlarging neck masses — core biopsy (not FNAC alone) often required to differentiate; Hashimoto's thyroiditis is a risk factor for both
> - In thyroid eye disease, the sequential rehabilitative surgical approach is critical: decompression first, then strabismus surgery, then lid surgery — reversing this order leads to unpredictable results
> - Prophylactic CND adds morbidity (hypoparathyroidism) without proven survival benefit in low-risk DTC — shared decision-making with the patient is essential
