## Introduction

Wound assessment, appropriate dressing selection and knowledge of suture materials are essential practical competencies for the otolaryngologist. Wound assessment determines the type, depth and condition of the wound, guiding the choice of dressing and closure method. Suture materials vary in their absorption profile, tensile strength and tissue reactivity, and selection depends on the tissue type, site and required duration of support. Surgical cricothyrotomy is a life-saving emergency procedure that every ENT surgeon must be able to perform in a "can't intubate, can't ventilate" (CICV) scenario.

## Part A: Wound Assessment

### Assessment Parameters

Systematic wound assessment involves evaluation of:

1. **Wound size:** Measured by tracing the outline onto transparent acetate sheets; surface area calculated by multiplying the longest diameter in one plane by the longest diameter at right angles

2. **Wound edges:** Indicate aetiology -- venous ulcers have sloping edges, arterial ulcers appear "punched out"; rolled or everted edges mandate biopsy to exclude malignancy

3. **Wound location:** Aids diagnosis -- venous ulcers at the gaiter region/medial malleolus; diabetic foot ulcers over pressure areas (hallux, metatarsal heads, heel)

4. **Wound bed:** Healthy granulation tissue appears pink; dark red and bleeding suggests infection; excess granulation tissue in chronic wounds must be debrided

5. **Wound tissue types:**
   - Granulation tissue: red, moist, shiny, granular
   - Epithelial tissue: pink/white, growing from edges and skin appendages
   - Slough: moist, loose, stringy, typically yellow
   - Eschar: dry, thick, leathery (often black), requires prompt excision

### Assessment Techniques

| Invasive | Non-Invasive |
|---|---|
| Biopsy/histology | Wound depth measurement |
| Arteriography | Wound area measurement |
| Biochemical analysis | Wound volume assessment |
| Tensile strength testing | Thermal imaging |
| | Ultrasound |

### Signs of Wound Infection

- Pyrexia
- Pus
- Spreading erythema
- Malodour
- Wound dehiscence
- Poor healing / increased pain
- Sinus formation

When infection is suspected: obtain wound swab (threshold: 10^5 bacteria/g tissue) and determine antibiotic sensitivities.

## Part B: Wound Dressings

### Principles

An ideal wound dressing sustains a moist wound environment for optimal healing. Modern dressings are semi-permeable to gases (oxygen, CO2, moisture) and impermeable to liquids. Dressing selection depends on wound-related factors (size, depth, exudate, necrotic/viable tissue), product factors and patient factors.

### Five Main Types of Occlusive Dressings

| Type | Properties | Indications |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogels | Hydrate dry wounds; cooling effect; transparent | Dry necrotic wounds; burns; sloughy wounds |
| Hydrocolloids | Absorb exudate; form gel on wound surface; occlusive | Light-medium exudate; dry necrotic wounds; pressure ulcers |
| Foams | Highly absorbent; cushioning; thermal insulation | Deep cavity wounds; moderate-heavy exudate |
| Films | Transparent; semi-permeable; low adherence | Superficial clean dry wounds; secondary dressing |
| Alginates | Derived from seaweed; highly absorbent; haemostatic | Deep exudative wounds; cavity wounds; bleeding wounds |

### Dressings by Wound Type

**Acute wounds:**
- Low-adherence dressings (e.g., Mepore) for skin tears
- Transparent films (e.g., OpSite) for clean, dry wounds

**Necrotic wounds:**
- Wet dressings (saline or hypochlorite)
- Hydrocolloids or hydrogels for dry necrotic wounds
- Surgical/mechanical/biological (larvae) or enzymatic (collagenase) debridement

**Sloughy wounds:**
- Hydrofibres, hydrogels, hydrocolloids
- Polysaccharide dressings (beads/paste)
- Alginate dressings

**Granulating wounds:**
- Light-medium exudate: hydrocolloids, thin foams, hydrogels
- Deep cavity: alginate ribbons, foams, silicone foam, hydrofibers (Aquacel), VAC therapy

**Epithelializing wounds:**
- Dry: hydrocolloids, films, knitted viscose non-adherent dressings
- Exudative (burns/donor sites): paraffin gauze, hydrocolloids, alginates, hydrofibers

**Infected wounds:**
- Antibacterial dressings containing framycetin, fusidic acid, chlorhexidine or iodine
- Honey dressings
- Silver sulfadiazine dressings (Silvadene) or ionic silver hydrofiber dressings (Aquacel-AG)

**Over/hypergranulation tissue:**
- Change from occlusive to non-occlusive dressings
- Light pressure application to wound bed
- Short-term topical steroid application

## Part C: Classification of Suture Materials

### By Absorbability

| Feature | Absorbable | Non-Absorbable |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Lose tensile strength within 60 days | Maintain strength indefinitely |
| Mechanism | Hydrolysis (synthetic) or enzymatic degradation (natural) | Resist degradation |
| Use | Internal tissues; mucosal closures; subcuticular | Skin closure; fascial repair; vascular anastomosis |

### By Origin

| Natural | Synthetic |
|---|---|
| Plain catgut (absorbable) | Polyglycolic acid / PGA (absorbable) |
| Chromic catgut (absorbable) | Polyglactin 910 / Vicryl (absorbable) |
| Silk (non-absorbable) | Polydioxanone / PDS (absorbable) |
| Cotton (non-absorbable) | Poliglecaprone / Monocryl (absorbable) |
| Linen (non-absorbable) | Polyglactin Rapide (absorbable) |
| Stainless steel (non-absorbable) | Nylon / Ethilon (non-absorbable) |
| | Polypropylene / Prolene (non-absorbable) |
| | Polyester / Ethibond (non-absorbable) |
| | PTFE / Gore-Tex (non-absorbable) |

### By Physical Configuration

| Monofilament | Multifilament (Braided) |
|---|---|
| Single strand | Multiple strands woven together |
| Less tissue drag | Greater tissue drag |
| Less infection risk | Higher infection risk (bacterial wicking) |
| More memory/stiffness | Better handling/knot security |
| Examples: Prolene, PDS, Monocryl, Nylon, Steel | Examples: Vicryl, Silk, PGA, Ethibond |

### Commonly Used Sutures in ENT and H&N Surgery

| Suture | Type | Absorption | Common ENT Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vicryl (Polyglactin 910) | Absorbable, braided | 56-70 days | Mucosal closure, deep tissue, thyroidectomy |
| Vicryl Rapide | Absorbable, braided | 42 days (rapid) | Skin closure (children), tonsil fossa |
| PDS (Polydioxanone) | Absorbable, monofilament | 180-210 days | Fascial closure, septal quilting, cartilage suturing |
| Monocryl | Absorbable, monofilament | 90-120 days | Subcuticular skin closure |
| Prolene (Polypropylene) | Non-absorbable, monofilament | Permanent | Skin closure, septal fixation, rhinoplasty |
| Nylon (Ethilon) | Non-absorbable, monofilament | Permanent | Skin closure, microvascular anastomosis (9-0) |
| Silk | Non-absorbable, braided | Slowly absorbed over years | Drain fixation, [[Tracheostomy|tracheostomy]] ties |

## Part D: Surgical Knots

Secure knot tying is fundamental to surgical practice. Three commonly used knots include:

### 1. Square (Reef) Knot

The most commonly used surgical knot. It is formed by two throws in opposite directions (right over left, then left over right, or vice versa). The two strands exit the knot on the same side. It lies flat, has good security and does not tend to slip. Additional throws increase knot security. This is the standard knot for most tissue closures.

```
SQUARE KNOT

First throw:  Right over Left
Second throw: Left over Right

  ___     ___
 |   |   |   |
--+---+---+---+--
     |   |
     |   |
```

### 2. Surgeon's Knot

A modification of the square knot in which the first throw is a double throw (two loops) instead of a single throw. This provides greater friction in the initial throw, holding tissues in apposition while the second throw is placed. It is particularly useful when tying sutures under tension or in deep wounds. The first double throw prevents slipping while the second single throw completes the knot.

### 3. Granny Knot (To Be Avoided)

Formed when both throws are made in the same direction (right over left, then right over left again). The strands exit the knot on opposite sides. This knot is INSECURE and tends to slip or become a slip-knot under tension. It should be recognized and avoided. Understanding the granny knot is important because it is the most common error in knot-tying.

**General principles of surgical knot-tying:**
- A minimum of 3 throws for braided sutures and 4-5 throws for monofilament sutures
- Monofilament sutures are more prone to slipping and require additional throws
- Each throw must be snugged down individually ("flat and square")
- Excessive tails waste suture material and act as foreign bodies
- Hands-tied and instrument-tied techniques should both be mastered

## Part E: Excessive Wound Healing -- Hypertrophic Scars and Keloids

Hypertrophic scars and keloids result from excessive wound healing leading to abnormal fibrotic scar formation through overproduction of fibroblasts, collagen, elastin and proteoglycans. These occur in up to 5-15% of all wounds, with much higher rates in non-Caucasian populations with pigmented skin.

### Hypertrophic Scars

More common in:
- Wounds under tension
- Deep dermal burns
- Wounds healing by secondary intention

Hypertrophic scars remain within the confines of the original wound and tend to improve over time.

### Keloids

A benign fibroproliferative scar that continues to grow beyond the confines of the original wound, invading healthy surrounding tissue. There is a strong genetic predisposition. The magnitude of the injury does not correlate with keloid size, although some form of dermal injury is always associated.

| Feature | Hypertrophic Scar | Keloid |
|---|---|---|
| Extent | Within wound margins | Extends beyond wound |
| Natural history | Tends to improve | Continues to grow |
| Recurrence after excision | Less common | Very common |
| Genetic component | Less prominent | Strong predisposition |
| Common sites | Any site under tension | Earlobes, sternum, shoulders |
| Response to treatment | Often responds | Frequently recurs |

## Part F: Surgical Cricothyrotomy

### Indications

Surgical cricothyrotomy is performed in a "can't intubate, can't ventilate" (CICV) scenario -- when the anaesthetized patient cannot be oxygenated by face mask or laryngeal mask and tracheal intubation is not possible. CICV accounts for 25% of all anaesthetic deaths.

### Anatomy of the Cricothyroid Membrane (CTM)

The CTM is the preferred site for emergency front-of-neck access because it is:

- Superficial and easy to locate
- Present in most patients
- Rarely calcifies
- Relatively avascular
- Wide enough to accept a 6.0 mm tube
- Inferior to the vocal cords
- The cricoid ring holds the airway open
- The posterior lamina of the cricoid protects the back wall

### Three Types of Cricothyrotomy

**1. Needle/small bore cannula cricothyrotomy:**
- 14 G needle (internal diameter 2 mm) inserted through CTM in caudad direction
- Requires high-pressure oxygen (2-4 bar) via jet injector (Sanders/Manujet) for inspiration
- Temporary measure -- provides oxygenation only; does NOT allow adequate gas exchange
- Fatal hypercarbia can ensue if relied upon for prolonged ventilation
- Risk of pulmonary barotrauma if exhalation through upper airway is obstructed

**2. Large cannula cricothyrotomy:**
- Purpose-built cannula with internal diameter >4 mm
- Allows adequate inspiratory gas flows with standard breathing system pressures
- Exhalation of 500 mL takes approximately 5-6 seconds
- No requirement for high-pressure oxygen
- Can use cannula-over-needle or Seldinger-type (Melker) device
- Melker device allows placement of a 6.0 mm cuffed tube

**3. Surgical cricothyrotomy:**

**Technique (standard):**
1. Make a 3 cm midline vertical incision in the skin
2. Make a horizontal stab incision in the inferior part of the CTM
3. Spread the incision horizontally and vertically
4. Insert a 6.0 mm tube

**Rapid four-step technique:**
1. A single horizontal incision through both skin and CTM together
2. Faster than standard (43 vs 134 seconds in cadaver study)
3. Higher complication rate; may cause more haemorrhage

**Complications:**
- Complication rate 40% in one series of 38 emergency cases
- Misplacement of tube (most common)
- Bleeding requiring vessel ligation
- Subcutaneous emphysema
- Posterior tracheal wall perforation

**Post-procedure:**
- Cricothyrotomy tube should NOT be left in place for more than a few days
- Conversion to formal tracheostomy is recommended
- Prolonged cricothyrotomy risks dysphonia and [[Subglottic Stenosis|subglottic stenosis]]

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Wound assessment involves systematic evaluation of wound size (traced on acetate), edges (sloping = venous; punched out = arterial; rolled/everted = suspect malignancy), location, wound bed tissue type (granulation, epithelial, slough, eschar) and signs of infection.
>
> 2. Five main types of occlusive wound dressings are used: hydrogels (dry/necrotic wounds), hydrocolloids (light-moderate exudate), foams (deep cavity/heavy exudate), films (clean dry superficial wounds) and alginates (deep exudative/bleeding wounds).
>
> 3. Suture materials are classified by absorbability (absorbable vs non-absorbable), origin (natural vs synthetic) and physical configuration (monofilament vs multifilament/braided); monofilament sutures have less tissue drag and lower infection risk but require more throws for knot security.
>
> 4. Commonly used sutures in ENT include Vicryl (absorbable braided, mucosal/deep tissue), PDS (absorbable monofilament, fascial/cartilage), Prolene (non-absorbable monofilament, skin/septal) and 9-0 nylon for microvascular anastomosis.
>
> 5. The square (reef) knot is the standard surgical knot formed by two throws in opposite directions; the surgeon's knot uses a double first throw for extra friction when tying under tension; the granny knot (throws in same direction) is insecure and must be avoided.
>
> 6. Excessive wound healing produces hypertrophic scars (remain within wound margins, tend to improve) or keloids (grow beyond wound margins, strong genetic predisposition, commonly affect earlobes/sternum/shoulders in pigmented skin).
>
> 7. Surgical cricothyrotomy is the definitive emergency front-of-neck access procedure in CICV scenarios; the CTM is preferred because it is superficial, avascular, rarely calcifies and is wide enough for a 6.0 mm tube.
>
> 8. Standard surgical cricothyrotomy technique involves a 3 cm midline skin incision followed by horizontal stab through the inferior CTM, spreading of the incision and insertion of a 6.0 mm tube; complication rate is 40% in emergency settings.
>
> 9. A needle cricothyrotomy (14 G, 2 mm) is a temporary measure providing oxygenation only -- it requires high-pressure oxygen (2-4 bar) jet injection and does NOT allow adequate ventilation; fatal hypercarbia can develop if used alone.
>
> 10. Cricothyrotomy tubes should not remain in place for more than a few days; conversion to formal tracheostomy is essential to prevent the complications of dysphonia and subglottic stenosis.


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Rolled or everted wound edges are a red flag for malignancy (squamous cell carcinoma/Marjolin's ulcer) and mandate urgent biopsy regardless of the wound's appearance otherwise.
> - VAC (vacuum-assisted closure) therapy is increasingly used for deep cavity wounds in head and neck surgery, particularly post-[[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] wounds with salivary fistulae.
> - In ENT, Vicryl Rapide is particularly useful for children's skin closure and intraoral mucosal wounds as it dissolves rapidly and negates the need for suture removal.
> - The CICV scenario accounts for 25% of all anaesthetic deaths -- all ENT surgeons must be competent in performing emergency surgical cricothyrotomy as the definitive rescue procedure.
> - The rapid four-step cricothyrotomy technique (single horizontal incision through skin and CTM) is faster (43 vs 134 seconds) but has a higher complication rate -- in practice, use whichever technique you have rehearsed most.
> - The concept of a moist wound environment for optimal healing is now well established -- traditional dry gauze dressings that allow wounds to desiccate are inferior to modern semi-permeable occlusive dressings.
