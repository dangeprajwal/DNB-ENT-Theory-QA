## Introduction

A wound is defined as a break in the integrity of the skin epithelium, often accompanied by concurrent disruption of the underlying dermis. Having a clear understanding of wound healing is fundamental in all aspects of surgery. All wounds heal by a complex, dynamic, highly orchestrated biological process, and a surgeon's success is largely dependent upon uncomplicated successful procession through the different phases of normal wound healing. A wound is considered "healed" when connective tissue repair and complete re-epithelialization have occurred and skin cover has been restored with scar tissue without the necessity of drains or dressings.

---

## Part A: Types of Wound Healing

Wound healing occurs by three mechanisms depending on the nature and timing of wound closure.

### 1. Primary Healing (First Intention)

Healing by first intention is characterized by closure of a wound within 12-24 hours of its formation. These wounds are clean and well perfused, for example following surgical excision or a clean laceration. Wound edges are approximated using sutures, skin glue, steri-strips or other mechanical devices. Apposition of wound edges reduces the distance for epidermal proliferation and results in a hairline scar.

### 2. Secondary Healing (Second Intention)

Healing by secondary intention occurs in large wounds where it is not possible to appose the edges or in wounds complicated by infection. The edges may be gaping and lacerated. Epithelial cells must grow both downwards and across the wound bed, with granulation tissue filling the defect. Myofibroblasts appear around day 3 and play a key role in wound contraction. This process is much slower than primary intention and may lead to contracture formation with potential functional impairment.

### 3. Delayed Primary Healing (Tertiary Intention)

This occurs mainly in contaminated wounds (e.g., following bites, abdominal wounds with peritoneal soiling). The wound edges are left unopposed for 3-4 days, during which host phagocytes are recruited and inflammatory cells destroy contaminating bacteria. The wound edges are then approximated, resulting in a wide scar.

### Superficial (Partial-Thickness) Wound Healing

Occurs in partial-thickness burns, split-thickness skin grafts and abrasions. The basal cell layer often remains intact and dermal regeneration occurs from epithelial cells within dermal appendages (hair follicles and sebaceous glands). Healing occurs by re-epithelialization and is usually complete.

## Part B: Four Phases of Wound Healing

Wound healing occurs in four overlapping stages: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling.

| Phase | Timing | Key Cells | Key Events |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haemostasis | Immediate | Platelets | Vasoconstriction, coagulation cascade, platelet activation/degranulation |
| Inflammation | Days 1-4 | Neutrophils, macrophages | Phagocytosis, cytokine release, debridement |
| Proliferation | Day 3 to week 2-4 | Fibroblasts, keratinocytes, endothelial cells | Re-epithelialization, granulation tissue, angiogenesis |
| Remodelling | Week 1 to months | Fibroblasts, myofibroblasts | Collagen reorganization, scar maturation |

### Phase 1: Haemostasis

Tissue injury disrupts the vascular endothelium, causing extravasation of blood constituents and exposure of the basal lamina. Constriction of endothelial cells and platelet activation ensues. Aggregation, activation and degranulation of platelets releases several important growth factors:

- PDGF (platelet-derived growth factor) -- chemotaxis
- TGF-beta (transforming growth factor-beta) -- ECM deposition
- EGF (epidermal growth factor) -- epithelialization
- VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) -- angiogenesis

Four cascades are initiated: clotting cascade, complement cascade, kinin cascade and plasmin generation. The clot acts as a matrix for subsequent cell migration. Vasoactive mediators released from platelets lead to vasodilatation and increased vascular permeability.

### Phase 2: Inflammation

This phase lasts up to 4 days and is divided into early and late sub-phases.

**Early inflammation (days 1-2):** Activation of the classical and alternative complement cascades leads to neutrophil infiltration within 24-48 hours. Chemoattractants include TGF-beta, complement constituents, bacterial peptide products and ECM protein fragments. Neutrophils adhere to endothelial cells (margination), move through the vessel wall (diapedesis), release proteolytic enzymes to digest bacteria and debris, and form oxygen-free radicals through the myeloperoxidase pathway to sterilize the wound.

**Late inflammation (days 2-3):** Monocytes are attracted by cytokines, complement components and collagen/elastin breakdown products. At the wound site, they differentiate into macrophages -- the most important cell type of the late inflammation stage (48-72 hours). Macrophages are essential for:

- Phagocytosis of bacteria and cell debris
- Release of key cytokines: PDGF, TGF-beta, TGF-alpha, FGF, IL-1
- Formation of ECM by fibroblasts
- Proliferation of smooth muscle cells
- Endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis

Macrophage/monocyte depletion leads to poor wound healing with poor debridement, delayed fibroblast proliferation, delayed ECM synthesis, inadequate angiogenesis and poor fibrosis.

### Phase 3: Proliferation (Day 3 to Week 2-4)

This encompasses re-epithelialization and granulation tissue formation.

**Re-epithelialization:** Involves replication and migration of epidermal cells from wound edges. Increased mitotic activity in basal cells occurs within 12 hours of wounding. Epidermal cells migrate as a sheet by extending lamellipodia ("epiboly"), a process dependent on oxygen tension and most rapid in hyperbaric conditions. "Contact inhibition" prevents further movement when epithelial sheets meet. In partial-thickness wounds, skin appendages act as a source of epithelial cells. During migration, keratinocytes phagocytose foreign particles and slough off non-viable tissue.

**Granulation tissue formation** comprises three processes:

1. **Fibroplasia:** Fibroblasts are attracted 2-4 days after injury by PDGF and TGF-beta. They produce fibronectin, hyaluronan, collagen and proteoglycans to construct the new ECM. Myofibroblasts (phenotypically altered fibroblasts) have capacity for contraction and increased motility. Collagen type III is secreted maximally within 5-7 days of wounding.

2. **Wound contraction:** Begins day 7, peaks at 2 weeks. Reduces defect size at 0.6-0.7 mm/day. In humans, contraction alone rarely completely closes a wound due to skin immobility.

3. **Angiogenesis:** New blood vessel formation commences 2-3 days after wounding. Key steps: degradation of basement membrane, endothelial cell migration, endothelial proliferation into capillary tubes. Important angiogenic factors: VEGF, PDGF, TGF-beta and basic FGF.

### Phase 4: Remodelling and Scar Maturation (Week 1 to Several Weeks/Months)

ECM and collagen fibres are continually synthesized and remodelled. Metalloproteinases (collagenases, gelatinases, stromelysins) are proteolytic enzymes that degrade collagen, controlled by tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). Around 21 days, equilibrium is reached between collagen formation and degradation.

Scar maturation results in breakdown of fibronectin and hyaluronan, larger diameter collagen bundles, reduced metalloproteinases, reduced macrophages and fibroblasts, reduced capillary outgrowth and reduced contractile connective tissue. **The mature scar achieves a maximum of only 80% of the strength of unwounded skin.**

## Part C: Role of Collagen and Cytokines in Wound Healing

### Collagen in Wound Healing

Collagen is the principal structural protein providing structure and strength to healing wounds. Around 18 different types of collagen exist. Key points about collagen in wound healing:

- Collagen type III is the first collagen deposited, secreted maximally 5-7 days after wounding
- Over time, type III collagen is replaced by type I collagen during remodelling
- Collagen synthesis is stimulated by: PDGF, basic FGF, TGF-beta, IL-1 and TNF
- Collagen degradation is performed by metalloproteinases (collagenases, gelatinases, stromelysins)
- Equilibrium between synthesis and degradation is reached at approximately day 21
- Final scar collagen achieves maximum 80% tensile strength of unwounded skin
- Fibronectin aids fibroblast adhesion to ECM, stimulates migration and mediates wound contraction

### Cytokines and Growth Factors

| Cytokine/Growth Factor | Sources | Role in Wound Healing |
|---|---|---|
| TGF-alpha | Macrophages, platelets, epithelial cells | Stimulates epithelial cells and fibroblasts; forms granulation tissue |
| TGF-beta | Platelets, macrophages, fibroblasts, neutrophils, keratinocytes | Stimulates fibroblast and smooth muscle proliferation; chemotactic for macrophages |
| VEGF | Platelets, neutrophils | Stimulates angiogenesis and collateral blood vessel formation |
| PDGF | Platelets, macrophages, endothelial cells | Stimulates fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis and wound contracture |
| FGFs | Fibroblasts, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, macrophages | Chemotactic and mitogenic for fibroblasts and keratinocytes; stimulates angiogenesis |
| IL-1 | Macrophages, lymphocytes | Activates granulocytes and endothelial cells; stimulates haematopoiesis |
| TNF | Macrophages | Stimulates collagen synthesis |

### Major Cell Types and Their Roles

| Cell Type | Role in Wound Healing |
|---|---|
| Platelets | Clot formation; release cytokines (TGF-beta, PDGF, platelet factor-4, beta-thromboglobulin) |
| Neutrophils | First cells to wound site; phagocytosis and intracellular destruction of bacteria |
| Macrophages/monocytes | Phagocytosis; remove debris/necrotic tissue; release cytokines; essential for fibroblast division, collagen synthesis and angiogenesis |
| Lymphocytes | Role unclear; may release some cytokines |
| Fibroblasts | Produce most of the ECM; form granulation tissue; reorganize ECM; wound contracture via myofibroblasts |
| Keratinocytes | Identify and destroy foreign microorganisms; wound re-epithelialization |

## Part D: Factors Affecting Wound Healing

### Local Factors

- **Poor blood supply:** Reduced oxygen and nutrient delivery impairs all phases of healing
- **Infection:** Bacterial count >10^5/g tissue or presence of beta-haemolytic streptococcus prevents healing; prolongs inflammatory phase
- **Increased skin tension:** Wounds under tension heal poorly and form hypertrophic scars
- **Presence of foreign body:** Prolongs inflammatory phase
- **Underlying osteomyelitis:** Persistent infection impairs soft tissue healing
- **Poor venous drainage:** Causes tissue oedema and impaired healing
- **Lymphoedema or venous oedema:** Impairs cellular migration and nutrient delivery
- **Malignant transformation:** Marjolin's ulcer in chronic wounds
- **Peripheral vascular disease:** Tissue ischaemia and hypoxia

### Systemic Factors

- **Increasing age:** Delayed epithelialization; altered inflammatory and cell adhesion molecule profile; reduced collagen content with "fragmented" elastic fibres; reduced tensile strength; reduced inflammatory cells; impaired granulation tissue and angiogenesis
- **Obesity:** Poor vascularity of adipose tissue; increased wound tension
- **Nutritional status:** Protein calorie malnutrition delays healing. Markers: serum albumin (<30 g/L), pre-albumin (<10 mg/dL), serum transferrin (<1.5 g/L). Pro-healing nutrients: arginine, vitamins A and C, zinc
- **Diabetes mellitus:** Increased vascular permeability; impaired oxygen/nutrient diffusion; non-enzymatic glycolization of structural proteins; glycosylated collagen resistance to degradation; decreased granulation tissue, collagen, fibroblasts
- **Hypothyroidism:** Reduced fibroblast function and wound strength
- **Chronic venous insufficiency:** Reduced venous drainage and blood supply
- **Medications:** Steroids (arrest inflammation, interfere with macrophages, angiogenesis and contraction); NSAIDs (reduce collagen synthesis via impaired prostaglandin metabolism); chemotherapeutic agents (reduce wound contraction and fibroblast proliferation); immunosuppressants

## Part E: Classification of Wounds

### By Chronicity

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Acute | Occur secondary to trauma; generally heal well | Thermal, surgical, traumatic wounds |
| Chronic | Persist >6 weeks due to underlying pathology; potential for malignant transformation (Marjolin's ulcer) | Diabetic, pressure, venous, arterial ulcers |

### Surgical Wounds by Level of Contamination

| Class | Type | Description | Wound Closure |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Clean | Made aseptically; no contact with respiratory/alimentary/urinary tract | Primary intention |
| II | Clean-contaminated | Made aseptically; enters respiratory/alimentary/urinary tract | Primary intention |
| III | Contaminated | Open wound or major break in sterile technique; GI spillage | Primary or secondary; requires significant washing |
| IV | Dirty | Dirty/infected; devitalized tissue or pre-existing infection/perforated viscera | Often left open for drainage |

### Acute Wound Types

- **Thermal wounds:** Burns (superficial, partial-thickness, full-thickness), radiation injury, frostbite
- **Surgical wounds:** Incisions, excisions, skin grafts (split/full-thickness), amputations, biopsies
- **Traumatic wounds:** Abrasions, punctures, lacerations, bruises, gunshots, blisters, bites, stab, crush injuries

### Chronic Wound Types

- **Diabetic ulcers:** 15% greater amputation risk; secondary to neuropathy; loss of pain perception
- **Pressure ulcers:** Over bony prominences; Stages I-IV (non-blanching erythema to full-thickness tissue loss exposing muscle/bone)
- **Venous ulcers:** Most common ulcer type; medial malleolus; shallow, large, irregular edges, sloughy base
- **Arterial ulcers:** Lateral malleolus; small, regular, deep (to bone), no granulation tissue; diabetics and smokers

## Part F: Best Practices to Improve Wound Healing

### Pre-operative Optimization

- **Nutritional assessment:** Screen all patients using validated tools (e.g., MUST); supplement with arginine, vitamins A and C, and zinc in malnourished patients; pre-operative nutritional support for moderate-severe malnutrition
- **Glycaemic control:** Optimize blood glucose in diabetics before surgery
- **Smoking cessation:** Advise cessation at least 4 weeks pre-operatively
- **Medication review:** Identify medications that impair healing (steroids, NSAIDs, immunosuppressants, chemotherapeutics); adjust where possible
- **Treat underlying conditions:** Manage hypothyroidism, chronic venous insufficiency, peripheral vascular disease

### Intra-operative Measures

- **Aseptic technique:** Strict adherence to sterile protocols to minimize wound contamination
- **Gentle tissue handling:** Minimize trauma to wound edges; avoid excessive cautery
- **Tension-free closure:** Appose wound edges without excessive tension to reduce hypertrophic scarring
- **Appropriate suture selection:** Match suture material to tissue type and location
- **Adequate haemostasis:** Prevent haematoma formation which acts as nidus for infection
- **Minimal foreign body:** Remove all devitalized tissue; minimize use of foreign materials
- **Adequate blood supply:** Preserve tissue vascularity during dissection

### Post-operative Care

- **Appropriate dressings:** Maintain moist wound environment using suitable dressings
- **Infection surveillance:** Monitor for signs (pyrexia, pus, erythema, malodour, dehiscence, increased pain)
- **Pressure offloading:** Use pressure-relieving devices for at-risk areas
- **Early mobilization:** Encourage mobilization to improve circulation
- **Wound assessment:** Regular evaluation of wound size, edges, bed and tissue type

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. A wound is defined as a break in the integrity of the skin epithelium; wounds heal by three mechanisms -- primary intention (closure within 12-24 hours), secondary intention (granulation and contraction in wounds unable to be closed) and tertiary intention (delayed closure after 3-4 days in contaminated wounds).
>
> 2. Wound healing occurs in four overlapping phases: haemostasis (immediate platelet activation and coagulation), inflammation (days 1-4, neutrophils then macrophages), proliferation (day 3 to week 2-4, re-epithelialization and granulation) and remodelling (week 1 to months).
>
> 3. Macrophages are the most important cell type in late inflammation (48-72 hours) and their depletion leads to poor debridement, delayed fibroblast proliferation, delayed ECM synthesis, inadequate angiogenesis and poor fibrosis.
>
> 4. Key cytokines in wound healing include TGF-beta (fibroblast stimulation, chemotaxis for macrophages), PDGF (fibroblast proliferation, angiogenesis, wound contracture), VEGF (angiogenesis) and FGFs (chemotactic and mitogenic for fibroblasts and keratinocytes).
>
> 5. Collagen type III is secreted maximally within 5-7 days of wounding and is gradually replaced by type I collagen during remodelling; the mature scar achieves only 80% of the tensile strength of unwounded skin.
>
> 6. Wound contraction begins at day 7, peaks at 2 weeks, proceeds at 0.6-0.7 mm/day and is driven by myofibroblasts that arise from phenotypic alteration of fibroblasts under mechanical tension and PDGF influence.
>
> 7. Local factors impairing wound healing include poor blood supply, infection (bacterial count >10^5/g tissue), increased tension, foreign bodies and poor venous drainage; systemic factors include age, malnutrition, diabetes, hypothyroidism and medications (steroids, NSAIDs, chemotherapeutics).
>
> 8. Surgical wounds are classified by contamination level: Class I (clean), Class II (clean-contaminated, enters respiratory/alimentary/urinary tract), Class III (contaminated, major break in sterile technique) and Class IV (dirty/infected with devitalized tissue or pre-existing infection).
>
> 9. Nutritional markers predicting delayed wound healing include serum albumin (<30 g/L), pre-albumin (<10 mg/dL) and serum transferrin (<1.5 g/L); pro-healing nutrients include arginine, vitamins A and C and zinc.
>
> 10. Best practices to improve wound healing include pre-operative nutritional optimization, glycaemic control, smoking cessation, gentle tissue handling, tension-free closure, appropriate suture selection, adequate haemostasis and maintenance of a moist wound environment with appropriate dressings.

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Macrophage depletion experiments have conclusively shown that macrophages are indispensable to wound healing -- no other cell type can compensate for their cytokine production and phagocytic functions.
> - Wound healing by secondary intention is not always undesirable -- concave surfaces of the face (medial canthus, temple, conchal bowl) often heal with excellent cosmetic results by secondary intention.
> - The "bystander effect" of neutrophils explains why clean surgical wounds (Class I) with minimal contamination close the inflammatory phase quickly, while Class IV dirty wounds experience prolonged inflammation that delays subsequent phases.
> - In diabetics, the effect on wound healing is multifactorial -- vascular permeability, non-enzymatic glycolization of structural proteins, glycosylated collagen resistant to degradation and decreased fibroblast function all contribute independently.
> - Remember that epidermal cell migration during re-epithelialization is oxygen-dependent and most rapid in hyperbaric conditions -- this provides the rationale for hyperbaric oxygen therapy in difficult wounds.
> - Steroids arrest wound healing at the inflammatory phase by interfering with macrophages, angiogenesis and wound contraction -- vitamin A can partially reverse steroid-induced impairment of wound healing.
