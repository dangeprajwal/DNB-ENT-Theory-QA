# Endoscopic Ear Surgery

## Questions Covered
- **Q29 (2020):** Discuss the comparative position of endoscopic ear surgery and microscopic ear surgery. Discuss the role of endoscopes in lateral skull base surgery.
- **Q83 (2023):** Describe technique of endoscopic [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]]. Write the advantages and disadvantages of it as compared to microscope.

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology 8th Edition, Volume 2 — Chapter 86 (Endoscopic Ear Surgery)

---

## Part A: Endoscopic vs Microscopic Ear Surgery — Comparative Position (Q29, Q83)

### Historical Context

The operating microscope was introduced to otology in the 1950s and revolutionized middle ear surgery by providing magnification and illumination that had not previously been available. Endoscopes were first used in ear surgery by Mer et al. in 1967 for middle ear examination, but it was not until Thomassin in the 1990s and Tarabichi in 1997 that endoscopic middle ear surgery was truly pioneered as a surgical technique. The past decade has witnessed exponential growth in endoscopic ear surgery (EES) worldwide, driven by improvements in high-definition camera systems, angled endoscopes, and dedicated instrument design. The current consensus is that EES is complementary to microscopic ear surgery (MES) rather than a replacement — the two approaches have distinct strengths, and many surgeons now employ a combined strategy that leverages the advantages of both.

### Head-to-Head Comparison

| Feature | Endoscopic Ear Surgery (EES) | Microscopic Ear Surgery (MES) |
|---|---|---|
| **Vision** | Wide-angle, panoramic field; angled scopes (30, 45, 70 degrees) allow visualization around corners | Line-of-sight only; limited by narrowest point in surgical corridor |
| **Access** | Transcanal in majority of cases; no external incision needed | Often requires post-auricular incision and/or canalplasty |
| **Magnification** | Digital zoom; adjustable on monitor; proximity to target improves detail | Optical zoom; fixed working distance (~200–400 mm) |
| **Illumination** | Light source at endoscope tip — illuminates deep recesses directly | External light source — shadowing in deep recesses behind bony overhangs |
| **Hidden areas** | Excellent visualization of sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, protympanum, hypotympanum, facial recess | Poor visualization of hidden recesses; cannot see around corners without angled mirrors |
| **Hands** | One hand holds endoscope, one for instruments (one-handed surgery) | Both hands free for bimanual surgery |
| **Depth perception** | 2D image on standard systems; 3D emerging but not widely adopted | True 3D stereoscopic vision — inherent advantage |
| **Drilling** | Limited; heat from light source, irrigation needed, scope removed during drilling | Unlimited; standard approach for mastoidectomy and skull base drilling |
| **Mastoidectomy** | Not suitable for standard mastoidectomy | Gold standard — bimanual drilling under 3D vision |
| **Learning curve** | Steep initially (one-handed technique, 2D vision) | Established training paradigm; widely taught in residency |
| **Residual disease** | Reduced residual [[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|cholesteatoma]] — better inspection of hidden recesses | Higher residual rates in hidden areas |
| **Cosmesis** | No external scar (transcanal approach) | Post-auricular scar |
| **Recovery** | Faster; less tissue dissection; reduced post-operative pain | Longer; more tissue disruption from post-auricular approach |
| **Cost** | Endoscope equipment, HD/4K camera system, monitor | Operating microscope (durable, long-lasting, one-time purchase) |
| **Teaching** | Shared visualization on monitor — entire team sees what surgeon sees | Only surgeon and one assistant see through microscope (unless camera attached) |

### Key Anatomical Areas — Endoscopic Superiority

Several "hidden areas" of the middle ear are significantly better visualized with angled endoscopes than with the operating microscope, and understanding these areas is fundamental to appreciating the role of EES.

The **sinus tympani** is the most clinically important of these hidden areas. Located behind the posterior canal wall buttress, it is the commonest site of residual cholesteatoma after canal wall up surgery. A 30 or 45 degree endoscope provides a direct view into the sinus tympani, whereas the microscope offers only indirect visualization, even with the use of angled mirrors.

The **anterior epitympanum (supratubal recess)** lies anterior to the malleus head and is obscured from microscopic view by the anterior buttress. The endoscope visualizes this space directly through a transcanal approach, without requiring drilling or canalplasty.

The **protympanum**, which contains the eustachian tube orifice, is clearly seen endoscopically but is poorly visualized from a microscopic transcanal approach. Similarly, the **hypotympanum** (floor of the middle ear) can be directly inspected endoscopically but offers only limited microscopic visibility, especially inferiorly.

The **facial recess** can be visualized with an angled endoscope through the transcanal route, whereas microscopic access to this area typically requires a posterior tympanotomy performed via a mastoidectomy. The **posterior mesotympanum**, including the ponticulus, subiculum, posterior sinus, and the sinus tympani itself, is excellently displayed endoscopically but is often hidden by the overhanging posterior canal wall when viewed microscopically.

## Part B: Advantages of Endoscopic Ear Surgery (Q29, Q83)

### Visualization Advantages

The most compelling advantage of endoscopic ear surgery lies in its superior visualization of the middle ear and its recesses. Angled endoscopes (30, 45, and 70 degrees) allow the surgeon to see around corners and behind bony overhangs that are invisible to the operating microscope, including the sinus tympani, anterior epitympanum, protympanum, and hypotympanum. The wide-angle panoramic view provided by the endoscope enables the entire tympanic cavity to be seen in a single field without repositioning, and digital zoom on the monitor allows magnification to be adjusted in real time. Visualization of the tympanic membrane margins — particularly the anterior margin of the annulus, which is notoriously difficult to see microscopically — is significantly improved. The ossicular chain and its relationships are clearly demonstrated, facilitating assessment of pathology and planning of reconstruction.

### Surgical Access Advantages

Endoscopic ear surgery is performed almost exclusively through a transcanal approach, making it inherently minimally invasive with no requirement for an external incision in the majority of cases. The wide-angle lens of the endoscope compensates for moderately narrow ear canals, reducing the need for canalplasty that would otherwise be required microscopically. Even anterior tympanic membrane perforations and attic pathology, which frequently demand a post-auricular approach under the microscope, can be managed transcanally with endoscopic visualization. Additionally, the transcanal endoscopic approach lends itself to office-based procedures, including diagnostic otoendoscopy, myringotomy, and grommet insertion.

### Outcome Advantages

Multiple studies have demonstrated reduced residual cholesteatoma rates after endoscopic cholesteatoma surgery compared to microscopic canal wall up mastoidectomy, owing to the ability to directly inspect hidden recesses where disease most commonly persists. Cosmetic outcomes are superior because there is no visible external scar. Patients experience less post-operative pain due to the minimal tissue dissection involved, and recovery is faster with earlier return to work and shorter hospital stays. For simple and moderate procedures such as tympanoplasty and atticotomy, operative times may actually be shorter with the endoscopic approach.

### Educational Advantages

The endoscopic approach offers a transformative advantage in surgical education. Because the entire surgical team views the procedure on a shared monitor simultaneously, trainees see exactly what the operating surgeon sees in real time — a significant improvement over the operating microscope, where only the surgeon and one assistant have a direct view. High-definition video recording provides excellent documentation for teaching, audit, and medicolegal purposes.

## Part C: Disadvantages of Endoscopic Ear Surgery (Q29, Q83)

### Technical Limitations

The most frequently cited disadvantage of endoscopic ear surgery is the constraint of one-handed surgery. The non-dominant hand must hold the endoscope while the dominant hand operates a single instrument, which precludes the bimanual dissection techniques that are fundamental to many microscopic otological procedures. Standard endoscopic systems provide a two-dimensional image on the monitor, lacking the true three-dimensional stereoscopic depth perception inherent to the operating microscope. Although 3D endoscopic systems are emerging, they are not yet widely available or affordable. Endoscope holder systems have been developed to free both hands, but they sacrifice the dynamic repositioning of the scope that is essential for navigating complex anatomy.

### Scope-Related Issues

Several practical issues related to the endoscope itself present challenges. Lens fogging, caused by blood, irrigation fluid, and temperature differences between the scope and the middle ear environment, necessitates frequent cleaning with anti-fog solution. Bleeding is particularly problematic because blood on the lens tip causes immediate and complete loss of visualization, and no second hand is available for simultaneous suctioning. The light source at the tip of the endoscope generates significant heat, creating a risk of thermal injury to middle ear structures — the scope must be kept moving and never left stationary against tissue. Endoscopes are fragile and expensive to repair or replace. The diameter of the scope (typically 3 or 4 mm) occupies significant space within the ear canal, reducing the working room available for instruments, and the scope must be repeatedly removed and reinserted for cleaning, instrument changes, and drilling.

### Procedural Limitations

Endoscopic ear surgery is not suitable for standard mastoidectomy. Safe drilling cannot be performed with an endoscope in the ear canal due to heat generation, the need for continuous irrigation (which compromises the endoscopic view), and bone dust contamination of the lens. Consequently, extensive cholesteatoma requiring complete mastoidectomy necessitates a microscopic or combined (microscope plus endoscope) approach. In lateral skull base surgery, the endoscope is best used as an adjunct rather than as the sole approach. The steep learning curve associated with one-handed technique and two-dimensional vision requires dedicated training, including cadaveric dissection. In some patients with particularly narrow external auditory canals, the canal may be too small to accommodate both the endoscope and instruments, and a canalplasty or post-auricular approach may still be required.

### Other Concerns

Instrument design remains a limitation. Standard micro-instruments were designed for microscopic use, and dedicated endoscopic instruments (curved, angled, ergonomic) are still evolving. The evidence base, while growing rapidly, is still incomplete — most comparative studies of endoscopic versus microscopic outcomes are retrospective and non-randomized, and long-term data continue to accumulate.

## Part D: Technique of Endoscopic Tympanoplasty (Q83)

### Pre-operative Assessment

Before endoscopic tympanoplasty, a thorough pre-operative assessment is essential. Pure tone audiometry is performed to document hearing levels and the air-bone gap. The tympanic membrane perforation is examined to determine its size, location, margins, and the condition of the middle ear mucosa visible through the defect. A CT scan of the temporal bone is obtained when there is suspicion of associated cholesteatoma, ossicular erosion, or recurrent disease. Active infection must be ruled out, as surgery should be performed in a dry ear. The external auditory canal is assessed for patency, curvature, and the presence of exostoses that may affect transcanal access.

### Equipment Required

The standard equipment for endoscopic tympanoplasty comprises rigid Hopkins rod endoscopes in 0 degree and 30 degree angles, with 3 mm diameter and 14 cm length forming the standard otologic set. A high-definition or 4K camera system with a monitor provides the surgical display. Anti-fog solution (FRED or warm saline) is essential for maintaining lens clarity. Standard micro-ear instruments including picks, elevators, curettes, and scissors are required, and dedicated endoscopic ear instruments such as curved suctions and angled picks are advantageous. Monopolar or bipolar diathermy is used for haemostasis. Graft material — most commonly tragal perichondrium with or without cartilage, or temporalis fascia — is selected based on surgeon preference and perforation characteristics.

### Surgical Steps

1. **Anaesthesia and preparation:** General anaesthesia is preferred for a stable, motionless surgical field, though local anaesthesia with sedation is an alternative. Hypotensive anaesthesia is employed when possible to reduce bleeding, which is critical in endoscopic surgery where blood on the lens immediately obscures the view. The patient is positioned supine with the head turned to the contralateral side, and the monitor is placed at the head end opposite the surgeon. Local infiltration using 1% lidocaine with 1:50,000 adrenaline is injected at the four quadrants of the external auditory canal (6, 12, 3, and 9 o'clock positions) to achieve haemostasis and hydro-dissect the canal skin from the underlying bone.

2. **Initial endoscopic examination:** A 0 degree endoscope (3 mm, 14 cm) is inserted and the tympanic membrane is systematically examined, documenting the perforation size, location, and margins (anterior, posterior, subtotal). The handle of malleus and the annulus are identified. Anti-fog solution is applied to the endoscope tip before insertion and reapplied as needed. A 30 degree endoscope is used if needed to visualize the anterior margins or other hidden areas.

3. **Freshening of perforation margins:** Using a sickle knife or micro-curette, the rim of the perforation is de-epithelialized. Epithelium is removed from approximately 2 mm circumferentially around the perforation edge. This step is essential because a raw, vascular bed promotes neo-vascularization and graft uptake. Any squamous debris or tympanosclerotic plaques on the remnant tympanic membrane margin are removed.

4. **Tympanomeatal flap elevation:** A circumferential or limited incision is made approximately 6–8 mm lateral to the annulus, typically from the 6 o'clock to 12 o'clock position to create a posterior-based flap. For anterior perforations, the flap may be extended anteriorly, with the 30 degree endoscope being particularly useful for visualization. The tympanomeatal flap is elevated with micro-elevators in the subperiosteal plane, proceeding from lateral to medial until the bony annulus is reached. The annulus is then separated from the tympanic sulcus with an annulus elevator, and the flap is reflected to expose the middle ear.

5. **Middle ear inspection:** A systematic inspection of middle ear structures is performed using both 0 degree and 30 degree endoscopes. The ossicular chain (malleus, incus, stapes) is assessed for mobility, erosion, and fixation. The round window niche is checked for patency, the eustachian tube orifice is examined via the protympanum, the sinus tympani is inspected using a 30 or 45 degree scope, and the hypotympanum is evaluated for occult pathology. This thorough inspection is a major advantage of the endoscopic approach. If ossicular discontinuity is found, [[Ossiculoplasty CSF Otorrhea Cochleostomy|ossiculoplasty]] is planned either as a primary or staged procedure.

6. **Graft harvesting:** Tragal perichondrium with or without cartilage is the most common graft for endoscopic tympanoplasty. A small incision is made on the posterior surface of the tragus, hidden in the conchal fold, and the perichondrium is harvested with or without underlying cartilage. The cartilage island graft technique uses a thin cartilage disc with perichondrium attached on one side to provide structural support. The cartilage is trimmed to a size slightly larger than the perforation, with a minimum of 2 mm overlap in all directions. Alternative graft materials include temporalis fascia (harvested through a post-auricular approach or small separate incision above the ear) and perichondrium from conchal cartilage.

7. **Graft placement (underlay technique):** Gelfoam is placed in the middle ear cavity to support the graft from below. The graft is positioned medial to the tympanic membrane remnant (underlay position) and placed over the handle of the malleus to ensure contact with the vascular strip. The graft must extend beyond the perforation margins by a minimum of 2 mm in all directions. For anterior perforations, the graft is tucked anterior to the malleus handle and under the anterior annulus remnant — this is a step where endoscopic visualization is far superior to microscopic. A 30 degree endoscope is used to confirm graft position at the anterior margin, which is often invisible microscopically.

8. **Flap repositioning and packing:** The tympanomeatal flap is repositioned over the graft, ensuring that the flap edges approximate with surrounding canal skin without folds or tenting. The external auditory canal is packed with Gelfoam (plain or antibiotic-soaked). Some surgeons use absorbable packing only, omitting ribbon gauze. Because there is no post-auricular wound to close, this step saves significant operative time.

### Post-operative Care

Post-operatively, oral antibiotics are prescribed for 5–7 days, with topical ear drops commenced after pack removal. The patient is instructed to keep the ear dry for 4–6 weeks. The Gelfoam dissolves or is removed at 2–3 weeks. Audiometric assessment is performed at 6–8 weeks post-operatively, and long-term follow-up is arranged to confirm graft integrity and hearing improvement.

### Key Endoscopic Advantages Specific to Tympanoplasty

The endoscope confers several advantages that are particularly relevant to tympanoplasty. For anterior perforations — the most difficult area to visualize microscopically — the endoscope provides direct visualization of the anterior tympanic membrane margin from a transcanal approach. The entire annulus can be visualized without canalplasty even in moderately narrow canals. Graft placement is performed under real-time magnified views of the graft–tympanic membrane–malleus interface, allowing superior verification of graft–annulus overlap. Cartilage graft shaping and precise placement are facilitated by the direct endoscopic view. The avoidance of canalplasty in most cases preserves canal anatomy and reduces operative time. Published success rates for endoscopic tympanoplasty are comparable to those of microscopic tympanoplasty, with graft uptake rates exceeding 85–90%.

## Part E: Role of Endoscopes in Lateral Skull Base Surgery (Q29)

### Concept: Endoscope-Assisted Surgery

In lateral skull base surgery, the endoscope is employed as an adjunct to the operating microscope rather than as a standalone approach — a paradigm termed "endoscope-assisted" surgery. The microscope remains the primary instrument for drilling and major dissection, while the endoscope is introduced at specific stages of the operation to inspect areas that are not visible microscopically. This combined approach (microscope plus endoscope) is increasingly adopted by skull base surgeons worldwide and represents the optimal strategy for maximizing visualization while maintaining the safety of bimanual dissection.

### Specific Applications

**Cholesteatoma surgery (endoscope-assisted CWU mastoidectomy):** After standard microscopic canal wall up mastoidectomy, the endoscope is introduced to inspect hidden areas through the posterior tympanotomy or transcanally. A 30 or 45 degree endoscope directly visualizes the sinus tympani — the commonest site of residual cholesteatoma — and the anterior epitympanum or supratubal recess where matrix may extend beyond the microscopic field of view. Multiple studies have demonstrated significantly reduced residual cholesteatoma rates with endoscope-assisted CWU surgery compared to microscope-only CWU surgery, and this approach reduces the need for planned second-look surgery in some cases.

**Endoscopic second-look surgery:** Rather than performing a full revision mastoidectomy for second-look procedures, the endoscope can be introduced through a small posterior tympanotomy or transcanally — a minimally invasive procedure suitable for office-based or day-case settings. The previously operated middle ear is inspected for residual or recurrent cholesteatoma, and findings correlate well with diffusion-weighted MRI. This approach carries significantly less morbidity than formal revision surgery.

**[[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]] surgery:** In the retrosigmoid (suboccipital) approach, after microscopic tumour removal the endoscope is inserted into the cerebellopontine angle to visualize the lateral end and fundus of the internal auditory canal (IAC) — an area often not directly visible microscopically without additional bone removal. Residual tumour at the lateral IAC fundus, around the transverse and vertical crests, can be identified and addressed. In the middle cranial fossa approach, the endoscope assists in identifying tumour in the lateral IAC. Studies indicate that the endoscope-assisted retrosigmoid approach reduces the need for extensive drilling of the posterior IAC wall and may improve rates of complete tumour removal and hearing preservation.

**[[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]] and tegmen defect repair:** The endoscope provides visualization of tegmen defects from the middle ear side (transcanal or transmastoid), allowing precise identification of the defect site and accurate placement of repair material. It is combined with a transmastoid approach for larger defects.

**Glomus tympanicum:** Small glomus tympanicum tumours confined to the mesotympanum can be removed exclusively through a transcanal endoscopic approach, with excellent visualization of tumour margins on the promontory. Angled endoscopes allow assessment of hypotympanic extension. This avoids mastoidectomy for small tumours.

**Endolymphatic sac surgery:** The endoscope assists in decompression of the endolymphatic sac by helping identify the sac boundaries and confirming adequate decompression after mastoidectomy. An angled endoscope provides visualization of the sac lumen.

**Other applications:** In [[Cochlear Implant|cochlear implant]] surgery, the endoscope confirms electrode position at the round window or cochleostomy site. For petrous apex cholesterol granuloma, endoscopic drainage can be achieved via an infracochlear or infralabyrinthine approach. The endoscope is also valuable for middle ear exploration in cases of congenital ossicular anomalies, temporal bone fractures, and suspected perilymph fistula.

### Limitations of Endoscopes in Lateral Skull Base Surgery

Despite its value as an adjunct, the endoscope cannot replace the operating microscope for major drilling operations — mastoidectomy and skull base bone removal require bimanual technique. The one-handed limitation is particularly problematic in vascular surgery, where simultaneous dissection and haemostasis are critical. The lack of depth perception is a significant concern near vital structures such as the facial nerve, dura, sigmoid sinus, and carotid artery. Heat generated by the endoscope light source poses a risk of thermal injury to neural structures, particularly the facial nerve and cochleovestibular nerve. The need for irrigation during drilling compromises the endoscopic view. For these reasons, the combined microscope-endoscope approach is increasingly recognized as the optimal strategy in lateral skull base surgery, using the strengths of both technologies.
