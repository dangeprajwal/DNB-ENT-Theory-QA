# Recurrent Preauricular Sinus Infection After Surgery

**Question:** *Discuss the embryology, clinical features, and management of recurrent preauricular sinus infection after surgery.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapter 16

---

## INTRODUCTION

Preauricular sinus is a congenital anomaly resulting from defective or incomplete fusion of the hillocks of His during auricular embryogenesis. The sinus tract has a tortuous, branching course in subcutaneous tissues and is adherent to the cartilage of the helix. Surgical excision is the definitive treatment for symptomatic cases, but recurrence following surgery is a recognized complication, primarily due to incomplete excision of the branching tract.

---

## EMBRYOLOGY

### Normal Auricular Development

The pinna develops from six nodular swellings called the hillocks of His, which arise from the first and second pharyngeal arches. Three hillocks are located on either side of the first pharyngeal cleft. These hillocks undergo growth, differentiation and fusion to form the normal pinna.

### Pathogenesis of Preauricular Sinus

Two theories explain the formation of preauricular sinus:

**Primary Theory:** Defective or incomplete fusion of the hillocks of His during auricular embryogenesis leads to epithelial remnants being trapped, forming the sinus tract.

**Alternative Theory:** Isolated or localized folding of ectoderm during auricular embryogenesis creates an invagination that persists as the sinus.

### Inheritance Pattern

| Pattern | Characteristics |
|---------|----------------|
| Sporadic | Generally unilateral |
| Inherited | More likely bilateral |
| Genetic association | Chromosome 8q11.1-q13.3 |

---

## ANATOMY OF PREAURICULAR SINUS

Understanding the anatomy is crucial for successful surgical excision:

**Opening:** Found in front of the helix, typically at the anterior margin of the ascending helix.

**Tract:** Lined with squamous epithelium. The course is tortuous and branching in the subcutaneous tissues, making complete excision challenging.

**Superior Relation:** The tract lies lateral to the temporalis fascia.

**Inferior Relation:** The tract lies lateral to the parotid fascia.

**Terminal Portion:** Adherent to the cartilage of the helix - this attachment must be excised to prevent recurrence.

---

## ASSOCIATED CONDITIONS

### Branchio-Oto-Renal (BOR) Syndrome

This is the most notable syndromic association with preauricular sinus. Features include hearing loss, branchial cleft anomalies, and renal malformations. Screening for renal anomalies is important in appropriate cases.

### Indications for Renal Ultrasonography

Preauricular sinus with any of the following warrants renal ultrasound:
- Maternal history of gestational diabetes
- Family history of ear anomalies or hearing loss
- Associated craniofacial abnormalities
- Associated cardiac abnormalities
- Associated gastrointestinal abnormalities
- Associated limb abnormalities

---

## CLINICAL FEATURES

### Presentation

Most preauricular sinuses remain asymptomatic throughout life. Symptomatic cases present with:

**Discharge:** Intermittent cheesy or mucoid discharge from the pit opening.

**Infection:** Recurrent episodes of cellulitis, abscess formation, and pain around the preauricular region.

**Swelling:** Inflammatory swelling during acute infections.

### Indications for Primary Surgery

The decision to operate is influenced by:
1. Frequency and severity of infective episodes
2. Chronicity of sinus discharge
3. Development of unsightly overlying skin inflammation

---

## MANAGEMENT OF ACUTE INFECTION

**Avoid Incision and Drainage:** This is critical because incision and drainage carries the risk of sinus disruption and seeding of epithelial cells, leading to higher recurrence rates. There is also a risk of facial nerve injury with poorly planned incisions.

**First-Line Treatment:** Intravenous antibiotics to control the acute infection.

**Severe Cases:** Needle aspiration may be performed with microbiology culture of the aspirate to guide antibiotic therapy.

**Timing of Surgery:** Definitive surgery should only be performed when the infection has settled completely.

---

## SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

### Historical Technique (No Longer Recommended)

**Curettage:** This technique is no longer routinely performed due to high recurrence rates and unsightly scarring. It fails to remove the entire tract and its branches.

### Current Surgical Techniques

**1. Microdissection Technique:**
- Relies on identification of sinus extent and branches
- Uses lacrimal probes for tract delineation
- Methylene blue injection to identify the tract and its branches
- Requires meticulous dissection to follow all branches

**2. Supra-auricular Approach (Wide Local Excision) - Preferred:**
- Identify the plane of temporalis fascia
- Dissect soft tissue between this plane and the helix of the pinna
- Remain posterior to parotid fascia (protects facial nerve)
- No formal attempt to identify sinus extent and branches
- Wide local excision of the entire area
- **Advantage:** Lowest recurrence rate

---

## CAUSES OF RECURRENCE

Understanding the causes of recurrence is essential for successful revision surgery:

**Incomplete Excision:** Failure to remove all sinus branches is the most common cause.

**Branching Anatomy:** The tortuous, branching course is not fully appreciated during primary surgery.

**Cartilage Attachment:** The terminal portion adherent to helix cartilage is not excised.

**Curettage:** This obsolete technique is associated with high recurrence rates.

**Sinus Disruption:** Incision and drainage causes seeding of epithelial cells.

**Scarring:** Previous surgery obscures tissue planes, making complete excision difficult.

---

## MANAGEMENT OF RECURRENT INFECTION

### Pre-operative Assessment

- Clinical examination to assess extent of inflammation and scarring
- Review of previous operative notes for technique used and findings at surgery
- Imaging (MRI or CT) may be helpful for complex cases with extensive scarring

### Surgical Principles for Revision Surgery

**Timing:** Operate when infection has completely settled.

**Antibiotic Cover:** Pre-operative and post-operative antibiotics are recommended.

**Technique:** Wide local excision (supra-auricular approach) is mandatory for recurrent cases.

**Facial Nerve Awareness:** Higher risk of injury in revision cases due to altered anatomy and scarring.

**Complete Excision:** Must include cartilage if the tract is attached.

**Extent of Excision:** Must be wider than primary surgery to encompass all residual disease.

---

## FACIAL NERVE CONSIDERATIONS

### Risk Factors for Facial Nerve Injury

- Revision cases (altered anatomy from previous surgery)
- Extensive skin and soft-tissue inflammation
- Deep extension of sinus tract
- Poorly planned incisions

### Prevention Strategies

- Thorough knowledge of anatomy
- Careful dissection technique
- Stay superficial to parotid fascia
- Consider facial nerve monitoring in high-risk cases

---

## PREVENTION OF RECURRENCE

**Correct Surgical Technique:** Wide local excision rather than simple excision or curettage.

**Complete Tract Removal:** Include all branches and cartilage attachment.

**Avoid Incision and Drainage:** Treat acute infections medically to prevent sinus disruption.

**Adequate Timing:** Wait for infection to settle completely before surgery.

**Experienced Surgeon:** Revision cases require expertise in the anatomy and surgical technique.

---

## POST-OPERATIVE CARE

- Keep wound clean and dry
- Continue antibiotics if indicated
- Regular follow-up to monitor for signs of recurrence
- Signs of recurrence: discharge, swelling, inflammation at or near the surgical site
