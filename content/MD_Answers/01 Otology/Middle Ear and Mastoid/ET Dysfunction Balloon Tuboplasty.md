# Eustachian Tube Dysfunction and Balloon Dilatation Eustachian Tuboplasty

## DNB ENT Exam Comprehensive Answer

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, Head & Neck Surgery, 8th Edition — Volume 2, Chapter 86 (Eustachian Tube) and Chapter 83 (Chronic Otitis Media)

**Question Covered:**
- **Q105 (2024):** Discuss the aetiopathogenesis of Eustachian tube dysfunction. Describe the various methods to assess the Eustachian tube function. Discuss the indications and complications of Balloon dilatation Eustachian tuboplasty.

---

## Part A: Anatomy of the Eustachian Tube (Pharyngotympanic Tube / Auditory Tube)

### Basic Dimensions and Orientation

The Eustachian tube (ET) in adults measures approximately 36 mm in total length, oriented at roughly 45 degrees to the horizontal plane as it courses from the middle ear to the nasopharynx. In children, the tube is considerably shorter at approximately 18 mm and lies nearly horizontal at about 10 degrees from the horizontal plane, which is a major factor predisposing children to middle ear disease. The tube is divided into two structurally distinct segments: a posterolateral bony portion comprising the lateral one-third (approximately 12 mm), and an anteromedial cartilaginous portion comprising the medial two-thirds (approximately 24 mm). These two segments meet at the isthmus, the narrowest point of the ET at only 1-2 mm in diameter, which represents a critical bottleneck where pathology significantly impairs ventilation. In children, both portions are proportionally shorter, and the cartilaginous segment is notably more compliant.

### Structural Components

The **bony portion (protympanum)** lies within the petrous temporal bone and constitutes the posterolateral one-third of the tube. Its rigid walls ensure it remains always patent, and it opens into the anterior wall of the middle ear at the protympanum. An important anatomical relationship is the carotid canal, with the internal carotid artery lying inferomedially to the bony ET — a relationship of critical surgical significance.

The **cartilaginous portion** forms the anteromedial two-thirds and is composed of a medial cartilaginous lamina with a superior hook-shaped configuration, and a lateral membranous wall formed by soft tissue. The lateral wall is deficient in cartilage and is the portion that collapses and opens during function. At rest, the cartilaginous ET is normally collapsed; it opens actively during swallowing, yawning, and the Valsalva manoeuvre. The Ostmann fat pad lies lateral to the cartilaginous ET and contributes to passive closure of the tube by exerting gentle medial pressure on the lateral wall.

The **isthmus** lies at the junction between the bony and cartilaginous portions. As the narrowest segment of the entire tube (approximately 1-2 mm in diameter), it represents a critical point where even minor mucosal oedema or pathology can significantly impair ventilatory function.

### Mucosal Lining

The ET is lined by respiratory epithelium — pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with abundant goblet cells. The ciliary beat is directed towards the nasopharynx, serving the crucial mucociliary clearance function. Goblet cell density increases towards the nasopharyngeal end of the tube, and submucosal glands are present particularly in the cartilaginous portion. At the nasopharyngeal orifice, mucosal folds guard the entrance to the tube.

### Musculature of the Eustachian Tube

The **tensor veli palatini (TVP)**, supplied by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (CN V3), is the primary and most important muscle for ET opening. During swallowing, its contraction pulls the lateral membranous wall of the cartilaginous ET laterally and inferiorly, actively opening the lumen. The **levator veli palatini (LVP)**, innervated by the pharyngeal plexus via CN X, elevates the soft palate and may assist ET opening through medial rotation of the cartilage, though its role is secondary. The **salpingopharyngeus**, also supplied by CN X via the pharyngeal plexus, pulls the lateral wall of the ET inferiorly and plays only a minor role in ET opening; it forms the salpingopharyngeal fold. The **tensor tympani**, supplied by CN V3, tenses the tympanic membrane but does not directly open the ET, though it may have a minor protective role.

### Physiology of ET Function — Three Key Functions

The ET serves three essential functions. First, **ventilation and pressure equalization**: the tube equilibrates middle ear pressure with atmospheric pressure through active opening during swallowing, which occurs approximately 1-2 times per minute. Second, **drainage and clearance**: mucociliary transport moves secretions from the middle ear towards the nasopharynx. Third, **protection**: the normally closed state of the ET provides a barrier preventing nasopharyngeal secretions, pathogens, and sound pressure from reaching the middle ear.

### Landmarks at the Nasopharyngeal Orifice

The **torus tubarius** is the cartilaginous elevation formed by the medial end of the ET cartilage, lying posterior and superior to the ET orifice. Posterior to the torus tubarius lies the **fossa of Rosenmuller (pharyngeal recess)**, an important site for [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]]. The **salpingopharyngeal fold** is a mucosal fold extending inferiorly from the torus tubarius containing the salpingopharyngeus muscle, while the **salpingopalatine fold** extends anteroinferiorly from the torus tubarius.

## Part B: Aetiopathogenesis of Eustachian Tube Dysfunction (Q105)

### Classification of ETD — Three Subtypes

| Type | Mechanism | Key Clinical Features |
|------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Dilatory / Obstructive ETD** | Failure of the ET to open adequately; produces negative ME pressure, effusion, retraction | Most common type; ear fullness, muffled hearing, otalgia, conductive hearing loss, retracted TM |
| **Patulous ETD** | Failure of the ET to close; ET remains persistently open | Autophony (hearing own voice/breathing), fullness; TM moves with respiration; improves with head-down position or nasal congestion |
| **Baro-challenge induced ETD** | ET opens normally at ambient pressure but fails to equalize under rapid pressure changes | Ear pain/barotrauma during flying, diving, or rapid altitude changes; normal ET function at sea level |

### Causes of Obstructive ETD

Obstructive ETD, the most common subtype, arises from a wide spectrum of functional, mechanical, inflammatory, and iatrogenic causes that can be organized into several categories.

**Functional (dynamic) causes** are particularly important in children. The immature ET in paediatric patients is shorter, more horizontal, and has more compliant cartilage with reduced efficiency of TVP contraction, all of which predispose to dysfunction. In both adults and children, poor tensor veli palatini contraction from neuromuscular disorders or hypotonia, and high compliance of the cartilaginous ET (floppy cartilage with reduced elastic recoil), represent functional causes of obstruction.

**Mechanical intrinsic causes** involve pathology within the ET lumen itself. Mucosal oedema from upper respiratory tract infections, [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|allergic rhinitis]], or rhinosinusitis narrows or occludes the lumen. Bacterial biofilm formation within the ET leads to chronic inflammation. Polyps at the ET orifice can obstruct the nasopharyngeal opening, while mucosal scarring and fibrosis from prior inflammation or surgery cause cicatrization. Viscid secretions may also cause mucous plugging of the lumen.

**Mechanical extrinsic causes** produce compression or obstruction from outside the ET. Adenoid hypertrophy in children physically obstructs the nasopharyngeal ET orifice and serves as a reservoir of infection. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma or other masses can directly compress or invade the ET orifice — this must be excluded in any adult with unilateral [[OME Pathophysiology|otitis media with effusion]]. Post-[[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] fibrosis from treatment of nasopharyngeal or head and neck malignancy is another important extrinsic cause, as is peritubal lymphoid hypertrophy (Gerlach tonsil hypertrophy) around the ET orifice.

**Craniofacial anomalies** represent a significant category. Cleft palate disrupts the normal decussation and insertion of the TVP and produces abnormal cartilage morphology, resulting in a very high incidence of OME. Down syndrome (Trisomy 21) causes midface hypoplasia, a narrow nasopharynx, and muscular hypotonia. Other craniofacial syndromes associated with ETD include Treacher Collins, Pierre Robin, Apert, and Crouzon syndromes.

**Inflammatory causes** include allergic rhinitis with IgE-mediated mucosal oedema extending to the ET mucosa, gastroesophageal reflux and laryngopharyngeal reflux where pepsin and acid cause peritubal mucosal inflammation, and granulomatous diseases such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and sarcoidosis causing granulomatous inflammation at the ET orifice.

**Iatrogenic causes** include post-adenoidectomy scarring at the nasopharyngeal orifice, post-radiotherapy fibrosis and mucosal atrophy, and post-nasopharyngeal surgery trauma to the ET orifice region.

### Causes of Patulous ETD

Patulous ETD results from conditions that reduce the bulk or tone of peritubal tissues. **Weight loss** is the most recognized cause, leading to loss of the Ostmann fat pad and peritubal tissue volume. **Atrophy of peritubal tissue** occurs with aging and connective tissue disorders. **Pregnancy** affects mucosal turgor through hormonal changes. **Dehydration** reduces mucosal and submucosal tissue volume. **Post-radiotherapy** changes cause tissue atrophy and fibrosis with loss of ET compliance. **Neuromuscular disease** alters muscle tone affecting passive closure. Certain **medications** including diuretics and oral contraceptives have historical associations with patulous ET.

### Pathophysiological Cascade of Obstructive ETD

The following cascade illustrates the progressive consequences of untreated obstructive ETD:

```
Eustachian Tube Dysfunction (failure to open)
         |
         v
Failure of Middle Ear Ventilation
         |
         v
Gas Absorption from Middle Ear (N2, O2 absorbed by mucosal blood vessels)
         |
         v
Negative Middle Ear Pressure (relative vacuum)
         |
         v
Tympanic Membrane Retraction
         |
         v
Transudation of Fluid into Middle Ear
         |
         v
Otitis Media with Effusion (OME / Serous Otitis Media)
         |
         v
Mucosal Metaplasia (goblet cell hyperplasia, glandular proliferation)
         |
         v
Persistent / Chronic Effusion (mucoid — "Glue Ear")
         |
         v
Progressive TM Retraction / Atelectasis
         |
         v
Adhesive Otitis Media / Retraction Pockets
         |
         v
[[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]] Formation (secondary acquired; invagination theory)
```

**Key Concepts in Pathophysiology:**
- **Politzer's hydrops ex vacuo theory:** Negative ME pressure leads to transudation of fluid
- **Middle ear gas exchange:** Normally maintained by ET opening (~1-2 times/minute during swallowing); ME gas composition depends on mucosal gas exchange and ET ventilation
- **Mucosal gas exchange:** Even with intact ET function, the ME mucosa constantly absorbs gas; ET opening replenishes this
- **Critical closing pressure:** The pressure at which the cartilaginous ET collapses; increased in obstructive ETD

## Part C: Assessment of Eustachian Tube Function (Q105)

### I. Symptom-Based Assessment

#### ETDQ-7 (Eustachian Tube Dysfunction Questionnaire — 7 Items)

| Item | Symptom Assessed |
|------|-----------------|
| 1 | Pressure in ears |
| 2 | Pain in ears |
| 3 | Feeling that ears are clogged or underwater |
| 4 | Ear symptoms when having a cold or sinusitis |
| 5 | Crackling or popping sounds in ears |
| 6 | Ringing in ears |
| 7 | Feeling that hearing is muffled |

Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = no problem to 7 = severe problem), yielding a total score range of 7 to 49. A score of 14.5 or greater suggests clinically significant ETD. The ETDQ-7 is a validated patient-reported outcome measure that is useful for diagnosis, treatment monitoring, and research purposes.

### II. Clinical (Bedside) Tests

Several clinical tests can be performed at the bedside to assess ET function. The **Valsalva manoeuvre** involves the patient forcefully exhaling against a pinched nose and closed mouth; a positive result is TM bulging outward seen on otoscopy or tympanometry, with the patient feeling an ear "pop." This demonstrates ET patency under positive nasopharyngeal pressure but is non-physiological, testing only forced opening, and can be negative even with a partially functional ET.

The **Toynbee test** requires the patient to swallow with the nose pinched. The TM initially retracts due to negative pressure generated by swallowing and then returns to neutral, demonstrating physiological ET opening during swallowing. An intact TM is required for observation, and assessment is somewhat subjective.

The **Politzer test** involves insufflating air into one nostril using a Politzer bag while the patient swallows or says "kick." Air entering the middle ear is heard via an auscultation tube (Toynbee tube), or TM movement is observed, confirming ET opening during swallowing. This test is operator-dependent and requires patient cooperation.

The **modified Valsalva (Frenzel manoeuvre)** uses tongue thrust against a closed palate with nose pinched to produce outward TM movement. This is an alternative forced opening test commonly used by divers but is highly technique-dependent.

**Otoscopic examination** provides indirect evidence of ETD through direct visualization of the TM, revealing retraction, air-fluid levels, bubbles behind the TM, effusion, or retraction pockets. **Pneumatic otoscopy (Siegle speculum)** applies positive and negative pressure to the TM; reduced TM mobility suggests effusion or negative ME pressure. Both are static assessments and do not directly test ET function.

### III. Objective / Instrumental Tests

#### A. Tympanometry

| Tympanogram Type | Middle Ear Pressure | Compliance | ETD Significance |
|-----------------|---------------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Type A** | Normal (-50 to +50 daPa) | Normal | Normal ET function |
| **Type C** | Negative (< -100 daPa) | Normal or reduced | Negative ME pressure suggesting obstructive ETD |
| **Type B (flat)** | Cannot be determined | Absent peak | ME effusion (severe ETD) or TM perforation |
| **Type As** | Normal | Reduced | Ossicular fixation (not primarily ETD) |

#### B. Tympanometric ET Function Tests

**For perforated TM or ventilation tubes in situ**, the **Toynbee-Politzer test** is performed by applying +200 daPa through the EAC via tympanometer, then having the patient swallow 3-5 times, followed by repeat tympanometry to measure pressure change. Pressure should equalize (return toward 0) with each swallow if the ET is functional, producing a step-wise reduction in ME pressure. Quantitatively, a good ET equalizes more than 50% of the pressure within 5 swallows, while a poor ET equalizes less than 25%.

**For intact TM**, the **Williams test** involves inflating the middle ear via Valsalva, performing tympanometry to confirm positive ME pressure, having the patient swallow, then repeating tympanometry. A shift of the tympanometric peak toward 0 daPa indicates ET opening and pressure equalization. The more comprehensive **Nine-Step Inflation-Deflation Test (Bluestone)** involves a sequence of: (1) baseline tympanogram, (2) Valsalva, (3) tympanogram, (4) swallow, (5) tympanogram, (6) Toynbee, (7) tympanogram, (8) swallow, (9) tympanogram. Serial peak pressure shifts indicate ET opening ability with both inflation (Valsalva) and deflation (Toynbee) phases assessed.

#### C. Tubomanometry (TMM)

Tubomanometry measures ET function by applying controlled positive pressure to one nostril during provoked ET opening (swallowing), while detecting pressure changes in the middle ear via tympanometry or a microphone in the EAC. It yields an R-value (resistance index) along with opening and closing pressures. A low R-value indicates good ET function, while a high R-value suggests obstructive ETD. Tubomanometry offers the advantages of being quantitative, reproducible, and testing physiological opening with an intact TM, though it requires specialized equipment that is not widely available and has a learning curve.

#### D. Sonotubometry

Sonotubometry delivers a sound source (broadband noise, approximately 6-8 kHz) to the nostril while a sensitive microphone in the ipsilateral EAC detects sound transmitted through the ET when it opens during swallowing or Valsalva. A characteristic sound burst detected in the EAC during ET opening can be measured for amplitude and duration. This non-invasive, physiological test can also assess patulous ET (showing sustained sound transmission), though ambient noise interference, variable results, and limited standardization are its main drawbacks.

#### E. Endoscopic Assessment

Endoscopic assessment of the ET uses either rigid nasopharyngoscopy (0-degree or 30-degree endoscope) or flexible nasopharyngoscopy, the latter being better tolerated and allowing dynamic assessment during swallowing. Key assessment points include the morphology of the ET orifice, torus tubarius, and Rosenmuller fossa; the presence of adenoid tissue, masses, oedema, or scarring; and dynamic opening during swallowing by observing lateral wall movement. In patulous ET, a persistently open orifice with respiratory excursions of the TM is observed. Balloon catheter endoscopy allows direct visualization during the BDET procedure itself.

#### F. Imaging

CT of the temporal bone delineates bony anatomy of the ET, the bony isthmus, middle ear status, tegmen integrity, and mastoid aeration. CT with contrast evaluates nasopharyngeal masses and adenoid size. MRI provides superior soft tissue detail for nasopharyngeal carcinoma staging, peritubal tissue assessment, and cartilaginous ET evaluation. Cone-beam CT offers detailed bony anatomy with a lower radiation dose.

#### Summary: Choosing Assessment Methods

| Clinical Scenario | Recommended Tests |
|-------------------|-------------------|
| Screening / Primary care | Otoscopy, tympanometry, ETDQ-7 |
| Suspected obstructive ETD (intact TM) | Tympanometry (Type C/B), ETDQ-7, Valsalva, Williams test, nasopharyngoscopy |
| Pre-BDET workup | ETDQ-7, tympanometry, tubomanometry, nasopharyngoscopy, CT/MRI to exclude mass |
| Perforated TM / grommet in situ | ET function test through perforation, nasopharyngoscopy |
| Suspected patulous ET | Otoscopy (TM respiratory excursions), sonotubometry, tympanometry during breathing |
| Unilateral OME in adult | Nasopharyngoscopy (MANDATORY to exclude NPC), MRI/CT nasopharynx |

## Part D: Balloon Dilatation Eustachian Tuboplasty (BDET) — Indications (Q105)

### Indications

Balloon dilatation Eustachian tuboplasty is indicated in carefully selected patients with confirmed obstructive ETD. The primary indication is **chronic obstructive ETD failing conservative management**, defined as symptoms persisting for more than 3-6 months despite medical therapy including nasal steroids, antihistamines, decongestants, and treatment of any underlying cause. Objective confirmation of ETD is required, typically through an ETDQ-7 score of 14.5 or greater, abnormal tympanometry (Type C or B), or abnormal tubomanometry.

**Recurrent OME in adults** is an important indication, but only after mandatory exclusion of nasopharyngeal pathology, in patients with persistent or recurrent effusion despite medical management. BDET may also be performed as an **adjunctive procedure alongside [[Tympanoplasty Myringoplasty|tympanoplasty]] or mastoid surgery** to improve middle ear ventilation and optimize surgical outcomes in patients with ETD-related chronic ear disease.

Patients with **failed grommet therapy and persistent ETD** — those with recurrent OME after grommet extrusion who would otherwise require repeated grommet insertions — benefit from BDET as it addresses the underlying ET pathology. **Baro-challenge-induced ETD** in divers, frequent flyers, or aircrew with recurrent barotrauma refractory to medical treatment and auto-inflation techniques is another recognized indication. Finally, **early retraction pockets with ETD** may be considered, as improving ET function may prevent progression to cholesteatoma.

### Contraindications

| Contraindication | Type | Rationale |
|-----------------|------|-----------|
| Nasopharyngeal carcinoma / mass | Absolute | Must be excluded before BDET; biopsy any suspicious lesion |
| Patulous ET | Absolute | BDET will worsen symptoms by further dilating an already patent tube |
| Active nasopharyngeal infection | Relative | Risk of spreading infection; treat first |
| Carotid artery dehiscence / aberrant ICA | Relative | Risk of catastrophic vascular injury; pre-operative CT essential |
| Children (age < 18) | Relative | Limited evidence; some emerging studies show safety and efficacy in select paediatric patients |
| Cleft palate (uncorrected) | Relative | Underlying anatomical cause of ETD may not be addressed by BDET alone |
| Previous nasopharyngeal radiotherapy (severe fibrosis) | Relative | May limit balloon dilation efficacy; increased tissue fragility |

## Part E: BDET — Surgical Technique (Q105)

### Step-by-Step Procedure

1. **Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia (preferred) or sedation with topical anaesthesia (4% lignocaine with oxymetazoline pledgets)
2. **Preparation:** Patient supine with head slightly elevated; nasal cavity and nasopharynx decongested with topical vasoconstrictors
3. **Endoscopic approach:** Transnasal endoscopic approach using a 0-degree or 30-degree rigid nasal endoscope
4. **Identify ET orifice:** Visualize the nasopharyngeal orifice of the ET; identify the torus tubarius posteriorly and the ET opening anteroinferior to it
5. **Introduce balloon catheter:** Insert the balloon catheter system (e.g., Bielefeld balloon / AERA balloon catheter / Acclarent system) through the nostril under endoscopic guidance
6. **Cannulate the ET:** Advance the catheter tip into the ET orifice and into the cartilaginous portion of the ET
7. **Position the balloon:** Advance until the balloon segment lies within the cartilaginous portion of the ET; the balloon does NOT enter the bony portion (critical for safety, as the ICA lies near the bony portion)
8. **Inflate the balloon:** Inflate balloon with saline to **10-12 atmospheres** pressure
9. **Maintain inflation:** Hold inflation for **2 minutes** (120 seconds) — this is the standard dilation time
10. **Deflate and remove:** Deflate the balloon completely; withdraw the catheter under endoscopic vision
11. **Inspect ET orifice:** Endoscopically inspect the ET orifice and surrounding mucosa for mucosal integrity, tears, or bleeding
12. **Concurrent procedures:** May be combined with adenoidectomy, inferior turbinate reduction, septoplasty, FESS, or tympanoplasty as indicated

### Key Technical Points

The balloon dilates only the **cartilaginous portion** of the ET — the membranous lateral wall is stretched and remodelled. The **bony portion is NOT dilated**, which is critical for safety as the internal carotid artery is intimately related to the bony ET. The mechanism of action involves microtrauma to the cartilaginous framework and submucosal tissue, leading to remodelling, fibrosis, and improved luminal patency. Balloon dimensions are typically 16-20 mm in length and 3-5 mm in diameter when inflated. The procedure is typically performed as day-case surgery.

## Part F: BDET — Complications (Q105)

### Complications

The most frequent complication is **mucosal tears and minor bleeding**, which is common but self-limiting. Epistaxis or mucosal abrasion at the ET orifice usually requires no intervention, and nasal packing is rarely needed. **Subcutaneous emphysema** is rare and occurs when air tracks through tissue planes from mucosal disruption; it is typically cervicofacial, self-resolving, and patients should avoid Valsalva post-operatively.

**Iatrogenic patulous ET** from over-dilation is rare but can cause persistent ET patency with autophony and aural fullness; this may resolve spontaneously or require further intervention. **Tympanic membrane perforation** from pressure transmission to the TM during balloon inflation is rare and usually heals spontaneously, though it may require myringoplasty. **Temporary taste disturbance** may occur occasionally from chorda tympani irritation related to pressure transmission through the middle ear; it is typically transient.

**Internal carotid artery injury** is an extremely rare, largely theoretical risk that is minimized because the balloon is confined to the cartilaginous portion; pre-operative CT should identify aberrant ICA or carotid dehiscence. **Negative pressure pulmonary oedema** is extremely rare and may occur if forced respiratory efforts against obstruction happen during the procedure under general anaesthesia. **Infection** (otitis media or nasopharyngeal infection) is rare, and prophylactic antibiotics may be used. **Middle ear barotrauma** with haemotympanum from pressure transmission during balloon inflation is also rare.

**Treatment failure or recurrence** occurs in 15-30% of patients, with incomplete symptom resolution or recurrence of ETD. Such patients may require repeat BDET or alternative management.

### Outcomes and Evidence

Approximately 60-75% of patients show significant improvement in ETDQ-7 scores following BDET, and 50-70% achieve normal (Type A) tympanograms post-procedure. BDET significantly reduces the need for repeat ventilation tube placement. The procedure received FDA approval in **2016** for treatment of persistent ETD in adults, and NICE has issued Interventional Procedures Guidance (IPG) considering BDET safe with adequate short-term efficacy evidence. Emerging data suggest sustained benefit at 1-2 years, though long-term data beyond 5 years are still accumulating. Repeat BDET may be performed in cases of recurrence, with potential for improved outcomes on second dilation. Preliminary evidence also suggests improved tympanoplasty outcomes when BDET is combined with tympanoplasty in patients with ETD.

## Question-Answer Mapping

### Q105 (2024): Aetiopathogenesis of ETD, Assessment, BDET Indications and Complications

**Suggested Answer Structure (15-mark long answer):**

**Introduction (1 mark):**
- Define ETD: Failure of the ET to adequately perform its functions of ventilation, drainage, and protection of the middle ear
- Briefly state the three subtypes: obstructive (most common), patulous, and baro-challenge induced

**Part 1 — Aetiopathogenesis (5 marks):**
- Classify ETD into three types (obstructive, patulous, baro-challenge) with brief definitions (Part B table)
- Detail causes of obstructive ETD: functional, mechanical (intrinsic and extrinsic), craniofacial, inflammatory, iatrogenic (Part B prose)
- Describe the pathophysiological cascade: ETD leads to negative ME pressure, then transudation, then OME, then mucosal metaplasia, then retraction, then cholesteatoma risk (Part B cascade)
- Highlight special situations: children (immature ET), cleft palate (disrupted TVP), unilateral OME in adults (must exclude NPC)

**Part 2 — Assessment of ET Function (5 marks):**
- ETDQ-7 questionnaire: 7 items, score of 14.5 or greater indicates ETD
- Clinical tests: Valsalva, Toynbee, Politzer (prose descriptions with method and interpretation)
- Tympanometry: Type C (negative pressure), Type B (effusion)
- Specific ET function tests: Williams test, nine-step test (intact TM); pressure equalization test (perforated TM)
- Tubomanometry: R-value measurement
- Sonotubometry: Sound transmission through ET
- Nasopharyngoscopy: Direct visualization of ET orifice; MANDATORY in unilateral adult OME
- Imaging: CT/MRI for anatomy and exclusion of nasopharyngeal pathology

**Part 3 — BDET Indications (2 marks):**
- Chronic obstructive ETD failing 3-6 months of conservative management
- Confirmed by ETDQ-7 and objective tests
- Recurrent OME in adults (NPC excluded)
- Failed grommet therapy
- Adjunct to tympanoplasty
- Baro-challenge induced ETD refractory to medical treatment
- Contraindications: NPC, patulous ET, aberrant ICA

**Part 4 — BDET Complications (2 marks):**
- Common: Minor mucosal tears and epistaxis (self-limiting)
- Uncommon: Subcutaneous emphysema, TM perforation, taste disturbance
- Rare/Theoretical: ICA injury, patulous ET, negative pressure pulmonary oedema
- Treatment failure/recurrence in 15-30%
- Outcomes: 60-75% symptom improvement; 50-70% tympanometric normalization; FDA approved 2016

**Conclusion (brief):**
- BDET is a safe, minimally invasive, endoscopic technique for chronic obstructive ETD
- Addresses the underlying ET pathology rather than bypassing it (unlike grommets)
- Growing evidence base supports its use in carefully selected patients
- Pre-operative exclusion of NPC is mandatory
