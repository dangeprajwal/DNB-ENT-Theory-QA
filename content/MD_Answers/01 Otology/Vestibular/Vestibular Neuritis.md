# Vestibular Neuritis

**Question:** *Discuss the clinical features, differential diagnosis, and current therapy of vestibular neuritis.*

Source: Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery, Volume 2, Chapter 66

---

## INTRODUCTION

Vestibular neuritis is characterized by sudden onset of sustained severe dizziness, nausea, sickness, and postural imbalance. The patient typically complains of vertigo (a subjective sensation of rotation or movement) with symptoms worsening with change of position and head movements. The severely affected patient will not be able to walk unsupported and will prefer to lie down on their side, often lying on the affected ear.

The condition is the preferred nomenclature over labyrinthitis, which strictly includes cochlear pathology (hearing loss/[[Tinnitus|tinnitus]]) in addition to vestibular symptoms. In the adult population, around 40% of all dizziness is caused by peripheral vestibular pathology, of which 3-23% are due to vestibular neuritis. The prevalence is approximately 3.5 per 100,000, with typical age of presentation around 40-50 years. There is no gender difference in prevalence, and it is less common in children (1.8% of children presenting with vertigo).

---

## PART A: CLINICAL FEATURES

### Symptoms

The cardinal symptoms of vestibular neuritis include:

- Sudden onset of persistent vertigo (commonly subjective sensation of rotation)
- Severe nausea and vomiting
- Sweating and pallor (autonomic symptoms)
- Postural imbalance - patient unable to walk unsupported
- Symptoms worsen with position change and head movements
- Duration of severe symptoms: 3-7 days followed by gradual spontaneous resolution
- Recovery period: 6-12 weeks due to central vestibular compensation

Important negative features:

- No associated cochlear symptoms (hearing loss or tinnitus)
- No neurological symptoms or signs of brainstem lesion

### Types Based on Nerve Involvement

| Type | Nerve Affected | Percentage | Structures Affected | Clinical Features |
|------|----------------|------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Superior Vestibular Neuritis | Superior vestibular nerve | 82% | Anterior and horizontal SCCs, utricle | More common due to longer, narrower bony canal susceptible to swelling |
| Inferior Vestibular Neuritis | Inferior vestibular nerve | 18% | Posterior SCC, saccule | Spontaneous nystagmus rare; caloric test normal |

### Clinical Signs

#### Spontaneous Peripheral Vestibular Nystagmus

Characteristics of nystagmus in vestibular neuritis:

- Unidirectional (fast phase towards healthy ear)
- Horizontal with torsional component
- Conjugate
- Temporary (subsides within 48 hours with fixation, 1-2 weeks without)
- Enhanced by removal of optic fixation (Frenzel glasses)
- Obeys Alexander's law (increases when looking towards fast phase)
- Can be observed up to 5-10 years with fixation removed

Important: Purely torsional, vertical, or dysconjugate nystagmus indicates central pathology.

#### Other Clinical Examination Findings

- Headthrust test (Head Impulse Test): Positive on affected side with corrective saccades
- Romberg test: Marked increase in sway
- Gait test: Deviation towards the side of lesion
- Unterberger test: Deviation towards affected side (but has poor sensitivity and specificity)
- Hallpike test: Should be performed in non-acute stage to exclude [[BPPV|BPPV]]

### Associated Features

- Visual vertigo: Dizziness provoked by visually busy environments (traffic, crowds, supermarkets)
- BPPV develops as sequel in 10-15% of cases
- Anxiety disorders and panic episodes are common associations
- Recurrence rate is very low (only 1.9% within 39 months)

## PART B: DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

### Conditions to Consider

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Vestibular migraine | Recurrent episodes, migraine history, photo/phonophobia |
| BPPV | Brief episodes triggered by position change, positive Dix-Hallpike |
| [[Menieres Disease and Intratympanic Therapy|Meniere's disease]] | Hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness, recurrent episodes |
| Labyrinthitis | Includes cochlear symptoms (hearing loss, tinnitus) |
| Central causes (stroke) | Neurological symptoms, vertical/torsional nystagmus, negative HINTS |
| [[Vestibular Schwannoma|Vestibular schwannoma]] | Progressive hearing loss, gradual onset |

### Red Flags Requiring Further Investigation

- Sudden onset severe vertigo with hearing loss
- Associated headache
- Neurological symptoms or signs
- Atypical nystagmus (vertical, purely torsional, dysconjugate)
- Recurrent episodes (consider other diagnoses)

### Diagnostic Vestibular Testing

| Test | Findings in Vestibular Neuritis |
|------|--------------------------------|
| Caloric test | Gold standard - reduced or absent response on affected side; 50% show partial canal paresis |
| VNG/ENG | Identifies typical peripheral nystagmus; records eye movements |
| Video Head Impulse Test (VHIT) | Abnormal with vestibular loss >62.5%; normal if <40% deficit |
| o[[VEMP and Menieres Disease|VEMP]] | Abnormal in superior vestibular neuritis (reduced n10 amplitude contralateral to lesion) |
| cVEMP | Abnormal in inferior vestibular neuritis (increased asymmetry ratio, reduced amplitude) |
| Subjective Visual Vertical | Deviation to side of lesion |
| MRI | Enhancement of vestibular nerve; decreased cross-sectional area in affected patients |

## PART C: CURRENT THERAPY

### Acute Phase Management

#### Symptomatic Treatment

Medications for acute symptoms (use only for 3 days maximum):

- Antihistamines (e.g., cinnarizine, cyclizine)
- Anticholinergic agents
- Antidopaminergic agents (e.g., prochlorperazine)
- Fluid replacement in severe cases

Important: Most antiemetics are vestibular sedatives that suppress sensory mismatch required for habituation and compensation. Prolonged use prevents vestibular compensation development.

#### Corticosteroid Therapy

- Oral methylprednisolone has been studied
- Shows significant effect on complete caloric recovery at 1 month
- No significant effect on caloric recovery at 12 months
- No significant difference in symptomatic recovery (Dizziness Handicap Inventory scores)
- Currently insufficient evidence to support routine use considering adverse side effects

#### Antiviral Therapy

- No benefit of antiviral treatment in acute stage has been shown despite possible viral aetiology

### Subacute and Chronic Phase Management

#### Vestibular Rehabilitation

Vestibular rehabilitation is a safe and effective treatment for unilateral vestibular dysfunction:

- Should be started when acute nausea and vomiting have passed
- Compensation develops more quickly if patient is active as soon as possible
- Includes Cawthorne-Cooksey exercises and gait retraining
- Customized exercises for visual vertigo may be beneficial
- Central nervous system needs sensory mismatch stimulus for habituation and compensation

#### Patient Education and Counseling

- Explain benign nature of condition
- Reassure that symptoms will settle
- Inform about fluctuant nature of recovery
- Advise on safety (driving, workplace, fall risk)
- Address anxiety and panic symptoms

#### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy

May be required for patients where anxiety and panic disorders are the main presenting symptoms.

### Natural Recovery

- Majority recover fully without intervention within 6-12 weeks
- 50-70% achieve complete recovery of peripheral vestibular function on caloric testing
- Central compensation mechanisms involve brainstem, cerebellar, cortical, and spinal functions
- Recovery does not follow linear pattern - symptoms often fluctuate
- Some patients (especially elderly and those with CNS disorders) have less effective compensation

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Vestibular neuritis presents with sudden-onset sustained vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and postural imbalance WITHOUT hearing loss or tinnitus
> 2. Superior vestibular nerve is affected in 82% of cases due to longer, narrower bony canal
> 3. Characteristic nystagmus is horizontal-torsional, unidirectional, beating away from affected side, enhanced without fixation
> 4. Caloric test is the gold standard showing reduced or absent response on affected side
> 5. VHIT shows abnormality when vestibular loss exceeds 62.5%
> 6. Symptomatic treatment (vestibular sedatives) should not exceed 3 days to allow compensation
> 7. No proven benefit for corticosteroids or antivirals in routine practice
> 8. Vestibular rehabilitation is safe and effective - should start once acute symptoms settle
> 9. Majority recover within 6-12 weeks through central vestibular compensation
> 10. Recurrence rate is very low (1.9% within 39 months)
> 11. BPPV develops as sequel in 10-15% of cases
> 12. Red flags include hearing loss, neurological symptoms, atypical nystagmus


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> 1. Labyrinthitis includes cochlear symptoms; vestibular neuritis does not - this is a key distinguishing feature
> 2. Vertical or dysconjugate nystagmus indicates central pathology, not vestibular neuritis
> 3. The headthrust test (HIT) is positive on the affected side - a simple bedside test for diagnosis
> 4. Prolonged use of vestibular sedatives actually delays recovery by preventing compensation
> 5. Visual vertigo (discomfort in supermarkets, traffic) is common and may persist after acute phase
> 6. Recurrent episodes should prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses (vestibular migraine, Meniere's disease, BPPV)
