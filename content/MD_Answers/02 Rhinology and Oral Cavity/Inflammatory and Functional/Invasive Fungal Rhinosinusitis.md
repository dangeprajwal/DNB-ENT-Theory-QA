# Invasive [[AFRS Fungal Ball|Fungal Rhinosinusitis]]

**Question:** *Discuss the classification, etiopathogenesis, clinical features and management of invasive fungal rhinosinusitis.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 21 (Fungal Infections)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Fungi can affect the paranasal sinuses through inhalation of spores. Fungi are ubiquitous within the environment but rarely cause disease within the paranasal sinuses, often but not always in the presence of predisposing factors. The spectrum of disease is largely dependent on the immunological competence of the host. Invasive fungal disease is defined as the presence of fungal hyphae within mucosa, submucosa, bone, blood vessels and nerves of the paranasal sinuses, as opposed to non-invasive disease which implies colonization of the surface of epithelial tissues.

---

## CLASSIFICATION OF FUNGAL RHINOSINUSITIS

Fungal rhinosinusitis is classified into three invasive and two non-invasive subgroups:

| Type | Duration | Immune Status | Key Pathogen |
|------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| **AIFR** (acute invasive) | Days to weeks | Immunocompromised | Aspergillus; Zygomycetes (Mucor, Rhizopus) |
| **CIFR** (chronic invasive) | Months to years | Immunocompetent or mildly compromised | Aspergillus flavus/fumigatus |
| **CGIFR** (chronic granulomatous) | Months to years | Immunocompetent | Aspergillus flavus |
| Fungal ball (mycetoma) | Chronic | Immunocompetent | Aspergillus; Pseudallescheria |
| **AFRS** (allergic fungal) | Chronic | Atopic, immunocompetent | Dematiaceous species (Bipolaris, Alternaria, Curvularia) |

---

## PART A: ACUTE INVASIVE FUNGAL RHINOSINUSITIS (AIFR)

### Pathogenesis

AIFR is a rare but rapidly progressive disease that can result in fatality within days. It is the **most lethal** form of fungal sinusitis with mortality rates of at least **50%**. Following inhalation of spores, fungi grow and invade neural and vascular structures, leading to vessel thrombosis and resultant mucosal necrosis. Fungi then extend beyond the affected sinus through bony destruction, perineural and perivascular spread.

### Predisposing Factors

| Factor | Specific Risk |
|--------|-------------|
| Diabetes (especially ketoacidosis) | Zygomycetes thrive: active ketone reductase system in high glucose acidotic conditions |
| Neutropenia | Haematological malignancy, chemotherapy |
| Immunosuppression | Systemic steroids, bone marrow transplant, AIDS |
| Iron overload | Deferoxamine treatment: Rhizopus binds deferoxamine to extract iron for growth |

### Clinical Features

**Clinical hallmark:** Febrile neutropenia and facial pain with or without nasal congestion and orbital signs.

- Symptoms occur rapidly, often within hours
- **Nasal features:** Mucosal discolouration, granulation, ulceration, crusting; **black crusts and sloughing** (pathognomonic); [[Septal Perforation Repair|septal perforation]]
- **Orbital features:** Proptosis, diplopia, visual loss (occur in ~50% of patients); extension to cavernous sinus → CN III, IV, VI palsies → ophthalmoplegia
- **Intracranial features:** Carotid thrombosis → cerebral/brainstem infarction; headaches, neurological deficit, seizures, coma; altered mental status in ~20%
- **Important:** Patients can present with advanced AIFR with orbital and cerebral complications with **few signs of rhinosinusitis** and essentially normal nasoendoscopy

### Diagnosis

| Investigation | Details |
|--------------|---------|
| Nasoendoscopy | Pale mucosa (early); black crusts and necrosis (late); mucosal anaesthesia |
| Biopsy | Multiple sites; especially middle turbinate and septum |
| Frozen section | Sensitivity **84%**, specificity **100%** — provides rapid evidence of invasion |
| Permanent sections (KOH) | Gold standard; identifies invasive features; distinguishes Mucorales from Aspergillus |
| Fungal culture | Sensitivity only 54%; essential for species identification and sensitivity testing |
| CT | Hypoattenuating mucosal thickening, bony erosion, periantral fat thickening |
| MRI | Superior for extra-sinus disease; leptomeningeal enhancement, cavernous sinus thrombosis, cerebral infarction |

### Management

**Three pillars of treatment:**

**1. Reversal of underlying immunosuppression:**
- Control hyperglycaemia and reverse ketoacidosis
- Reversal of neutropenia improves survival
- Role for granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF)

**2. Surgical debridement (endoscopic or open):**
- Aims to confirm diagnosis and remove non-viable tissue
- Debridement until clear, **bleeding margins** are observed
- Little evidence that radical resection (orbital exenteration, radical maxillectomy) improves survival
- Patients undergoing **endoscopic resection had significantly improved survival** compared to open surgery (Turner et al. systematic review) — though this may partly reflect less advanced disease
- Orbital exenteration: reserved for completely non-functioning eye
- **Multidisciplinary approach** utilizing ophthalmology, maxillofacial and neurosurgical expertise is paramount

**3. Broad-spectrum antifungal therapy:**

| Agent | Indication | Dose |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Amphotericin B deoxycholate | First-line (active vs Mucorales AND Aspergillus) | 1-1.5 mg/kg/day IV |
| Liposomal amphotericin B | Safer; less nephrotoxic; may be more effective in cancer patients with mucormycosis | 5-10 mg/kg/day IV |
| Voriconazole | First-line for invasive aspergillosis (IDSA recommendation); NOT reliable against Mucor | 6 mg/kg IV × 2 doses then 4 mg/kg IV q12h |
| Posaconazole | Step-down oral therapy after clinical improvement | Oral |

- Start broad-spectrum antifungal therapy as soon as AIFR is suspected
- Switch from amphotericin to voriconazole once Mucor is excluded (less toxic, more effective for Aspergillus)
- Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) proposed as adjunct but **no clear evidence of efficacy** to date

### Prognosis

- Diabetic patients have **better prognosis** despite more aggressive disease (underlying condition more easily reversed)
- Poor prognostic factors: intracranial involvement, failure to receive surgery
- Mortality remains >50%

## PART B: CHRONIC INVASIVE FUNGAL RHINOSINUSITIS (CIFR)

### Pathogenesis

CIFR has a similar clinical appearance to AIFR but a **slower progressive course** occurring over months to years. It tends to occur in immunocompetent or mildly immunocompromised individuals. Aspergillus species (flavus or fumigatus) or Mucor species are the most common causative organisms.

### Clinical Features

- Subtle, non-specific symptoms initially: unilateral bloody nasal discharge, nasal obstruction, headache, cacosmia, purulent discharge
- Advanced disease: proptosis, orbital apex syndrome, cranial nerve deficits
- Palatal erosion and soft tissue swelling of the cheek (maxillary sinus wall destruction)
- Chronic headache, seizures, focal neurological deficit (cribriform plate/anterior cranial fossa invasion)

### Diagnosis

- **CT:** Hyperattenuating mass with destruction of sinus bony walls — primary differential is malignancy
- **MRI:** Decreased signal intensity on both T1 and T2-weighted images; meningeal enhancement if intracranial spread; cavernous sinus thrombosis: swollen non-enhancing sinus with carotid compression on coronal T1
- **Biopsy:** Shows mucosal invasion; few inflammatory cells (unlike AIFR); lacks granulomas (unlike CGIFR)

### Management

- Surgical resection to clear, bleeding margins
- Systemic amphotericin B until cultures exclude Mucor species
- Azoles (voriconazole, itraconazole) as alternatives — effective orally, easier for long-term treatment
- Prospective randomized study: amphotericin B and itraconazole were equally efficacious for CIFR

## PART C: CHRONIC GRANULOMATOUS INVASIVE FUNGAL SINUSITIS (CGIFR)

### Distinguishing Features

- Rarely seen in the West; more common in **North Africa, Middle East and Asia**
- Caused primarily by **Aspergillus flavus**
- Defined by presence of **non-caseating granulomas with Langhan's type giant cells** and fungal hyphae
- May coexist with other types of fungal sinusitis
- Follows an indolent course; affects both immunocompetent and immunodeficient patients

### Management

- Surgery: resection of involved tissues to bleeding margins
- Post-operative antifungals: oral **itraconazole or voriconazole** (not amphotericin B as first-line since Aspergillus flavus is the causative agent)
- For limited extension: itraconazole or voriconazole
- For advanced disease (brain extension): voriconazole preferred

## PART D: NON-INVASIVE FUNGAL DISEASE (BRIEF NOTE)

### Fungal Ball (Mycetoma)

- Dense mass of hyphae separating easily from sinus mucosa
- Typically immunocompetent patients; no evidence of mucosal invasion
- Maxillary and sphenoid sinuses most commonly affected
- Association with previous dental treatment (56-84%)
- **CT:** Hyperdense on CT, especially maxillary and sphenoid
- **Treatment:** Surgical removal only; antifungals not required

### Allergic Fungal Rhinosinusitis (AFRS)

- Non-invasive fungal disease; first reported by Safirstein (1976)
- More common in younger individuals (mean age 21-33 years); atopic
- **Bent and Kuhn diagnostic criteria** (all 5 major criteria required):
  1. Type I hypersensitivity
  2. [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|Nasal polyposis]]
  3. Characteristic CT findings (double density sign)
  4. Eosinophilic mucin without invasion
  5. Positive fungal stain

- **Clinical features:** Thick, highly viscous mucin ("consistency of peanut butter"); green or black casts; Charcot-Leyden crystals; bony erosion from expansion (not invasion)
- **CT:** Pansinusitis, hyperattenuation with "double density" sign, sinus expansion with wall erosion
- **MRI:** Signal void on T2 (high metal concentration in mucin) — can mimic aerated sinus
- **Management:** [[FESS Steps Complications|FESS]] to remove all antigenic fungal elements + post-operative systemic and topical steroids; [[Allergic Rhinitis Immunotherapy|immunotherapy]] for refractory cases; omalizumab (anti-IgE) for refractory AFRS

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. AIFR is the most lethal form of fungal sinusitis with >50% mortality; clinical hallmark is febrile neutropenia and facial pain
> 2. Frozen section: sensitivity 84%, specificity 100% — provides rapid evidence of invasion vs gold standard permanent sections
> 3. Endoscopic resection showed significantly improved survival compared to open surgery for AIFR (Turner et al. systematic review)
> 4. Zygomycetes thrive in diabetic ketoacidosis (active ketone reductase, high glucose); Aspergillus affects immunosuppressed patients
> 5. Amphotericin B is first-line (covers both Mucorales and Aspergillus); switch to voriconazole once Mucor excluded (IDSA recommendation)
> 6. Deferoxamine increases risk of Rhizopus infection: the fungus binds deferoxamine to extract iron for growth
> 7. Patients can present with advanced AIFR and orbital/cerebral complications with essentially NORMAL nasoendoscopy
> 8. CIFR mimics malignancy on CT; primary differential in most cases; biopsy essential
> 9. CGIFR: more common in North Africa/Middle East; Aspergillus flavus; non-caseating granulomas with Langhan's giant cells
> 10. AFRS: Bent and Kuhn criteria require all 5 major criteria; "double density" sign on CT; signal void on T2 MRI


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - High degree of suspicion required for AIFR in immunocompromised patients with orbital or cerebral signs even without rhinosinusitis symptoms
> - Diabetic patients with AIFR paradoxically have better prognosis than other immunocompromised groups because their underlying condition is more easily reversed
> - Rhizopus specifically uses deferoxamine as an iron-chelating siderophore for growth — stop deferoxamine if Rhizopus is identified
> - Liposomal amphotericin B (5-10 mg/kg/day) is safer and can be used at higher concentrations for longer than conventional amphotericin B deoxycholate
> - Cavernous sinus thrombosis from AIFR can extend to circle of Willis causing cerebral/brainstem infarction — once this occurs, prognosis is very poor
