# Mandible and Mandibular Reconstruction

**Question:** *What are the differences between the young and elderly mandible? Discuss the management of defects following segmental mandibulectomy. Describe the principles of mandibular reconstruction and the available reconstructive options.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 3, Chapter 12 (Oral Cavity Tumours — Management of the Mandible) and Chapter 93 (Reconstructive Microsurgery in Head and Neck Surgery); Volume 1, Chapter 107 (Nasal and Facial Fractures — Mandibular Fractures)

---

## Introduction

The mandible is a parabolic-shaped bone with a complex articulation consisting of paired synovial joints — the temporomandibular joints. Tumours of the mandibular alveolus, floor of mouth, buccal mucosa and retromolar trigone may all involve the mandible. The presence of mandibular involvement has significant consequences regarding management: squamous cell carcinoma invades the mandible either in an invasive or erosive manner. With modern reconstructive techniques, the Andy Gump deformity should no longer be encountered. Segmental mandibular resection and composite microvascular free-flap reconstruction is the standard of care for extensive mandibular defects, while rim resection maintains bony continuity and is preferred when oncologically acceptable.

---

## PART A: Differences Between the Young and Elderly Mandible

### Anatomy and Structure

| Feature | Young (Dentate) Mandible | Elderly (Edentulous) Mandible |
|---|---|---|
| Alveolar process | Well-developed; supports dentition | Resorbed; thin alveolar ridge |
| Bone height | Full height with alveolar process | Reduced — significant resorption |
| Bone density | Dense cortical and cancellous bone | Osteoporotic, thin cortical bone |
| Inferior alveolar canal | Located within the body | Closer to the crest (near surface) |
| Mental foramen | Located below premolar roots | Closer to the alveolar crest |
| Teeth | Present; guide occlusion | Absent; loss of occlusal guidance |
| Mucoperiosteum | Firmly attached alveolar mucosa | Thin strip on crest of mandible |
| Blood supply | Rich from alveolar, periosteal sources | Reduced; relies more on periosteum |

### Key Differences in Pathology and Surgery

**In the dentate (young) mandible:**
- Tumours enter the mandible at the junction of the attached and reflected mucosa, whether dentate or edentulous
- The mandible should be considered at risk at any point where tumour contacts bone
- Teeth in the line of fracture or resection must be extracted
- The inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle lies deep within the body of the mandible
- Rim resection is safer due to adequate residual bone height

**In the edentulous (elderly) mandible:**
- Following loss of the dentition, considerable resorption of the alveolar process occurs
- Only a thin strip of attached mucoperiosteum remains on the crest of the mandible between the floor of mouth and buccal mucosa
- Every effort should be made to preserve the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle during marginal resection to minimize the risk of avascular necrosis and iatrogenic fracture
- If the residual mandibular depth following rim resection would be less than 10 mm, conversion to segmental resection should be considered
- The mandibular angles may be weakened by unerupted wisdom teeth (in young patients), the parasymphysis by the long root of the lower canine, and the condylar neck by its slender anatomy
- Points of weakness where bone is relatively thin determine fracture patterns

## PART B: Patterns of Mandibular Invasion and Assessment

### Patterns of Invasion

| Pattern | Characteristics | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Erosive | Broad advancing front, osteoclast activity, connective tissue between tumour and bone | More common in early involvement |
| Invasive | Fingers/islands of tumour advancing deeply into bone, no obvious osteoclastic activity | Associated with cervical metastasis with ECS; more aggressive |

Large, deeply invading tumours are more likely to demonstrate an invasive pattern. As depth of invasion increases, erosive tumours may become more invasive in character.

### Assessment of Mandibular Involvement

- **OPG radiograph** — should be requested for all oral cancer patients; useful for demonstrating bony invasion, mandibular height, dental anatomy and pathology; sensitivity limited as plain X-rays do not detect invasion until 30% demineralization has occurred
- **MRI (T1 and STIR sequences)** — very sensitive for imaging the primary site with adequate specificity; axial views are most useful
- **CT** — high specificity but low sensitivity for bone invasion (especially retromolar tumours)
- **SPECT** — may have up to 100% sensitivity for determining bone invasion; useful in equivocal cases
- **Periosteal stripping** — intraoperative assessment; looking for cortical bone disruption when imaging is equivocal

Clinical examination and OPG alone are inadequate for accurate assessment of mandibular invasion. Clinical fixation of the tumour to bone is not necessary for bone invasion to occur.

## PART C: Principles of Mandibular Resection

### Rim Resection (Marginal Mandibulectomy)

**Indications:**
- T1/T2 tumours with early invasion (<5 mm) and adequate mandibular bone height
- Pre-operative imaging, clinical examination and periosteal stripping show no significant bone involvement
- No prior [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] to the mandible

**Technique:**
- 1 cm margin of bone should be achieved
- Anterior and posterior margins should be curved to minimize risk of iatrogenic fracture
- Rounding of edges minimizes risk of bone exposure and pedicle trauma
- If residual mandibular depth is <10 mm, convert to segmental resection
- Traditionally conducted horizontally; however, understanding that tumour may enter at any point of contact means cuts should be angled accordingly
- Retromolar rim resections may include the coronoid process
- In the edentulous mandible, preserve the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle

**Advantages:** Maintains bony continuity; preserves lower lip sensation; preserves muscular attachments; excellent functional and cosmetic outcome. When oncologically sound, rim resection should be the resection of choice.

### Segmental Mandibulectomy

**Indications:**
- Extensive mandibular invasion or deeply invading soft-tissue tumours abutting the full depth of the mandible
- Previously irradiated mandible
- Involvement of the inferior dental nerve or intraosseous tumours
- Residual mandibular depth <10 mm after planned rim resection
- Large soft tissue tumours requiring mandibulotomy combined with marginal resection (increased ORN risk — segmental resection warranted)

**Principles:**
- 1 cm margin of soft tissue; it is unusual to get a positive bone margin in the absence of a positive soft-tissue margin
- Preferential spread within the mandible via the inferior alveolar nerve or medullary space is rare, justifying 1 cm bone margins
- Bony resection should be dictated by the extent of soft tissue disease
- The presence of bone involvement (rather than depth of invasion) is the main determinant of long-term prognosis

### Defect Classification

The H, C, L, o, m, s classification system accurately describes mandibular surgical defects and helps plan reconstruction:
- **H** = hemimandibulectomy (including condyle)
- **C** = central segment (includes both canines)
- **L** = lateral segment (up to and including canine)
- **o** = orbit
- **m** = mucosa/soft tissue
- **s** = skin

## PART D: Mandibular Reconstruction Options

### Reconstruction Plates Alone

- Small, lateral mandibular defects may be reconstructed with a reconstruction plate and soft-tissue flap only
- Central defects or larger lateral defects should be reconstructed with vascularized bone
- Reconstruction plates alone are particularly prone to failure in the retromolar region

### Vascularized Bone Flaps

Since radiotherapy will almost certainly be indicated following segmental resection, vascularized bone flaps result in more predictable healing with lower risk of non-union or resorption.

| Flap | Pedicle | Bone Length | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fibula | Peroneal artery | 18–24 cm | Current benchmark; can reconstruct entire mandible |
| DCIA (iliac crest) | Deep circumflex iliac artery | Variable | Excellent bone stock; ideal vertical height |
| Scapula | Circumflex scapular artery | Variable | Versatile; dual skin paddles possible |
| Radial forearm (osseous) | Radial artery | Short | Limited role; secondary option only |

### Free Fibula Flap (Current Standard)

- First described by Taylor for tibial pseudarthrosis; popularized by Hidalgo and Rekow for mandibular reconstruction
- Based on the peroneal artery and venae comitantes
- Provides 18–24 cm of bone — sufficient to reconstruct almost the entire mandible
- Can be osteotomized into multiple segments to recreate mandibular shape
- Skin paddle (12–16 cm x 8–10 cm) of appropriate thickness for oral cavity reconstruction
- 'Double-barrelling' — one segment stacked on another — may maximize vertical height for secondary osseointegration
- Pedicle: artery 3–4 mm, vein 4–5 mm diameter
- At least 6 cm of fibula at the ankle and knee must be preserved to avoid instability and common peroneal nerve injury
- Contraindicated in peripheral vascular disease
- **Pre-operative assessment:** Non-invasive vascular imaging (colour Doppler, MRA or CTA) of the leg; Doppler of perforating branches for skin island planning

### DCIA Flap (Iliac Crest)

- Based on the deep circumflex iliac artery arising from the lateral external iliac artery
- Ideal for hemimandibular reconstruction — excellent bone stock for both body and ramus
- Superior corticocancellous proportions and vertical height for osseointegration
- The myo-osseous version (with internal oblique) is preferred over the osseocutaneous version due to unreliable skin paddle
- Pedicle: artery and vein 2–3 mm, pedicle length 6–8 cm
- **Disadvantages:** Donor site morbidity (hernia risk, chronic abdominal wall laxity); painful; difficult mobilization in elderly; technically demanding harvest

### Scapula Osteocutaneous Flap

- Versatile — provides skin, muscle, bone or combinations
- In thin individuals, can reconstruct extensive defects involving mandible, soft palate, tongue base and buccal mucosa with a single flap
- Dual skin paddles possible — one for mucosal surface, one for external skin
- An excellent option for complex defects combining bone loss with large soft-tissue requirements

### CAD/CAM (Computer-Aided Design/Manufacturing)

Computer-aided planning is invaluable for complex bony reconstructions, allowing 3D printing of cutting guides, patient-specific implants and pre-bent reconstruction plates for optimal cosmetic and functional outcomes.

## PART E: Comparison — Rim vs Segmental Resection

| Feature | Rim Resection | Segmental Resection |
|---|---|---|
| Bony continuity | Maintained | Disrupted |
| Lower lip sensation | Preserved | Usually lost |
| Reconstruction | Primary closure or soft tissue | Composite [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]] |
| Occlusion | Maintained | Requires rehabilitation |
| Andy Gump deformity | No | Avoided with free flap |
| Prognosis | No difference if guidelines followed | No difference if guidelines followed |
| QOL difference | Slightly better | Comparable with microvascular reconstruction (especially defects >4 cm) |

Long-term prognosis and recurrence do not differ between rim and segmental resection when oncological guidelines are followed. Local recurrence following bony resection is usually a consequence of soft-tissue margin status rather than the method of mandibular resection. It has been proposed that only tumours with an invasive tumour front (as opposed to erosive) should be classified as T4.

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. SCC invades the mandible by either invasive or erosive patterns — invasive tumours are more likely to give rise to cervical metastasis with extracapsular extension
> 2. Tumours enter the mandible at the point of contact, usually at the junction of attached and reflected mucosa — the mandible is at risk at any point where tumour contacts bone
> 3. Preferential spread via the inferior alveolar nerve or medullary space is rare, justifying 1 cm bone margins
> 4. Rim resection should be performed when oncologically acceptable — it maintains bony continuity, sensation and function
> 5. If residual mandibular depth following rim resection would be <10 mm, convert to segmental resection
> 6. The edentulous mandible undergoes significant alveolar resorption; the inferior alveolar neurovascular bundle must be preserved during marginal resection to prevent avascular necrosis
> 7. The free fibula flap is the current benchmark for composite mandibular reconstruction — provides up to 24 cm of bone and can be osteotomized into multiple segments
> 8. Vascularized bone flaps should be used for segmental defects, especially if post-operative radiotherapy is planned — superior healing with lower risk of non-union
> 9. The H, C, L, o, m, s classification system accurately describes mandibular defects and facilitates reconstructive planning
> 10. Prognosis is determined by the presence of bone involvement (not depth of invasion) and soft-tissue margin status, not the method of mandibular resection


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - MRI (T1 and STIR sequences) is very sensitive for imaging mandibular invasion, but bone scintigraphy/SPECT may have up to 100% sensitivity in equivocal cases — these modalities are complementary
> - The combination of mandibulotomy for access with posterior marginal mandibulectomy for large soft-tissue lesions should be avoided — the increased osteoradionecrosis risk warrants a segmental resection in these circumstances
> - In the dentate patient, biopsy tumour thickness >2 mm predicts final thickness >3.5 mm — imaging before biopsy is advisable for small alveolar tumours to prevent local marrow signal changes from upstaging disease
> - With modern microvascular reconstruction, there is little or no difference in quality of life between rim and segmental resection, particularly for defects >4 cm — patient age alone should not rule out major surgery
> - Computer-aided planning (CAD/CAM) with 3D-printed cutting guides and pre-bent plates is invaluable for achieving optimal cosmetic and functional outcomes in complex bony reconstructions
> - The general success rate of microvascular free-flap surgery exceeds 95%, but 5–15% of patients may require return to theatre for complications
