# Lacrimal System — Physiology, Endoscopic DCR, and Lacrimal Sump

**Question:** *"Describe the physiology of lacrimation and preoperative evaluation for endoscopic DCR. Discuss the lacrimal system physiology, endonasal DCR technique with advantages/disadvantages, and lacrimal sump syndrome. Describe endoscopic DCR indications, advantages, and complications."*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 1, Chapter 111 (Disorders of the Orbit)

---

## Introduction

The lacrimal system is intimately related to the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, making it an important area for otorhinolaryngologists. Epiphora from nasolacrimal system obstruction is a common condition, with anatomic obstruction accounting for 70% and functional obstruction for 30% of cases. Dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) is the standard surgical procedure to bypass nasolacrimal duct obstruction and restore lacrimal drainage. The endoscopic approach has become increasingly popular due to its advantages of no external scar, preservation of the lacrimal pump mechanism, and ability to address concurrent sinonasal pathology.

---

## Part A — Anatomy and Physiology of Lacrimation

### Tear Film

The tear film consists of three layers that protect, nourish, and lubricate the ocular surface:

- **Lipid layer (outermost):** Secreted by Meibomian glands (tarsal glands); prevents evaporation of the aqueous layer
- **Aqueous layer (middle):** Secreted by the main lacrimal gland (orbital and palpebral lobes) and accessory glands of Krause and Wolfring; constitutes the bulk of the tear film
- **Mucin layer (innermost):** Secreted by conjunctival goblet cells; converts the hydrophobic corneal epithelium to a hydrophilic surface

### Lacrimal Gland

The lacrimal gland is divided into orbital and palpebral lobes by the lateral horn of the levator aponeurosis. It is supplied by the lacrimal artery (branch of ophthalmic artery) and innervated by the lacrimal nerve (V1). Parasympathetic secretomotor fibres reach the gland via the greater petrosal nerve → nerve of pterygoid canal → pterygopalatine ganglion → zygomatic nerve → lacrimal nerve.

### Lacrimal Drainage Pathway

The drainage pathway follows a sequential anatomical course:

1. **Puncta:** Superior and inferior puncta on the eyelid margins at the medial canthus
2. **Canaliculi:** Initially run at 90° to the lid margin for 1–2 mm, then run parallel for approximately 10 mm
3. **Common canaliculus:** Formed by junction of superior and inferior canaliculi; angles inferiorly and forward for 3–5 mm
4. **Lacrimal sac:** Located in the concavity of the lacrimal fossa; anterior wall formed by the harder frontal process of maxilla (anterior lacrimal crest), posterior wall by softer lacrimal bone
5. **Nasolacrimal duct:** Exits inferiorly from lacrimal sac, courses 12 mm inferiorly
6. **Hasner's valve:** Opening into the inferior meatus, located 10–15 mm behind the anterior end of the inferior turbinate and 16 mm above the nasal floor

### Lacrimal Pump Mechanism

Blinking activates the lacrimal pump. Contraction of the orbicularis oculi (particularly the pretarsal and preseptal portions, Horner's muscle) creates negative pressure in the lacrimal sac, drawing tears from the canaliculi. Relaxation of the orbicularis allows the sac to collapse, propelling tears down the nasolacrimal duct into the nose.

## Part B — Pre-operative Evaluation for Endoscopic DCR

### Clinical Assessment

- **History:** Duration of epiphora, unilateral vs bilateral, history of dacryocystitis, radiation, trauma, granulomatous disease, sinonasal surgery, neoplasm
- **Inspection:** Mucocele (swelling at medial canthus), regurgitation of material on pressure over lacrimal sac

### Diagnostic Tests

| Test | Technique | Interpretation |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| FDDT | Fluorescein in eye; check nose at 10–15 min with blue light | Absence in nose = obstruction |
| Jones I | Fluorescein recovery from nose | Positive = patent system |
| Jones II | Saline flush after failed Jones I | Dye recovery = functional block; clear = complete block |
| Syringing | Saline through inferior canaliculus | Reflux via opposite canaliculus = distal block |
| Probing | Bowman probe via canaliculus | Identifies level of obstruction |

### Imaging

- **Dacryocystography (DCG):** Contrast injection identifies diverticula, stenosis, strictures, dacryoliths, or tumour
- **Dacryoscintigraphy:** 99mTc radionuclide — helpful for functional obstruction (free flow on syringe test with abnormal FDDT)
- **CT scan:** Not routinely indicated unless suspicion of neoplasm or midface trauma
- **Nasal endoscopy:** Essential to exclude anatomic abnormalities (septal deviation, turbinate hypertrophy, polyps) that may interfere with endoscopic approach

## Part C — Endoscopic DCR Technique

### Indications

A DCR is indicated for symptomatic distal obstruction of the nasolacrimal duct not relieved by probing and syringing. It is **not indicated** when the obstruction is in the canaliculi or puncta, as the endoscopic DCR will not bypass these areas.

### Surgical Steps

1. **Create posteriorly based mucosal flap:**
   - Superior horizontal incision 10 mm above the axilla of the middle turbinate, extending 2–3 mm posterior to the axilla and running forward 10 mm onto the frontal process of maxilla
   - Vertical incision on the frontal process from superior to just above the inferior turbinate insertion
   - Inferior horizontal incision from the uncinate insertion to join the vertical cut
   - Elevate flap with suction freer — stay on bone at the transition from hard maxillary bone to softer lacrimal bone

2. **Remove lacrimal bone:** Use a round otologic knife to flake off the lacrimal bone overlying the posterior portion of the lacrimal sac

3. **Remove frontal process of maxilla:** Harder bone overlying the anterior sac — use Kerrison punch or DCR drill bur; expose the sac fully up to the mucosal incisions; the sac forms a prominent bulge into the nasal cavity

4. **Expose agger nasi cell:** Allows lacrimal sac mucosa to lie against agger cell mucosa for wider marsupialization

5. **Marsupialize the lacrimal sac:**
   - Insert lacrimal probe through inferior canaliculus to tent the sac
   - Make vertical incision with two horizontal incisions (superior and inferior) to create widest possible anterior and posterior flaps
   - Sac should lie flat on the lateral nasal wall

6. **Reposition mucosal flap:** Trim flap to superior and inferior limbs; reapproximate over bone at the bone–sac junction

7. **Pass Crawford silastic tubes:** Through superior and inferior puncta into nasal cavity; secure by tying together; place Gelfoam to hold flaps

### Post-operative Management

- Saline sprays, 7-day oral antibiotics, topical antibiotic eye drops for 2 weeks
- Endoscopic debridement and crust removal at 2 weeks
- Crawford tubes removed at 4–6 weeks
- Patency checked using fluorescein dye disappearance test

### Advantages of Endoscopic DCR

- No external scar
- Preserves the lacrimal pump mechanism (orbicularis oculi not disrupted)
- Faster recovery time
- Can be performed bilaterally simultaneously
- Allows treatment of concurrent sinonasal pathology
- Can be done under local anaesthesia

### Disadvantages

- Learning curve
- Not suitable for canalicular obstruction
- Dependent on adequate nasal endoscopic access
- Cost of endoscopic equipment
- May require powered instruments or laser

## Part D — Lacrimal Sump Syndrome

### Definition

Lacrimal sump syndrome occurs when a residual lacrimal sac pocket persists below the level of the surgically created rhinostomy opening. This inferior remnant of the sac collects tears and debris, creating a stagnant pool (sump).

### Causes

- Rhinostomy placed **too high** relative to the sac
- **Inadequate bone removal** — insufficient removal of frontal process of maxilla inferiorly
- Failure to open the sac to its full inferior extent

### Clinical Features

- Persistent or recurrent epiphora despite a patent rhinostomy
- Recurrent dacryocystitis from stagnant sac contents
- Mucocele formation in the residual sac remnant

### Management

- **Revision endoscopic DCR** with extension of the bone removal inferiorly
- Complete marsupialization of the residual inferior sac
- Ensure the final rhinostomy extends to the inferior limit of the sac

## Part E — Complications of Endoscopic DCR

Complications are similar to those of endoscopic sinus surgery:

- **Haemorrhage:** Occurs in approximately 2% of patients; usually from branches of the angular or anterior ethmoidal artery
- **Synechia and granulation tissue:** Can obstruct the rhinostomy; managed with endoscopic debridement
- **Restenosis/closure of rhinostomy:** Most common cause of failure; may require revision surgery
- **Orbital fat herniation:** From excessive bone removal laterally
- **[[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]]:** Extremely rare; from inadvertent skull base injury
- **Frontal/maxillary sinusitis:** From obstruction of drainage pathways
- **Stent-related complications:** Displacement, granulation tissue formation, cheese-wiring effect on puncta, premature extrusion
- **Orbital haematoma:** Rare but serious — requires immediate decompression

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. The nasolacrimal drainage system runs from puncta → canaliculi → common canaliculus → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → Hasner's valve (inferior meatus)
> 2. Epiphora is 70% anatomic and 30% functional obstruction
> 3. Endoscopic DCR is indicated for distal nasolacrimal duct obstruction — NOT for canalicular or punctal obstruction
> 4. A posteriorly based mucosal flap is the first step in endoscopic DCR
> 5. The lacrimal fossa is bounded anteriorly by the harder frontal process of maxilla and posteriorly by the softer lacrimal bone
> 6. Crawford silastic tubes are placed through both puncta and removed at 4–6 weeks
> 7. Lacrimal sump syndrome results from a rhinostomy placed too high with residual inferior sac remnant
> 8. Advantages of endoscopic DCR include no scar, pump preservation, bilateral simultaneous surgery, and ability to treat concurrent sinonasal pathology
> 9. Restenosis is the most common cause of DCR failure
> 10. Up to 15% of patients may sustain nasolacrimal injury after FESS, but actual epiphora requiring DCR is very rare


> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Stay on bone during mucosal flap elevation** — losing the plane at the transition from hard maxillary to soft lacrimal bone is a common pitfall
> - **Expose the agger nasi cell** to allow wider marsupialization of the lacrimal sac
> - **Dacryoscintigraphy with 99mTc** is the best investigation for functional lacrimal obstruction
> - **Always perform nasal endoscopy** pre-operatively to exclude anatomic abnormalities that may prevent endoscopic access
> - **The key to preventing lacrimal sump** is complete inferior bone removal and full marsupialization of the sac to its lowest extent
