# Diagnostic Nasal Endoscopy, FESS Ergonomics, Endoscope Care and Revision FESS

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition — Volume 1, Chapter 98 (FESS Outcomes); Volume 3, Chapter 66 (Endoscopic Assessment of the Larynx — Sterilization and Endoscope Care)

## INTRODUCTION

Nasal endoscopy is the cornerstone of rhinological practice, serving both diagnostic and surgical purposes. The Hopkins rod-lens rigid endoscope provides superior image quality for examination of the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses. Proper ergonomic setup during FESS ensures optimal visualization and surgeon comfort, while meticulous endoscope care and sterilization protocols protect both patients and equipment. Revision FESS carries higher complication rates than primary surgery due to distorted anatomy, and requires careful preoperative planning with consideration of image-guided navigation.

---

## PART A: DIAGNOSTIC NASAL ENDOSCOPY — INDICATIONS AND TECHNIQUE

### Indications

- Assessment of nasal obstruction, rhinorrhoea and [[Epistaxis Management|epistaxis]]
- Evaluation of sinonasal inflammatory disease (CRS, polyps)
- Screening for sinonasal malignancy
- Post-operative surveillance after FESS or sinonasal tumour surgery
- Assessment of nasopharynx (NPC screening, adenoid hypertrophy, fossa of Rosenmuller)
- Evaluation of [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF rhinorrhoea]]
- Guided biopsy or microbiological culture
- Pre-operative assessment before FESS
- Assessment of nasal valve function and septal pathology

### Preparation

- Topical decongestant (oxymetazoline or xylometazoline) applied on cotton pledgets or spray
- Topical anaesthetic (lignocaine 4% or co-phenylcaine spray)
- Allow 5–10 minutes for effect before commencing examination

### Technique — Three Standard Passes

**First pass (floor):** The endoscope is passed along the nasal floor between the septum and inferior turbinate. Examine the inferior meatus (for accessory ostia and nasolacrimal duct opening), the choana and the nasopharynx (fossa of Rosenmuller, Eustachian tube orifice, adenoid pad).

**Second pass (middle meatus):** The endoscope is directed between the middle turbinate and lateral wall. Examine the uncinate process, hiatus semilunaris, ethmoid bulla, maxillary sinus ostium and the middle meatus for polyps, purulent discharge or mucosal oedema.

**Third pass (superior/sphenoethmoidal):** The endoscope is directed superiorly to examine the superior meatus, sphenoethmoidal recess, sphenoid ostium and olfactory cleft.

### Endoscope Types

| Type | Diameter | Viewing Angle | Best Use |
|------|----------|---------------|----------|
| Rigid Hopkins rod | 4 mm (adult) | 0°, 30°, 45°, 70° | Superior image quality; standard for clinic and OR |
| Rigid (paediatric) | 2.7 mm | 0°, 30° | Children and narrow nasal cavities |
| Flexible fibreoptic | 3.2–3.7 mm | Variable | Better tolerated; simultaneous laryngeal assessment |

## PART B: FESS ERGONOMIC SETUP

### Patient Positioning

- Supine with **reverse Trendelenburg** position (15° head-up) — reduces venous pressure and bleeding
- Head slightly turned towards the surgeon
- Eyes visible to the surgeon (to detect orbital complications)

### Surgeon and Equipment

- Surgeon seated or standing at the patient's head
- Dominant hand holds instruments; non-dominant hand holds the endoscope
- **Monitor positioned at the patient's foot end**, directly in the surgeon's line of sight — avoid neck rotation
- HD camera system with white balance calibration
- Anti-fog solutions applied to endoscope tip

### Endoscope Selection for FESS

- **0° endoscope**: initial orientation and overall cavity assessment
- **30° endoscope**: primary instrument for middle meatus work
- **45° and 70° endoscopes**: specific areas (frontal recess, maxillary sinus interior)

### Optimizing the Surgical Field

- Adequate topical decongestion (1:1000 adrenaline-soaked pledgets)
- **Controlled hypotensive anaesthesia** (target MAP 60–70 mmHg)
- Anti-Trendelenburg position (head-up 15°)
- Controlled positive pressure ventilation (avoid straining)
- Warm saline irrigation to maintain clear field
- Systematic identification of landmarks before instrumentation

## PART C: ENDOSCOPE CARE AND STERILIZATION

### Care and Maintenance

- Handle with care to prevent lens and optical fibre damage
- **Store vertically** — hang in storage cupboards or use custom-made storage/transport trays
- Vertical storage prevents repeated twisting and bending that breaks optical fibres, reducing the endoscope's lifetime
- Visual inspection and formal **leak testing** before each use
- Immediate cleaning after use to prevent biological material from drying on surfaces
- A designated health practitioner should be responsible for endoscope care

### Sterilization and Decontamination

The risk of transmitting infection between scope and patient has not been precisely quantified for nasoendoscopes. For gastroscopes it is estimated at **1 in 1.8 million**. The risk of variant CJD (vCJD) transmission is extremely low with scrupulous decontamination.

**Infection prevention measures:**
- Remove all jewellery including wristwatches
- Proper hand decontamination
- Wear disposable gloves
- Minimize user-to-scope contamination

### Sterilization Methods

| Method | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-------------|------------|--------------|
| Chemical soak | Most common; disinfectant type and duration vary | Established method | Non-standardized; variable practice |
| Disposable sheaths | Effective barrier even to viral particles | No special equipment; improved designs | Scope contamination if breached; daily HLD still needed |
| Alcohol wipes | Surface wipe between patients | Quick and simple | Least effective method |
| AEWDs | Automatic washer-disinfectors | Standardized protocol; clinical governance | High setup costs; slow turnaround |

### UK National Guidelines (Key Points)

- Maintain a **logbook** recording: patient details, time/date of use, name of user, person responsible for sterilization, and sterilization method
- Record **instrument serial numbers** in the patient's notes
- Training programmes for all involved staff with up-to-date authorized personnel lists
- **Daily high-level disinfection** required even when disposable sheaths are used
- Out-of-hours cleaning by junior staff remains problematic — mainly due to lack of knowledge and access to appropriate facilities
- Transport used endoscopes to decontamination site in a labelled disposable bag

## PART D: REVISION FESS — CAUSES OF FAILURE AND COMPLICATIONS

### Causes of Primary FESS Failure

- **Residual ethmoid cells**: agger nasi, frontal cells, Haller cells
- Lateralized middle turbinate with synechiae formation
- Retained uncinate process or incomplete uncinectomy
- **Stenosis** of maxillary antrostomy or [[Frontal Recess Frontal Sinus Surgery|frontal sinus]]otomy
- Missed natural ostium → **mucous recirculation**
- Persistent mucosal disease: eosinophilic CRS, [[AFRS Fungal Ball|AFRS]], Samter's triad (aspirin, asthma, polyps)
- **Osteoneogenesis** in the frontal recess (new bone formation)

### Revision Rates

- Overall: **10–20% over 5-year period**
- CRSwNP: **21%** revision rate
- CRSsNP: **15%** revision rate
- Eosinophilic CRS has the highest recurrence rates
- Full-house FESS with postoperative steroid irrigations significantly reduces recurrence in CRSwNP

### Complications of Revision FESS

Revision FESS carries **higher complication rates** than primary surgery due to distorted or absent landmarks, scarring and osteoneogenesis.

**Major complications:**
- CSF leak (lateral lamella of cribriform plate is most vulnerable)
- Orbital injury: haematoma, diplopia, blindness (optic nerve injury)
- Internal carotid artery injury (catastrophic — muscle patch or endovascular stent)
- Meningitis, intracranial abscess

**Minor complications:**
- Epistaxis (postoperative)
- Synechiae formation
- Infection
- Orbital emphysema
- Hyposmia/anosmia

## PART E: PRECAUTIONS AND PREVENTION IN REVISION FESS

### Preoperative

- Thorough CT review with attention to all anatomical variants
- **Image-guided navigation strongly recommended** — revision cases often lack recognizable landmarks
- Review previous operative notes and identify causes of primary failure
- Optimize medical management before considering revision (topical steroids, saline irrigation, antibiotics)

### Intraoperative

- **Systematic landmark identification**: inferior turbinate → middle turbinate → lamina papyracea → skull base
- Never dissect in areas without identifiable landmarks
- Begin in well-recognized areas and work systematically towards areas of scarring
- Use powered instruments (microdebrider) with the guard always facing **away from orbit and skull base**
- Powered instruments should **NOT be used on tissue that has not been clearly identified**
- Controlled hypotensive anaesthesia to minimize bleeding
- Topical adrenaline pledgets and bipolar cautery for haemostasis
- Intraoperative correlation with IGS at regular intervals

### Postoperative

- Saline nasal douching (alkaline or isotonic)
- Topical nasal steroids
- Clinic debridement at 1–2 weeks (removal of crusts, clots, adhesions)
- Regular follow-up endoscopy with documentation
- Long-term medical management for CRS (especially eosinophilic subtypes)
- Consider biologic therapy (anti-IL5, anti-IgE) for refractory CRSwNP

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Diagnostic nasal endoscopy uses three standard passes — floor (inferior meatus/nasopharynx), middle meatus (uncinate/bulla/hiatus) and superior meatus/sphenoethmoidal recess
> 2. FESS ergonomic setup requires the patient in reverse Trendelenburg (15° head-up) with the monitor at the foot end directly in the surgeon's line of sight
> 3. Rigid Hopkins rod endoscopes are available in 0°, 30°, 45° and 70° — the 0° provides initial orientation while the 30° is used for middle meatus work
> 4. Endoscope sterilization methods include chemical soak, disposable sheaths (effective even against viral particles) and automatic washer-disinfectors (AEWDs)
> 5. Infection transmission risk via endoscopes is estimated at 1 in 1.8 million — UK guidelines mandate logbook documentation of patient details, user, sterilization method and instrument serial numbers
> 6. Flexible endoscopes must be stored hung vertically to prevent repeated bending and breaking of optical fibres
> 7. FESS revision rates are 10–20% over 5 years — common causes of failure include residual ethmoid cells, lateralized middle turbinate, stenosed antrostomy and missed natural ostium
> 8. Revision FESS carries higher complication rates than primary surgery due to distorted landmarks, scarring and osteoneogenesis
> 9. Image-guided navigation is strongly recommended for revision FESS where anatomical landmarks may be absent
> 10. Systematic landmark identification in revision FESS follows IT → MT → lamina papyracea → skull base — powered instruments must NOT be used on unidentified tissue


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Disposable endoscope sheaths provide an effective viral barrier but daily high-level disinfection of the underlying endoscope remains mandatory
> - Mucous recirculation occurs when the true maxillary ostium is not connected with the surgically created antrostomy — this is a preventable and common cause of FESS failure
> - Osteoneogenesis in the frontal recess is a major cause of frontal sinus obstruction after FESS and may necessitate Draf III or osteoplastic flap procedures
> - Out-of-hours endoscope cleaning by junior medical staff is a recognized clinical governance concern — all departments should maintain training programmes and authorized personnel lists
> - Eosinophilic CRS has the highest post-FESS recurrence rate — full-house FESS with postoperative steroid irrigations significantly reduces this recurrence in CRSwNP
