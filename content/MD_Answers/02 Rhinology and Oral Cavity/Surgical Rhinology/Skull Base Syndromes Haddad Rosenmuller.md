# Skull Base Osteomyelitis, Skull Base Syndromes, Haddad Flap and Fossa of Rosenmuller

**Question:** *2015-9: Discuss skull base osteomyelitis including pathology, staging and diagnosis. 2015-20: Define skull base syndrome and enumerate its causes, features and management. 2016-7: What is the Haddad flap? Discuss its definition, indications and surgical landmarks. 2014-3: Describe the clinical significance of the fossa of Rosenmuller.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 2, Chapters 98, 99, 107, 111, 114; Volume 1, Chapters 85, 87, 114, 116; Volume 3, Chapter 8

## INTRODUCTION

The skull base is a complex anatomical region traversed by vital neurovascular structures through multiple foramina. Pathology at the skull base produces distinctive clinical patterns based on the specific foramina and cranial nerves involved. Skull base osteomyelitis is a severe infection arising most commonly from necrotizing otitis externa in diabetic and immunocompromised patients, with progressive bone destruction and cranial neuropathies. Skull base syndromes are named clinical patterns of cranial nerve dysfunction that help localize lesions to specific anatomical sites. The fossa of Rosenmuller (pharyngeal recess) is the commonest site of origin of [[Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma|nasopharyngeal carcinoma]], and its anatomical relations to the foramen lacerum and Eustachian tube determine the clinical presentation and spread of NPC. The Haddad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap has revolutionized endoscopic skull base reconstruction by providing a reliable vascularized tissue for closure of dural defects.

## PART A: SKULL BASE OSTEOMYELITIS (SBO)

### Definition and Nomenclature

Skull base osteomyelitis is a severe infection in which soft tissue pathogens from the external auditory canal spread to the periosteum and temporal bone, causing necrosis. The infection may progress to involve the facial nerve, carotid artery, jugular vein, bulbar and hypoglossal nerves, and eventually other bones of the skull base. Chandler coined the term "malignant external otitis" in 1968, though this is now considered a misnomer as the process is inflammatory rather than neoplastic. The preferred terminology distinguishes necrotizing otitis externa (aggressive soft tissue infection without bony involvement) from skull base osteomyelitis (infection with histological or radiological confirmed bony involvement).

### Pathophysiology

The infection spreads from the external auditory canal through the fissures of Santorini at the osteocartilaginous junction. It extends medially to the tympanomastoid suture along venous canals and fascial planes, invading connective tissue, cartilage, bone, nerves and blood vessels. The inflammatory process transforms compact bone into granulation tissue. As infection progresses to skull base foramina, cranial neuropathies develop. The facial nerve is most commonly affected due to its close relation to the external auditory canal. Disease spreads medially to affect nerves around the jugular foramen (IX, X, XI, XII), then the abducens and trigeminal nerves around the petrous apex, and also the optic nerve. Septic thrombosis of the sigmoid sinus and internal jugular vein may complicate the course. The otic capsule and middle ear structures are very resistant and are rarely involved.

### Epidemiology and Predisposing Factors

| Factor | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Age | Usually older than 60 years |
| Sex ratio | Male preponderance (2:1) |
| Diabetes | Present in 65-100% of cases |
| Other risk factors | HIV/AIDS, steroids, chemo, leukaemia, lymphoma, renal transplant |
| Incidence trend | Increasing (67 in 1999 to 421 in 2012-13) |

Diabetics are susceptible due to weakened immune response to Pseudomonas, endarteritis, microangiopathy, small vessel obliteration and high pH of cerumen. HIV-affected patients tend to be younger and more likely to have fungal infections (Aspergillus species), especially when CD4 counts are less than 50 cells/mm.

### Staging

**Chandler Classification (Necrotizing Otitis Externa):**
- Stage I: External otitis (soft tissue infection of EAC)
- Stage II: Periostitis of temporal bone
- Stage III: Skull base osteomyelitis (confirmed bony involvement)

**Cohen and Friedman Staging:**
- Stage I: Disease localized to temporal bone and external auditory canal
- Stage II: Extension to skull base and mastoid with cranial nerve involvement
- Stage III: Intracranial complications (meningitis, cerebral abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis)

### Clinical Features

Patients present with severe unremitting otalgia (prevalence 75-100%), unilateral otorrhoea and hearing loss. The pain is nocturnal, deep, lancinating and disproportionate to clinical signs. Examination reveals granulation tissue or exposed bone on the floor of the EAC. Cranial neuropathies develop in the following order as the infection progresses medially:

- **VII (facial nerve)** -- earliest and most common (up to 25% of patients)
- **IX, X, XI, XII** -- as infection reaches the jugular foramen and hypoglossal canal
- **VI (abducens)** -- at the petrous apex
- **II (optic nerve)** -- late involvement

Meningitis, cerebral abscess and sigmoid sinus thrombosis are late signs associated with poor prognosis. A meta-analysis found the following factors to have statistically significant negative effect on outcome: cranial neuropathies, EAC granulations, bilateral symptoms and positive Aspergillus cultures.

### Diagnosis

| Investigation | Role and Findings |
|---------------|-------------------|
| CT scan | Bony erosion, reduced density; limited (detects only >30% demineralization) |
| MRI | Soft tissue extent, bone marrow oedema; cannot assess treatment response |
| Tc-99m bone scan | Almost 100% sensitivity for early detection; high osteoblastic uptake |
| Gallium-67 scan | Monitors treatment response; normalizes with disease resolution |
| SPECT | 50% more effective than CT at detecting skull base destruction |
| FDG-PET | Most accurate for confirming/excluding osteomyelitis |
| Biopsy | Essential to exclude malignancy, histiocytosis, tuberculosis |

Microbiology: Pseudomonas aeruginosa is the most common organism. Other pathogens include Staphylococcus aureus, Proteus mirabilis, Klebsiella species and Enterobacter. Fungal infections (Aspergillus fumigatus most common) are associated with immunocompromised patients and carry a worse prognosis than Pseudomonas.

### Treatment

Long-term systemic culture-directed antimicrobial therapy is the mainstay of treatment:

- **First-line:** Oral ciprofloxacin 750 mg BD (good bone penetration, oral bioavailability) -- minimum 6-8 weeks
- **Alternatives:** Ceftazidime, cefepime, ticarcillin-clavulanate, aminoglycosides (for fluoroquinolone resistance)
- **Fungal SBO:** Amphotericin B followed by oral itraconazole
- **Adjuncts:** Rifampicin 600 mg BD may be added (no conclusive evidence of additional benefit)
- **Monitoring:** Gallium-67 scan repeated every 4 weeks until negative; antibiotics continued as long as gallium scan remains positive
- **Diabetes control:** Aggressive glycaemic control is as crucial as antimicrobial therapy
- **Hyperbaric oxygen:** Increases partial pressure of oxygen, relieves hypoxia, enhances oxidative killing of microbes; however, Cochrane review found no evidence from RCTs to support this
- **Surgery:** Limited role -- primarily for obtaining culture specimens, local debridement of granulation tissue, removing dead sequestra, draining abscesses, and excluding malignancy

## PART B: SKULL BASE SYNDROMES

### Definition

Skull base syndromes are clinical patterns of cranial nerve dysfunction arising from lesions at specific anatomical sites of the skull base. The pattern of cranial nerve involvement allows localization of the lesion to a particular foramen or anatomical space.

### Named Syndromes

| Syndrome | Nerves Affected | Location / Aetiology |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|
| Vernet's | IX, X, XI | Internal jugular foramen -- schwannomas, meningiomas, fracture |
| Collet-Sicard | IX, X, XI, XII | Posterior laterocondylar space -- glomus jugulare, skull base metastasis, schwannoma |
| Villaret's | IX, X, XI, XII + sympathetic | Posterior retroparotid space -- paragangliomas, carotid dissection, metastasis |
| Jackson's | X, XI, XII | Chance combination of peripheral lesions |
| Tapia's | X, XII | Difficult to explain anatomically; probable chance association |
| Avellis's | X + contralateral spinothalamic | Brainstem lesion |
| Wallenberg's | IX, X + sympathetic + contralateral spinothalamic | Dorsolateral medullary infarction -- most classical brainstem vascular syndrome |
| Garcin (half-base) | Multiple unilateral CN palsies | NPC, chordoma, large skull base tumours |

**Key Distinction:** Collet-Sicard syndrome (IX, X, XI, XII) spares the cervical sympathetic, while Villaret's syndrome (IX, X, XI, XII plus sympathetic) represents a complete external jugular foramen syndrome with sympathetic involvement.

Wallenberg's syndrome (lateral medullary infarction) is the most classical brainstem vascular syndrome encountered, characterized by severe vertigo, vomiting, hiccoughs, ipsilateral IX and X palsy, ipsilateral Horner's syndrome, ipsilateral spinothalamic loss over the face and contralateral spinothalamic loss in the limbs.

### Common Causes

- **[[Glomus Tumour|Glomus tumour]]s:** The commonest cause of multiple lower cranial nerve palsies. In a series of 36 patients with glomus tumours, 30 had deafness and [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], 13 had facial weakness, 13 had dysphagia and hoarseness (IX and X), 12 had shoulder weakness (XI) and 14 had tongue weakness and fasciculation (XII)
- **Nasopharyngeal carcinoma:** Enters skull through foramen lacerum or inferior orbital fissure; V nerve involved in 70% of cases, III/IV/VI in 50%
- **Skull base metastases:** Breast, prostate, renal, lung primaries
- **Skull base osteomyelitis:** Progressive CN involvement from VII through to IX-XII
- **Schwannomas and meningiomas:** Of the jugular foramen and lateral recess
- **Skull base fracture:** Especially affecting the petrous bone
- **[[Mastoid Surgery Cholesteatoma|Cholesteatoma]]:** Particularly affects VII and IX
- **Carotid artery dissection:** Can cause Collet-Sicard or Villaret's syndrome

### Investigation

- **MRI with gadolinium:** Gold standard for soft tissue assessment and CN involvement; salt-and-pepper appearance characteristic of paragangliomas
- **CT (including CBCT):** Bone destruction, bony landmarks, temporal bone involvement
- **Carotid angiography:** For vascular tumours -- defines blood supply, circle of Willis integrity, pre-operative embolization targets
- **MIBG / Indium-111-octreotide scintigraphy:** Detection of multiple paragangliomas or metastatic disease
- **SPECT:** 50% more effective than CT at detecting skull base destruction; shows specific uptake in glomus tumours with rapid wash-out
- **24-hour urine VMA/catecholamines:** For paragangliomas
- **Audiometry:** All skull base patients should have hearing assessment (49% have severe-profound deafness at presentation)

### Management

Management is directed at the underlying cause:

- **Glomus tumours:** Surgery (often via infratemporal fossa approach), with pre-operative embolization for vascular tumours; [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] for unresectable or residual disease
- **NPC:** Radiotherapy as primary modality with concurrent chemotherapy for advanced disease
- **Metastases:** Radiotherapy for palliation; systemic therapy depending on primary
- **Skull base osteomyelitis:** Long-course antibiotics as detailed in Part A
- **Schwannomas/Meningiomas:** Surgical excision or stereotactic radiosurgery

## PART C: FOSSA OF ROSENMULLER -- CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE

### Definition and Anatomy

The fossa of Rosenmuller (pharyngeal recess) is a mucosal depression in the lateral wall of the nasopharynx, situated posterosuperior to the torus tubarius (the cartilaginous cushion of the Eustachian tube orifice). It lies just anterior to the anteromedial aspect of the foramen lacerum and hence the petrous apex.

### Anatomical Relations

- **Anteriorly:** Eustachian tube orifice (torus tubarius)
- **Posteriorly:** Salpingopharyngeus muscle arising from the posterior aspect of the tubal orifice
- **Superiorly:** Pharyngobasilar fascia connecting the superior constrictors to the skull base
- **Deep:** Foramen lacerum and petrous apex of the temporal bone
- **Paratubal muscles:** Levator palati (medial, within the pharynx) and tensor palati (lateral, outside the pharynx) arise either side of the pharyngobasilar fascia

### Clinical Significance

1. **Commonest site of origin of NPC:** Most early-stage NPC primaries originate from the fossa of Rosenmuller. Nasopharyngeal tumours most commonly arise in the fossa of Rosenmuller or near the orifice of the Eustachian tube and are usually squamous carcinomas.

2. **Route of intracranial invasion:** NPC originating here may invade upwards through the foramen lacerum, producing a lateral rectus palsy (VI nerve) by compressing the abducens nerve as it crosses the apex of the petrous bone and enters the cavernous sinus.

3. **Neurological involvement pattern of NPC:** In 70% of NPC cases the V nerve is involved; in 50% the III, IV and VI nerves are involved; visual pathways in 8.5% and lower cranial nerves in 10%. The favourite routes of intracranial entry are through the inferior orbital fissure or via the foramen lacerum alongside the carotid artery.

4. **Eustachian tube dysfunction and OME:** The intimate relationship of the fossa with the Eustachian tube orifice means that NPC here commonly presents with unilateral [[OME Pathophysiology|otitis media with effusion]]. A unilateral OME in an adult mandates examination of the nasopharynx and specifically the fossa of Rosenmuller.

5. **Lymphoid tissue:** The region around the tubal orifice and fossa of Rosenmuller contains lymphoid tissue (part of Waldeyer's ring) which may be hyperplastic.

6. **Variable depth:** The fossa has variable depth between individuals, and a deep fossa may harbour small tumours that are difficult to detect clinically.

7. **Biopsy importance:** Biopsy of mucosa from BOTH fossae of Rosenmuller should be obtained when NPC is suspected, even if no visible lesion is present. Endoscopic visualization with an angled endoscope is essential for adequate assessment.

8. **Maxillary division of V nerve:** The maxillary division of the trigeminal nerve runs past the mouth of the Eustachian tube and the fossa of Rosenmuller, making it particularly vulnerable to NPC involvement causing facial numbness.

## PART D: HADDAD-BASSAGASTEGUY NASOSEPTAL FLAP (NSF)

### Definition

The Haddad-Bassagasteguy nasoseptal flap is a pedicled vascularized mucoperiosteal/mucoperichondrial flap raised from the nasal septum, used primarily for endoscopic skull base reconstruction. It is described as the workhorse for endoscopic skull base reconstruction.

### Blood Supply

The flap is based on the posterior septal artery, a branch of the sphenopalatine artery (internal maxillary artery). The posterior septal artery runs just below the sphenoid ostium to supply the majority of the septal mucosa. This vascular pedicle must NOT be violated during flap harvest or septectomy.

### Indications

1. Skull base reconstruction after endoscopic tumour resection (most common indication)
2. [[CSF Rhinorrhea|CSF leak]] repair, especially large defects (greater than 1 cm)
3. Reconstruction after transsphenoidal pituitary surgery (when CSF leak occurs)
4. Reconstruction after endoscopic craniofacial resection (e.g. olfactory neuroblastoma, sinonasal malignancy)
5. Sellar, suprasellar, clival and anterior cranial fossa defects following extended endonasal approaches

### Surgical Landmarks

| Landmark | Significance |
|----------|-------------|
| Sphenoid ostia | Superior border of flap harvest |
| Posterior septal artery | Vascular pedicle -- must be preserved |
| Olfactory strip (1.5 cm) | Posterosuperior septal mucosa to preserve smell and taste |
| Floor of nose / choana | Inferior border of flap |
| Pterygoid wedge | Pedicle attachment point |

### Key Technical Points

- The nasoseptal flap MUST be raised BEFORE performing septectomy or tumour resection to protect the vascular pedicle
- During harvest, at least 1.5 cm of posterosuperior septal mucosa in the olfactory cleft region should be preserved for smell and taste
- The flap can be stored in the nasopharynx or maxillary sinus during the tumour resection phase
- The flap is large enough to cover extensive defects including the entire anterior cranial fossa and planum sphenoidale
- In revision surgery, the sphenopalatine artery and posterior septal artery may have been compromised by a conventional transseptal microscopic approach, necessitating alternative flap options

### Rescue Flap Technique

The rescue flap is elevated in most patients undergoing endoscopic endonasal resection of pituitary adenomas. It mobilizes the pedicle from the face of the sphenoid, allowing a wide sphenoid opening without compromising the nasoseptal flap blood supply. The rescue flap harvest begins by incising the mucosa over the rostrum of the sphenoid sinus from the lateral wall of the nose at the level of the sphenoid ostium, carrying the incision over the sphenoid rostrum into the nasal septum mucosa. If a nasoseptal flap is not needed at the end of surgery, absorbable packing reapproximates the pedicle to the bony rostrum.

### Nasoseptal Flap Takedown

For patients who have had prior NSF placement, the flap can be reused (the "nasoseptal flap takedown technique"). The edges of the previously placed flap and surrounding nasal mucosa are identified and followed to the pedicle. The flap is mobilized centripetally using a Cottle elevator. Limitations include: the shape of a previously used flap is defined by the healing process over the prior defect, it is less pliable, and it tends to migrate out of the nasopharynx due to increased stiffness and fibrosis.

## PART E: SKULL BASE RECONSTRUCTION PRINCIPLES

The size and location of the skull base defect dictates the reconstruction strategy:

- **Small defects (< 1 cm):** Bath Plug type repair (Wormald) -- collagen matrix inlay placed through the dural defect
- **Medium defects (1-3 cm):** Multilayer closure with underlay of fascia lata or dural substitute (Duragen) followed by overlay of vascularized nasoseptal flap
- **Large defects (> 3 cm):** Addition of rigid buttress (septal cartilage or titanium mesh) between the underlay and overlay layers to minimize risk of encephalocele formation

Alternative vascularized flaps include the inferior turbinate flap, middle turbinate flap, and extracranial pericranial flap. Free tissue grafts (fat graft, fascial graft) may be used for small to moderate defects when vascularized flaps are not available.

Post-operative management includes nasal packing (removed at 3-5 days), lumbar drain if needed, and antibiotics. CSF leak rates decreased dramatically following the introduction of the nasoseptal flap for reconstruction. In one series, the post-operative meningitis rate was 6.8%.

## PART F: INVESTIGATIONS SUMMARY FOR SKULL BASE PATHOLOGY

Imaging is essential for all skull base pathology. The four principal reasons for imaging are: screening, investigation of suspicious symptoms, surgical planning and navigation, and monitoring for recurrence.

- **MRI with gadolinium:** Investigation of choice for most skull base lesions; superior soft tissue differentiation; T2-weighted fast spin echo for screening
- **HRCT:** Essential for bony anatomy, erosion patterns, pneumatization; complementary to MRI
- **Diffusion-weighted MRI:** Differentiates malignant from benign lesions; useful for detecting recurrent tumour versus post-treatment changes
- **PET:** Identifies metabolically active tissue; differentiates post-therapeutic changes from residual disease
- **SPECT:** 50% more effective than CT at detecting skull base destruction; shows characteristic rapid wash-out in glomus tumours
- **MIBG/Octreotide scintigraphy:** Detects multiple paragangliomas and metastatic disease
- **Carotid angiography/CTA/MRA:** For vascular tumours; determines circle of Willis integrity and suitability for pre-operative embolization

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Wallenberg's syndrome** (lateral medullary infarction) is the most classical brainstem vascular syndrome -- characterized by severe vertigo, hiccoughs, ipsilateral Horner's, and contralateral spinothalamic loss in the limbs.
>
> - **MIBG or Indium-111-octreotide scintigraphy** facilitates detection of multiple paragangliomas or metastatic disease and should be considered in all patients with suspected glomus tumours.
>
> - **A unilateral OME in an adult** mandates examination of the nasopharynx with specific attention to the fossa of Rosenmuller -- this is a classic examination question and must not be missed in clinical practice.
>
> - **The rescue flap technique** preserves the nasoseptal flap pedicle during sphenoid opening and avoids donor site morbidity; the nasoseptal flap takedown technique allows re-use of a previously placed flap by incising over the sphenoid rostrum.
>
> - **Aspergillus SBO carries a worse prognosis** than Pseudomonas SBO and is more common in HIV/AIDS patients with CD4 counts less than 50 cells/mm -- positive Aspergillus cultures are a statistically significant negative prognostic factor.
>
> - **Cone-beam CT (CBCT)** provides higher spatial resolution of bony structures with 60% reduced radiation dose compared to conventional CT and is increasingly used in skull base imaging, though it lacks soft tissue contrast.
