## Introduction

The introduction of reconstructive techniques incorporating microvascular free tissue transfer has transformed the quality of life of patients undergoing head and neck surgery over the past 30 years. The microsurgeon's ability to transfer autologous tissues comprised of bone, functioning muscle, skin, or composites has dramatically improved functional outcomes for all head and neck reconstructive procedures — whether oncologic, congenital, or traumatic. The revolution in understanding blood supply to anatomical regions (angiosomes, perforasomes) has dramatically increased the number and variety of reconstructive options, demanding that microvascular surgeons acquire specialized training. Free flap success rates in experienced centres exceed 95%.

## Part A — Definitions and Basic Concepts

**Skin Flap:** A block of tissue transferred from a donor site to a recipient site while maintaining its own blood supply. Unlike a graft (which depends on the recipient bed for revascularization), a flap has an intrinsic vascular pedicle. A flap may consist of skin, subcutaneous fat, fascia, muscle, bone, nerve, or combinations thereof.

**Free Tissue Transfer (Free Flap):** Autologous tissue transfer from a distant donor site in which the pedicle is completely detached. Blood supply is reinstated by microsurgical anastomosis of donor artery and vein(s) to recipient artery and vein(s).

**Reconstructive Ladder:**
1. Secondary intention (healing by granulation)
2. Primary intention (direct closure)
3. Delayed primary closure
4. Skin grafts
5. Tissue expansion
6. Local tissue transfer (local flaps)
7. Free tissue transfer (top rung)

The **reconstructive elevator** concept recognizes that some rungs should be skipped — e.g., a neck defect exposing great vessels warrants a flap rather than a graft, even though a graft could technically "take."

### Advantages of Free Flaps over [[Pedicled Flaps Skin Grafts|Pedicled Flap]]s

| Feature | Free Flap | Pedicled Flap |
|---------|-----------|---------------|
| Tissue choice | Wide variety of donor sites; best tissue match to defect | Limited options based on arc of rotation |
| Volume and composition | Can include bone, skin, muscle in custom combinations | Bulky; limited tissue composition |
| Reliability | >95% success in experienced centres | Very reliable but limited reach |
| Functional outcome | Better speech, swallowing, aesthetics | Adequate coverage but often bulky |
| Donor site flexibility | Distant sites; less morbidity for some defects | Regional; chest/back morbidity |
| Two-team surgery | Simultaneous harvest and ablation | Often sequential |
| Sensate reconstruction | Possible (e.g., forearm with lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve) | Not usually possible |
| Limitation | Requires microsurgical expertise; longer operative time | Limited arc of rotation; bulk |

## Part B — Classification of Free Flaps

### By Tissue Composition

| Type | Description | Examples |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Cutaneous (fasciocutaneous) | Skin, fascia, subcutaneous fat | Radial forearm, lateral arm, ALT |
| Myocutaneous | Muscle with overlying skin paddle | Rectus abdominis, latissimus dorsi |
| Osseocutaneous | Bone with skin | Fibula, radial forearm (osseocutaneous), scapula |
| Myo-osseous | Muscle and bone | Scapula, iliac crest (DCIA) |
| Chimeric | Multiple tissue types on separate perforators from a single source vessel | Subscapular system (combined scapula/latissimus) |
| Visceral | Enteric tissue | Jejunal free flap |

### By Perforator Anatomy

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Muscle perforator flap | Skin/subcutaneous tissue supplied by a vessel traversing through muscle (e.g., DIEP, ALT) |
| Septal perforator flap | Skin supplied by a vessel passing through intermuscular septum (e.g., fibula skin paddle) |
| Direct perforator | Vessel perforates deep fascia only |

### Angiosomes of the Head and Neck

An **angiosome** is a three-dimensional composite block of tissue (skin, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, bone) supplied by a single source artery and its accompanying veins. Taylor and Palmer described this concept. Houseman et al. describe **13 angiosomes** in the head and neck region, mostly supplied from the external carotid, internal carotid, and subclavian arteries. All 13 but the lingual, vertebral, and ascending pharyngeal angiosomes contribute to the abundant subdermal vascular network.

Adjacent angiosomes are connected by:
- **True anastomoses** — full-calibre connections
- **Choke vessels** — reduced-calibre connections that define the limits of vascular territories
- **Oscillating veins** — bidirectional venous flow between territories

The **perforasome** concept (Saint-Cyr et al.) defines the vascular territory of a single perforator. Key findings:
- Each perforasome has a multidirectional, complex flow pattern
- Perforasomes are linked by direct and indirect linking vessels
- When a perforator flap is harvested, all other branches are ligated, causing hyperperfusion of the selected perforator and recruitment of additional linking vessels — allowing large flaps based on a single perforator

## Part C — Radial Artery Forearm Free Flap (RAFF)

### Anatomy

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Arterial supply | Radial artery |
| Venous drainage | Venae comitantes of radial artery or branches to cephalic vein |
| Sensory nerve | Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve of the forearm (sensate flap) |
| Course of artery | Between tendons of brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis (distal third); deep to brachioradialis (proximal forearm) |
| Skin supply | Perforating vessels extending vertically through overlying fascia |
| Skin island | Can be as large as entire volar surface of forearm |
| Bone (osseocutaneous variant) | Perforating vessels to periosteum of distal radius; max bone length 10–12 cm |
| Anatomy | Remarkably consistent; rare anatomic variations |

### Advantages

- Remarkably consistent surgical anatomy — easy to learn and harvest reliably
- Thin skin on distal forearm — ideal for oral cavity and tongue reconstruction
- Can be harvested as a **sensate flap** — most robust sensation of all cutaneous free flaps for H&N
- Positioning allows **simultaneous harvest** with ablative procedure
- Long vascular pedicle for distant recipient vessels
- Pliable — conforms well to 3D defects (oropharynx, soft palate)

### Disadvantages

- Limited tissue volume — inadequate for large defects with significant dead space
- Poor skin colour match (in Caucasians) for external defects
- Donor site requires skin graft — relatively unsightly scar on volar forearm
- Potential for wrist stiffness, cold sensitivity, tendon exposure
- Osseocutaneous variant: risk of distal radius fracture (take no more than one-third of radius width)

### Technique

- Harvested under tourniquet control (blood-free dissection)
- **Mandatory Allen's test** pre-operatively — confirms patent ulnar artery for hand perfusion
- Avoid injury to cutaneous branch of radial nerve
- Preserve paratenon over flexor carpi radialis for skin graft bed
- Split-thickness skin graft most commonly used for donor site
- Osseocutaneous variant: curve osteotomy (avoid perpendicular bone cuts — stress risers); consider prophylactic plating; avoid in osteoporotic elderly females

### Clinical Applications

| Site | Advantage |
|------|-----------|
| Oral tongue | Thin flap preserves mobility of tongue remnant; sensate reconstruction optimal |
| Floor of mouth | Pliable, thin tissue ideal |
| Buccal mucosa | Only cutaneous flap with appropriate thickness |
| Palate | Thinness + long pedicle reaching neck vessels |
| Oropharynx (tonsil-tongue base-soft palate) | Flexibility contours into complex 3D defects |
| Total lip reconstruction | Flap + tendon sling (palmaris longus) for functional lip |
| Total nasal reconstruction | Provides lining/infrastructure (multistage with forehead flap) |

## Part D — Free Fibula Flap

### Anatomy

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Arterial supply | Peroneal artery (branch of tibioperoneal trunk) |
| Venous drainage | Paired venae comitantes of peroneal artery |
| Artery course | Passes distally on medial aspect of fibula between tibialis posterior and flexor hallucis longus |
| Periosteal supply | Perforating branches from peroneal artery supply periosteum and nutrient artery to fibula |
| Skin supply | Septocutaneous perforators in distal half of leg passing through intermuscular septum between peroneus longus and soleus |
| Skin island | 12–16 cm x 8–10 cm (lateral lower leg) |
| Bone length | 18–24 cm available |
| Pedicle diameter | 3–4 mm artery; 4–5 mm vein |
| Pedicle length | Depends on bone length harvested (longer bone = shorter pedicle) |

### Advantages

- Ideal bone length for almost entire mandible reconstruction
- Skin island appropriate thickness for most oral/oropharyngeal reconstruction
- Can be osteotomized into multiple segments to recreate mandibular shape
- Long vascular pedicle — major asset in midface reconstruction
- "Double-barrelling" possible for increased vertical height (osseointegration)

### Disadvantages

- Not suitable in patients with **peripheral vascular disease** — harvesting a single vessel could be catastrophic
- Relatively high skin island failure rates (mitigated with careful technique)
- Donor site: delayed healing of skin graft in venous insufficiency patients
- Ankle/leg stiffness, occasional ankle instability
- Flexion deformity of great toe if large cuff of flexor hallucis harvested
- Short vertical height — disparity with natural mandibular height

### Technique

- Pre-operative **vascular imaging** (colour Doppler, MRA, or CTA) of leg vasculature is routine
- Doppler perforating branches to ensure skin island placement
- Harvested under tourniquet via **lateral approach**
- Peroneus longus and brevis reflected anteriorly off fibula
- Leave at least **6 cm of fibula at ankle and knee** to avoid instability and common peroneal nerve injury
- Harvest cuff of flexor hallucis longus and soleus to protect perforators
- Osteotomized with low-profile reconstruction plates
- Harvest from leg contralateral to mandibular defect for optimal pedicle orientation

### Clinical Applications

| Application | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| Oromandibular reconstruction | Benchmark for composite mandible/oral cavity defects |
| Maxillary reconstruction | Ideal for premaxilla/central maxillary defects; long pedicle |
| Double-barrelling | Segments stacked to maximize vertical height for osseointegration |

## Part E — Other Free Flaps in Head and Neck

| Flap | Source Vessel | Key Features |
|------|--------------|--------------|
| Anterolateral thigh (ALT) | Descending branch of lateral circumflex femoral artery | Versatile; thin/thick options; superior donor site vs forearm; replacing forearm in Asian centres |
| Lateral arm | Posterior radial collateral artery | Excellent facial skin colour match; inconspicuous donor; primary closure possible; small pedicle (1.5 mm) |
| Latissimus dorsi (free) | Thoracodorsal artery (from subscapular) | Large tissue volume; long pedicle >10 cm; reliable; useful for scalp, pharyngeal reconstruction |
| Scapular system (chimeric) | Subscapular artery and branches | Multiple tissue combinations from single source vessel |
| Rectus abdominis/DIEP | Deep inferior epigastric artery | Large skin territory; muscle perforator flap variant (DIEP) |
| Iliac crest (DCIA) | Deep circumflex iliac artery | Good bone stock for mandible; bulky; stiff |
| Jejunal free flap | Second intestinal branch (mesenteric) | Conduit reconstruction for total pharyngolaryngectomy; must be isoperistaltic |

## Part F — The Compromised Flap: Factors and Interventions

### Factors Contributing to Flap Failure

**Extrinsic Factors:**
- Compression: tight dressing, skin closure tension, angulation over rigid bony surface
- Haematoma or seroma beneath flap — separates from bed or compresses pedicle
- Pedicle kink or compression from bandages, tapes, tracheal straps
- Tight wound closure obstructing venous outflow

**Intrinsic Factors:**
- Comorbidities: smoking, diabetes mellitus (2.3× higher flap failure incidence), atherosclerosis, peripheral vascular disease, cachexia
- Vasospasm after flap raising (normal physiological response; usually self-resolving)
- No-reflow phenomenon: reperfusion generates oxygen-derived free radicals
- Endothelin-1 (vasoconstrictor peptide) elevated in ischaemic flap tissues
- Age per se is NOT a significant aggravating factor for free flap survival

### Timing and Patterns of Failure

| Finding | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Most common cause | Vascular occlusion (4.5% arterial, 6.8% venous) |
| Venous > arterial | Venous thrombosis more common (low flow, technically difficult anastomosis) |
| Most failures | Within first 36 hours |
| Most common correctable cause | Compression bandages, tapes, tracheal straps compressing pedicle |
| Salvage rate | 50–80% (dependent on timeliness of detection and intervention) |

### Recipient Vessels in Head and Neck

| Arteries | Veins |
|----------|-------|
| Facial artery (most common) | Internal jugular vein (end-to-side) |
| Superior thyroid artery | External jugular vein |
| Transverse cervical artery | Transverse cervical veins |

### Interventions to Improve Flap Survival

| Intervention | Mechanism |
|--------------|-----------|
| Prompt release of dressings/sutures | Relieves extrinsic compression |
| Haematoma drainage | Removes source of compression and biochemical injury |
| Urgent pedicle exploration | Early re-exploration for venous/arterial occlusion |
| Medicinal leeches | Effective for venous congestion (Hirudo medicinalis) |
| Localized fibrinolytic agents | Perfused locally (not systemically) to improve salvage of venous occlusion |
| Topical nitroglycerin (GTN) | Vasodilator; greater effect on venous than arterial circulation |
| DMSO (dimethylsulfoxide) | Only substance with RCT evidence of improved human mastectomy flap survival |
| Heparin lumen irrigation | Prevents intraluminal clotting during anastomosis |
| Specialized nursing observation | Best practice for flap monitoring (percutaneous Doppler + specialized nursing units) |

### Pharmacological Research (Experimental)

| Agent | Mechanism | Status |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| Superoxide dismutase | Free radical scavenger | Improved animal flap survival; not shown in humans |
| Deferoxamine (iron chelator) | Reduces ROS via iron chelation | Beneficial but toxic; conjugated form DFO-H less efficacious |
| Allopurinol | Xanthine oxidase inhibitor | Animal benefit; not proven in humans |
| Nifedipine/Verapamil | Calcium channel blockers — smooth muscle relaxation | Animal studies; topical use on anastomosis only |
| Tezosentan | Endothelin receptor blocker — improves venous outflow | Experimental (porcine TRAM flaps) |
| VEGF gene therapy | Angiogenesis promotion | Experimental; reduced necrotic zones in rat models |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. A free flap is autologous tissue completely detached from its donor site with blood supply reinstated by microsurgical anastomosis to recipient vessels; success rates exceed 95% in experienced centres
> 2. Free flaps are classified by composition (cutaneous, myocutaneous, osseocutaneous, myo-osseous, chimeric, visceral) and by perforator anatomy (muscle perforator, septal perforator, direct)
> 3. Angiosomes are 3D tissue blocks supplied by a single source artery; 13 angiosomes exist in the head and neck; adjacent territories are connected by choke vessels and true anastomoses
> 4. The radial forearm free flap is based on the radial artery with venae comitantes; requires mandatory Allen's test; thin skin ideal for oral cavity; can be harvested as a sensate flap with lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve
> 5. The free fibula flap (peroneal artery) provides 18–24 cm of bone for mandibular reconstruction; septocutaneous perforators supply the skin paddle; leave at least 6 cm of fibula at ankle and knee
> 6. Venous occlusion (6.8%) is more common than arterial (4.5%) as a cause of free flap failure; the majority of failures occur within the first 36 hours; salvage rates are 50–80%
> 7. The most common correctable cause of flap failure is extrinsic compression from bandages, tapes, or tracheal straps compressing the vascular pedicle
> 8. Diabetes mellitus increases flap failure incidence 2.3-fold, while age per se is NOT an independent risk factor for free flap survival
> 9. DMSO is the only substance with RCT evidence of improved human flap survival; medicinal leeches and localized fibrinolytic agents are widely used for venous occlusion salvage
> 10. Recipient vessels in H&N include facial artery, superior thyroid artery, and transverse cervical artery; most surgeons advocate end-to-side venous anastomosis to the internal jugular vein


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Allen's test is mandatory before forearm flap harvest** — failure to confirm ulnar artery patency risks hand ischaemia; the ulnar artery is usually the dominant supply to the deep palmar arch
> - **"It is better to have a live flap out of position than a dead flap in position"** — if a local flap is compromised, reposition it back on the donor site to restore circulation
> - **Harvest fibula from the contralateral leg to the mandibular defect** — this positions the pedicle correctly for recipient vessel access and keeps the skin island oriented for oral cavity insetting
> - **Gillies' 15th principle: "The aftercare is as important as the planning"** — most flap failures result from failure to recognize compromised circulation in the first 36 hours; specialized nursing care is the most important monitoring modality
> - **PMMC is highly reliable even when the skin paddle fails** — underlying muscle usually survives and can be covered with a skin graft; always consider designing incisions to preserve a secondary deltopectoral flap option
> - **Excessive volume replacement paradoxically increases flap loss** — congestive heart failure and sequelae from over-resuscitation cause more flap failures than hypovolemia
