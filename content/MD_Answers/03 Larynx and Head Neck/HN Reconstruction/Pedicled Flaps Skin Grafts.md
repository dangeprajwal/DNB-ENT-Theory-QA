# Pedicled Flaps and Skin Grafts in Head and Neck Reconstruction

## Introduction

While free tissue transfer has become the gold standard for complex head and neck reconstruction, pedicled flaps and skin grafts remain essential components of the reconstructive armamentarium. Skin grafts provide coverage for wounds lacking the complexity that warrants a flap, and an understanding of graft healing biology is fundamental to reconstruction. Pedicled flaps, particularly the pectoralis major myocutaneous (PMMC) flap and deltopectoral (DP) flap, continue to serve as workhorses for H&N reconstruction and as reliable salvage options when free tissue transfer fails or is contraindicated. Knowledge of axial flap anatomy is essential for every head and neck surgeon.

## Part A — Skin Grafts: Types and Principles of Graft Uptake

### Definition

A **graft** is a piece of tissue transferred from one site to another, devascularizing it in the process. Its survival depends on gaining a blood supply from the recipient bed. A **flap**, by contrast, has its own blood supply.

### Classification

**By Source:**

| Type | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| Autograft | Graft from one part of the individual transferred to another part |
| Isograft | Between genetically identical individuals (identical twins) |
| Allograft | Between individuals of the same species |
| Xenograft | Between different species |

**By Composition:** Skin, bone, cartilage, fat, mucosa, or composite grafts (two or more tissue types, e.g., septal mucosal graft).

**Skin Grafts — By Thickness:**

| Type | Content | Properties |
|------|---------|------------|
| Split-thickness (STSG) | Epidermis + variable amount of dermis (thin/intermediate/thick) | More likely to take; more secondary contraction; abnormal pigmentation; hairless; poorer sensory recovery |
| Full-thickness (FTSG) | Epidermis + all dermis | Better colour match; less secondary contraction; more robust; better sensory recovery; contains hair follicles; requires better vascularized bed |

### Stages of Skin Graft Healing ("Take")

| Stage | Time Period | Mechanism |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| 1. Graft adherence | Immediate | Graft adheres to recipient bed through formation of fibrin layer |
| 2. Serum imbibition | First 24–48 hours | Graft survives via absorption of nutrients from plasma leaking from recipient bed capillaries; graft gains weight and swells |
| 3. Revascularization | 48 hours to 3 weeks | Three processes: **Inosculation** (joining of cut vessel ends in graft and bed); **Revascularization** (ingrowth of new vessels into graft); **Neovascularization** (ingrowth down basement membrane of degenerated old vessels) |
| 4. Remodelling | 3 weeks to 1 year | Maturation and remodelling with collagen reorganization and increasing strength |

### Contraction

- **Primary contraction** (immediate on harvest) — due to elastin within dermis; greater in FTSGs
- **Secondary contraction** (after revascularization) — due to myofibroblast activity in wound bed; greater in STSGs

### Factors Affecting Graft Take

**Local Factors:**
- Vascularity of recipient bed: healthy granulation tissue, muscle, and fascia accept grafts readily; fat is less suitable; exposed bone/tendon will NOT accept a graft (unless periosteum present or bone burred to cause pinpoint bleeding)
- Contact with recipient bed: graft must be immobilized, in direct contact; shearing forces must be avoided
- **Most frequent cause of graft loss**: haematoma or seroma separating graft from bed
- Meticulous haemostasis; mesh or fenestrate graft to allow fluid escape
- Infection: wounds with >10⁵ organisms will not support a graft; common pathogens include Streptococcus pyogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, MRSA

**Systemic Factors:**
- Diabetes mellitus, smoking, previous [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]], chemotherapy, nutritional status

### Donor Sites

| STSG | FTSG |
|------|------|
| Thigh, trunk, buttocks | Pre-/post-auricular, supraclavicular fossa, antecubital fossa, groin |
| Heals by re-epithelialization from adnexal structures (14–21 days) | Requires direct primary closure (limits size) |
| Harvested with dermatome (0.3–0.4 mm / 10–12 thousandths of an inch) | Harvested with scalpel |

## Part B — Axial Flaps in Head and Neck Reconstruction

An **axial flap** is based on a named arteriovenous pedicle running within the skin superficial to muscle, parallel to the overlying skin. Because the blood supply is determined by the vascular territory (not just length:breadth ratio), axial flaps can be raised much longer than random flaps.

### Enumeration of Axial Flaps in H&N

**Local Axial Flaps:**

| Flap | Blood Supply | Application |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| Median forehead flap | Supratrochlear artery | Nasal reconstruction (external nose) |
| Nasolabial flap | Distal branches of facial artery | Anterior floor of mouth, gingiva, nasal alar |
| Submental island flap | Submental branch of facial artery | Facial skin, intraoral lining; excellent facial colour match |
| FAMM flap (Facial Artery Myomucosal) | Facial artery (antegrade or retrograde) | Small oral mucosal defects, lip, floor of mouth, palate |
| Temporoparietal fascial flap | Superficial temporal artery | Orbital, auricular reconstruction, microtia repair |
| Glabellar flap | Supratrochlear artery | Small defects around nose/medial cheek |

**Distant Axial Flaps:**

| Flap | Blood Supply | Application |
|------|--------------|-------------|
| Deltopectoral (DP) flap | Internal mammary artery perforators (upper 3–4) | Anterior neck skin, lower face, pharyngeal fistula repair |
| Supraclavicular artery island (SAI) flap | Branch of transverse cervical artery | External skin defects neck to parotid; oral/oropharyngeal |
| Cervical-facial skin flaps | Random or named cervical vessels | Lower face, cheek skin defects |

**Myocutaneous Axial Distant Flaps:**

| Flap | Blood Supply | Vascular Pattern |
|------|--------------|-----------------|
| Pectoralis major (PMMC) | Pectoral branch of acromiothoracic artery | Type V (dominant + secondary segmental) |
| Latissimus dorsi | Thoracodorsal artery (from subscapular) | Type V |
| Trapezius | Transverse cervical artery | Type II (dominant + minor pedicles) |
| Sternocleidomastoid | Superior thyroid, occipital, suprascapular arteries | Type II |
| Platysma | Submental and facial arteries | Type II |

## Part C — Deltopectoral (DP) Flap

### History and Anatomy

Described by **Bakamjian and Littlewood in 1964**, the DP flap is an axial pattern flap on the anterior chest wall.

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Blood supply | Upper 3–4 perforating branches of the internal mammary artery emerging through medial intercostal spaces |
| Boundaries | Clavicle (superior), acromion (lateral), line through anterior axillary fold to above nipple (inferior) |
| Territory | Perforator vascular system extends to deltopectoral groove; extension beyond this is random and may fail |
| Anomalous pivot point | At the upper medial end of the flap (not lower); considerable lower border laxity with arm abduction |

### Technique

- Elevation begins laterally
- Pectoral fascia is left on the flap; muscle fibres below are left bare
- Monopolar diathermy used judiciously (could damage flap and compromise skin graft take on muscle)
- Must NOT be doubled back on itself during retraction (risk of buttonholing)
- Donor site usually covered with split-thickness skin graft

### Clinical Applications

- One-stage reconstruction of anterior neck skin
- Bridge reconstruction (pedicle divided at 3 weeks after take occurs)
- Large defects of lower face and upper neck — pedicle can be "waltzed"
- Repair of pharyngeal fistula (but muscle bulk usually preferred — other flaps better)
- Salvage flap when PMMC or [[Free Flaps HN Reconstruction|free flap]] fails

## Part D — Pectoralis Major Myocutaneous (PMMC) Flap

### History

First described by **Ariyan in the late 1970s**; remains one of the most important pedicled flaps in H&N reconstruction.

### Anatomy

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Origin | Clavicle, sternum, slips from upper 7 ribs, variable external oblique aponeurosis |
| Insertion | Bicipital groove of humerus |
| Three segments | Clavicular, sternocostal, external (most lateral) |
| Dominant blood supply | Pectoral branch of acromiothoracic artery (from 1st part of axillary artery) |
| Surface marking of hilum | 2–3 cm medial to coracoid process |
| Pedicle course | Runs on deep surface of muscle, downward and medially, giving off branches |
| Secondary supply | Internal mammary artery perforators (sternocostal segment), lateral thoracic artery (external segment) |
| Vascular pattern | **Type V** (one dominant pedicle + secondary segmental pedicles) |
| Arc of rotation | Up to zygomatic arch |

### Advantages

- Large skin territory (entire skin overlying muscle can be raised)
- Rich vascular supply; highly reliable
- Can be transferred without prior delay
- Large arc of rotation (up to zygomatic arch)
- Harvested in supine position
- Multiple configurations: muscle-only, skin + muscle, or double epithelial surfaces with de-epithelialized segment
- Primary donor site closure easily achieved
- Even if skin paddle fails, underlying muscle usually survives (granulation + skin graft)

### Disadvantages

- Large bulky muscle — relatively immobile; best for filling defects where mobility not paramount
- Violates breast in females (latissimus dorsi is alternative)
- Random distal extension beyond muscle edge unreliable (max 3–4 cm with rectus sheath)
- Cannot reliably be tubed for pharyngeal reconstruction

### Technique (Step-by-Step)

| Step | Detail |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Mark surface landmarks: acromion → xiphoid line; perpendicular from sternal notch; bisection point = pedicle location |
| 2 | Mark clavipectoral fascia (two-thirds along clavicle from sternal notch to coracoid) |
| 3 | Design skin paddle between lateral sternum (medially) and nipple (laterally); at ~6th rib level for thin flap |
| 4 | Extend incision into axilla for access |
| 5 | Start dissection inferiorly; incise to muscle; define inferior and lateral borders |
| 6 | Divide inferior muscle attachments from ribs/rectus sheath to access subpectoral plane |
| 7 | Mobilize upward; identify pectoralis minor muscle beneath |
| 8 | Place dermal-muscle sutures if skin paddle thick/mobile (prevents shearing) |
| 9 | Identify and ligate lateral thoracic artery to increase arc of rotation |
| 10 | Divide sternal insertion vertically (DO NOT curve medially or you may cut the pedicle) |
| 11 | Rotate flap; vascular pedicle clearly identified; divide sternal edge up to clavicle |
| 12 | Close donor site primarily (two-layer) with drains; design incisions to preserve secondary DP flap option |

### Key Pitfalls

- Congenital absence of pectoralis major (1:11,000; sternocostal head absence = Poland syndrome)
- Check for lateral thoracic artery contribution in ~50% of cases (external segment may be exclusively supplied by this vessel)
- In females: use inframammary incision; avoid vertical scars (breast deformity)

### Clinical Applications of PMMC

| Application | Detail |
|-------------|--------|
| Oral cavity reconstruction | Bulky tissue fills large defects; tongue base, floor of mouth |
| Neck wound coverage | Muscle-only flap covers exposed great vessels after [[Neck Dissection|neck dissection]] |
| Pharyngocutaneous fistula repair | Well-vascularized tissue closure |
| Salvage after free flap failure | Reliable backup option |
| Double skin paddle | Inner oral lining + external cover (with de-epithelialized bridge) |

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Skin grafts heal in four stages: adherence (fibrin, immediate), serum imbibition (nutrients from bed, 24–48 hrs), revascularization (inosculation + new vessel ingrowth, 48 hrs–3 weeks), and remodelling (collagen reorganization, 3 weeks–1 year)
> 2. The most frequent cause of graft loss is haematoma or seroma separating graft from bed; infection (>10⁵ organisms) is another common cause — Streptococcus pyogenes, Pseudomonas, and MRSA destroy fibrin and prevent adherence
> 3. STSGs undergo more secondary contraction but have a higher chance of successful take; FTSGs provide better colour match, robustness, and sensory recovery but require a better vascular bed
> 4. An axial flap is based on a named arteriovenous pedicle and can be raised much longer than a random flap (which relies on unnamed vessels in the base and has a maximum 1:1 or 1:1.5 length:breadth ratio in H&N)
> 5. The deltopectoral flap is based on upper 3–4 perforating branches of the internal mammary artery; has an anomalous pivot point at the upper medial end; vascular territory extends to the deltopectoral groove
> 6. The PMMC flap is supplied by the pectoral branch of the acromiothoracic artery; it has a Type V vascular pattern with segmental secondary supply from internal mammary perforators
> 7. The surface marking for the PMMC pedicle is where a perpendicular from the sternal notch meets a line from the acromion to the xiphoid process; the vascular hilum lies 2–3 cm medial to the coracoid process
> 8. When raising the PMMC, divide the sternal insertion vertically — never curve medially or you risk cutting the pedicle (the "ultimate disaster")
> 9. The PMMC flap can reach the zygomatic arch; can be modified to muscle-only, single or double skin paddle; even when the skin paddle fails, the underlying muscle usually survives
> 10. In females, use the inframammary incision for PMMC to minimize breast deformity; always design incisions preserving a potential secondary deltopectoral flap from the same side


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - **Meshing or fenestrating a skin graft is essential** — allows fluid drainage from beneath the graft, preventing haematoma/seroma; tie-over dressings ensure immobilization and contact
> - **FTSGs for facial reconstruction; STSGs for large non-cosmetic defects** — FTSG donor sites (pre-/post-auricular, supraclavicular fossa) provide best colour match for facial skin
> - **The DP flap remains a valuable salvage option** — when PMMC or free flap fails, the DP can provide neck/lower face coverage; always consider preserving DP flap option when designing PMMC incisions
> - **The nasolabial flap is a workhorse for small anterior oral cavity defects** — inferiorly based on facial artery branches, extremely reliable, with inconspicuous donor (especially in elderly with rhytids)
> - **Minimum skin paddle for PMMC is 5 × 3 cm** (palm size) — smaller paddles may not capture enough musculocutaneous perforators and risk failure
> - **The supraclavicular artery island flap is emerging as a versatile option** — broad application from neck base to parotid; however, venous congestion and failure rates higher than free tissue transfer
