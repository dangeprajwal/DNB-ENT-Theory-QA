# Transoral Endoscopic Laser Resection of Glottic Carcinoma

**Question:** *What are the indications and contraindications of transoral endoscopic laser resection of glottic carcinoma? Describe the same procedure and its complications.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 14 (Tumours of the Larynx), Chapter 27 (Open Conservation Surgery for Laryngeal Cancer)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) was introduced by Strong and Jako in the early 1970s using the CO2 laser for laryngeal microsurgery. Subsequently, Steiner and Ambrosch developed a systematic approach to endoscopic transoral laser resection of laryngeal tumours. The European Laryngological Society (ELS) classification system standardized the extent of endoscopic resection. TLM has now largely replaced open partial laryngectomy for early laryngeal cancers due to lower morbidity, better voice quality, better swallowing, and lower cost.

---

## PART A: INDICATIONS

### Primary Indications

| T Stage | TLM Role |
|---------|---------|
| T1a glottic | FIRST choice treatment |
| T1b glottic | First choice (equal to RT), based on patient choice and expertise |
| T2a glottic | First choice (equal to RT) |
| T2b glottic | Second choice (after CRT) |
| T1-T2 supraglottic | First choice (equal to RT/TORS) |

### Specific Indications
- Early glottic cancer (T1-T2) where adequate transoral exposure is possible
- Laryngeal dysplasia: recurrent focal severe dysplasia managed as T1 carcinoma with excision
- Selected T3 laryngeal cancers in expert centres (experience is relatively immature)
- Selected radiorecurrent laryngeal cancers (though local control rates may be inferior to OPL in this setting)

### Advantages of TLM Over Other Modalities

| Factor | TLM | OPL | (C)RT |
|--------|-----|-----|-------|
| Speech quality | Good | Poor | Good |
| Swallowing | Normal | Normal | Impaired |
| Feeding tube | No | Temporary (1 month) | 5-10% |
| [[Tracheostomy|Tracheostomy]] | No | Temporary (1 month) | Occasional |
| Treatment duration | 1 day | 1 day | 7 weeks |
| Cost | Low | Medium | Expensive |
| Repeat treatment | Yes (repeat laser possible) | No (only salvage TL or RT) | No (only salvage TL) |

## PART B: CONTRAINDICATIONS

### Absolute Contraindications
- Inadequate transoral exposure (anatomical constraints preventing visualization of the tumour)
- T4 disease with extralaryngeal spread (tumour extending beyond laryngeal framework)
- Tumour extending to prevertebral space, mediastinum, or encasing carotid artery

### Relative Contraindications
- Anterior commissure involvement with deep extension (though systematic reviews show all three modalities have similar results with AC involvement)
- Bilateral arytenoid involvement
- Subglottic extension >5 mm posteriorly or >10 mm anteriorly
- Gross thyroid cartilage invasion
- Pyriform sinus apex involvement
- Impaired cord mobility (T2b) — reduced local control, akin to T3 with fixation
- Lack of local expertise in TLM (should only be performed by trained surgeons in appropriately equipped centres)
- Patients with poor pulmonary reserve (though less relevant than for OPL)

## PART C: PROCEDURE

### Pre-operative Assessment
- Flexible nasal laryngoscopy: assess cord mobility, tumour extent, panoramic view
- Cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI): assess cartilage invasion, paraglottic space, pre-epiglottic space
- Assessment under GA: rigid endoscopy to determine transoral accessibility; anaesthetist assessment for jet ventilation

### Equipment
- CO2 laser: preferred laser type due to minimal penetration depth, reducing collateral tissue damage
- Microlaryngoscope with suspension system
- Operating microscope
- Jet ventilation capability (high-frequency or low-frequency)
- Microlaryngeal instruments

### Surgical Technique

**Step 1: Exposure**
- General anaesthesia with jet ventilation (avoids endotracheal tube obstruction)
- Suspension microlaryngoscopy for optimal bimanual access
- Operating microscope for magnified view

**Step 2: Assessment**
- Determine extent of tumour endoscopically
- Palpation of the vocal cord to assess depth of invasion
- Plan cordectomy type according to ELS classification (Types I-VI)

**Step 3: Resection**
- CO2 laser used for precise cutting with minimal thermal damage
- Resection depth determined by tumour extent:
  - Type I (subepithelial): through superficial lamina propria
  - Type II (subligamental): through Reinke's space and vocal ligament
  - Type III (transmuscular): through vocalis muscle
  - Type IV (total): to internal thyroid perichondrium
  - Types Va-Vd (extended): includes contralateral cord/arytenoid/subglottis/ventricle
  - Type VI: anterior commissurectomy
- Laser ablation alone (surface destruction without excision) is DISCOURAGED — no specimen for diagnosis

**Step 4: Specimen Management**
- All specimens must be orientated and mounted (e.g. on dehydrated cucumber) for histological analysis
- Intra-operative photographs before and after surgery
- Margin assessment: pathologist specifies sites where margins are <1 mm on orientated specimen

### Post-resection Protocol (Box 14.1)
1. If margins clear by ≥1 mm → routine clinical monitoring (no second look)
2. If surgical margins adequate but histology shows tumour at margins → second look at 6-8 weeks
3. If surgeon has margin concerns and residual tumour confirmed → consider further TLM resection or discuss alternative treatment at MDT

## PART D: COMPLICATIONS

### Intra-operative Complications
- Airway fire (rare but catastrophic) — risk with CO2 laser and oxygen-enriched environment
- Haemorrhage — generally rare with CO2 laser (good haemostasis)
- Teeth damage from laryngoscope
- Inability to achieve adequate exposure → may need to abort procedure

### Post-operative Complications
- Dysphonia — degree depends on cordectomy type (Type I has least impact; Type IV-V has most)
- Laryngeal web formation — especially after anterior commissure resection; silastic keel may be used
- Granuloma formation
- Oedema — usually mild and self-limiting
- Aspiration — rare for glottic resections; more common for supraglottic resections

### Long-term Complications
- Voice quality alteration — breathy voice and wider glottal gap compared to RT
- Laryngeal stenosis (rare)
- Need for further treatment — local recurrence rates comparable to other modalities

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. TLM was introduced by Strong and Jako (1972) using CO2 laser; Steiner and Ambrosch systematized the approach
> 2. TLM is FIRST choice for T1a glottic cancer; equal to RT for T1b and T2a
> 3. CO2 laser is preferred due to minimal penetration depth and reduced collateral tissue damage
> 4. Laser ablation (surface destruction) is DISCOURAGED — no specimen for histological diagnosis
> 5. Vocal cord stripping is NOT recommended — high risk of vocal cord damage
> 6. All specimens must be orientated and mounted for histological analysis; margin sites <1 mm should be specified
> 7. If margins clear ≥1 mm: routine follow-up; tumour at margins: second look at 6-8 weeks
> 8. TLM has LOWER morbidity than OPL: no feeding tube, no tracheostomy, better voice quality
> 9. TLM allows repeat laser resection for recurrence — unlike RT or OPL (only salvage TL possible)
> 10. Inadequate transoral exposure is the most common reason TLM cannot be performed


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Voice quality after TLM for early glottic cancer is similar or superior to RT and significantly better than OPL
> - A randomized trial showed overall voice quality between TLM and RT was rated similar, but voice was more "breathy" and glottal gap wider after TLM
> - Jet ventilation is often needed during TLM — assessment under GA should include an anaesthetist familiar with this technique
> - Anterior commissure involvement does NOT absolutely contraindicate TLM — systematic reviews show similar outcomes across all three modalities (TLM, OPL, RT)
> - For supraglottic TLM, post-operative swallowing support must be in place as swallowing dysfunction is expected
