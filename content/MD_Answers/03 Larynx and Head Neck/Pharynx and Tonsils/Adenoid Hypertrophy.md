# Adenoid Hypertrophy

**Question:** *Discuss the clinical features and investigations for adenoid hypertrophy. What are the non-surgical treatment options? Enumerate the indications, techniques and complications of adenoidectomy.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Volume 2, Chapter 26 (The Adenoid and Adenoidectomy)

---

## Introduction

Adenoidal hyperplasia in childhood is common and usually self-limiting. The adenoid, a midline mass of lymphoid tissue in the nasopharynx, forms part of Waldeyer's ring and plays a role in developing both local and systemic immunity. The adenoid may be implicated in upper respiratory tract disease through partial or complete nasal obstruction or as a result of acting as a reservoir for pathogenic bacteria. Pathological manifestations include rhinitis, rhinosinusitis, otitis media and [[OME Pathophysiology|otitis media with effusion]].

---

## PART A: Clinical Features

### Obstructive Symptoms

- Nasal obstruction and mouth breathing
- Snoring and sleep-disordered breathing
- [[Obstructive Sleep Apnoea|Obstructive sleep apnoea]] (depressed PaO2, elevated PaCO2)
- Hyponasal speech (rhinolalia clausa)
- Feeding difficulties in infants

### Infective Symptoms

- Recurrent rhinosinusitis (adenoid acts as bacterial reservoir)
- Chronic post-nasal drip
- Chronic cough
- Recurrent otitis media with effusion (OME)
- Chronic adenoiditis (acute or chronic)

### Systemic Effects

- Failure to thrive and growth retardation — adeno[[Tonsillectomy|tonsillectomy]] results in a significant increase in serum IGF-1, accounting for the growth spurt frequently observed post-operatively
- Neurobehavioural morbidity and poor academic performance — habitual snoring, even short of OSA, has been associated with hyperactive behaviour
- Adenoidal facies (long face, open mouth, narrow maxilla) in chronic mouth breathers

### Associated Conditions

- Otitis media with effusion — adenoid size alone does not account for benefit of adenoidectomy; chronic inflammation and biofilm infection are the likely mechanisms
- Reduced olfactory sensitivity — both physical obstruction and olfactory epithelial changes contribute
- Neoplasia — rare but Non-Hodgkin lymphoma has been reported; persistent asymmetric enlargement should prompt imaging and biopsy

---

## PART B: Investigations

### Clinical Examination

- Assessment of external nose for supratip skin crease (frequent nose rubbing from rhinitis)
- Anterior rhinoscopy with halogen light otoscope and large speculum
- Cold tongue depressor or large laryngeal mirror to assess nasal airway

### Nasendoscopy

Flexible paediatric nasendoscopy is a highly accurate method to assess adenoidal status in the outpatient setting. The adenoid is classified based on size and obstruction:

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| Grade I | Adenoid filling one-third of choanae |
| Grade II | Adenoid filling one-third to two-thirds of choanae |
| Grade III | Two-thirds to near-complete obstruction |
| Grade IV | Complete choanal obstruction |

### Lateral Soft-Tissue X-ray

When endoscopy is not tolerated, a lateral soft-tissue radiograph of the nasopharynx correlates well with endoscopic assessment. The adenoid-nasopharyngeal ratio can be calculated.

### Other Investigations

- **Audiometry and tympanometry** — if OME is suspected
- **Polysomnography** — if obstructive sleep apnoea is clinically suspected
- **Sickle-cell and thalassaemia screening** — as appropriate
- Routine pre-operative investigations are NOT indicated for ASA grade 1 or 2 children

---

## PART C: Non-Surgical Treatment

### Topical Nasal Steroids

There is reasonable evidence (meta-analysis of multiple RCTs) that topical nasal steroid sprays can cause reduction in adenoid size with improvements in:

- Presence of middle ear fluid
- Audiometric thresholds
- Nasal obstruction and rhinorrhoea
- Cough, snoring and sleep apnoea

However, the trials were judged to be of poor quality and further, better-quality studies are required. Topical nasal steroids will probably find a role in clinical practice, although that role is currently unclear.

### Watchful Waiting

Mild obstructive symptoms from adenoidal hyperplasia are not an indication for surgery. The adenoid tends to regress during late childhood and parents should be made aware of this.

---

## PART D: Adenoidectomy

### Indications

- **Upper airway obstruction** — significant obstructive symptoms resulting in sleep-disordered breathing with effects on daytime behaviour and cognition
- **Otitis media with effusion** — adjuvant adenoidectomy is effective in children over 3 years undergoing ventilation tube insertion (reduces need for reinsertion by absolute risk reduction of 21%)
- **Recurrent or [[CRS Nasal Polyposis Turbinate|chronic rhinosinusitis]]** — adenoid acts as bacterial reservoir; adenoidectomy improves symptoms in a high proportion
- **Chronic adenoiditis** — as a distinct infective entity

### Techniques

**1. Curettage adenoidectomy (most common in UK — 79.2%):**
- Blind procedure with digital palpation
- Disadvantages: unpredictable bleeding, poor access to choanal adenoid, risk of trauma to Eustachian cushions

**2. Suction diathermy under direct vision (preferred):**
- Mean blood loss 4 mL vs 50 mL with curettage
- Provides direct vision, haemostasis, and negligible risk of post-operative haemorrhage
- Allows partial adenoidectomy — leaving a ridge of tissue at the velopharyngeal junction to avoid VPI

**3. Microdebrider:** 20% faster than curettage; higher unit cost

**4. Coblation:** Less blood loss and more complete adenoid removal; cost limits its use as a sole procedure

**Meta-analysis conclusion:** Suction coagulation adenoidectomy has reduced intra-operative bleeding, reduced operative time, and a lower overall complication rate compared to curettage.

### Complications

- **Haemorrhage:** Reactionary rate <0.7%; managed with early return to theatre and postnasal packing for 4 hours
- **Secondary haemorrhage:** Rare; may indicate a clotting or coagulation defect
- **Velopharyngeal insufficiency:** Estimated 1 in 1500 to 1 in 10,000; mandatory to assess palate and uvula for submucous cleft before surgery; long-term VPI is rare
- **Dental trauma:** Particularly at risk are secondary incisors; use adult gag to avoid
- **Retained swab:** Mandatory to confirm swab count before removing gag
- **Coroner's clot:** Blood pooling and clotting in nasopharynx — must suction nasopharynx before removing gag
- **Grisel syndrome:** Non-traumatic atlantoaxial subluxation; post-operative torticollis should raise suspicion
- **Nasopharyngeal stenosis:** Reported with KTP laser (not with other techniques)
- **Regrowth:** 1.6% required reoperation following curettage; direct-vision techniques minimize this
- **Infection:** Foetor common post-operatively; azithromycin 10 mg/kg for 3 days reduces this
- **Death:** Rare; ultra-rapid codeine metabolizers at risk — avoid opioids in young children with sleep-disordered breathing

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Adenoidal hyperplasia is common and self-limiting — mild obstructive symptoms alone are not an indication for surgery
> 2. The benefit of adenoidectomy in OME is due to removal of biofilm infection rather than physical obstruction of the Eustachian tube
> 3. Adjuvant adenoidectomy in children over 3 years with OME reduces the need for reinsertion of ventilation tubes by 21%
> 4. Topical nasal steroids can reduce adenoid size and improve symptoms but the evidence quality is poor
> 5. Suction diathermy under direct vision is the preferred technique — mean blood loss 4 mL vs 50 mL with curettage
> 6. Submucous cleft palate (bifid uvula, zona pellucida, hard palate notch) must be excluded before adenoidectomy to avoid VPI
> 7. Partial adenoidectomy preserving tissue at the velopharyngeal junction reduces VPI risk in at-risk children
> 8. The adenoid grading system (Grades I–IV) is based on percentage of choanal obstruction on nasendoscopy
> 9. Routine pre-operative investigations are not indicated for ASA grade 1 or 2 children undergoing adenoidectomy
> 10. Down syndrome children are at increased risk of atlantoaxial subluxation — pre-operative plain radiographs have limited value below age 3


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Adenoid size is not significantly different between children with and without OME — it is the inflammatory/biofilm process, not obstruction, that drives middle ear disease
> - Habitual snoring short of OSA may result in neurobehavioural morbidity and poor academic performance — adenotonsillectomy is beneficial
> - The growth spurt after adenotonsillectomy is explained by the significant increase in serum IGF-1 levels following relief of airway obstruction
> - Always exclude post-transplantation lymphoproliferative disorder when symptoms of nasal obstruction develop in a transplant recipient
> - It is no longer appropriate to combine adenoidectomy with tonsillectomy routinely — a specific indication for each procedure must exist
