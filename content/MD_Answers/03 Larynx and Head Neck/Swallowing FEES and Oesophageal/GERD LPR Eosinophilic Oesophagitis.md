# Gastro-oesophageal Reflux, Laryngopharyngeal Reflux and Eosinophilic Oesophagitis

**Question:** *Discuss GERD in the context of ENT practice. What is laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR)? Differentiate LPR from GERD. Describe the pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnosis and management of LPR. Discuss eosinophilic oesophagitis.*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 3, Chapter 53 (Oesophageal Diseases), Chapter 77 (Reflux Disease)

---

## INTRODUCTION

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) and laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) are distinct but overlapping clinical entities of relevance to the ENT surgeon. GORD involves acid-mediated damage to the resilient oesophageal squamous epithelium, typically occurring in the supine position. LPR, first described by Koufman in 1991, involves pepsin-mediated damage to the far less resilient laryngeal and pharyngeal mucosa, often occurring in the upright position via gaseous reflux containing a fine aerosol of droplets. Although GORD and LPR may exist independently, as disease severity increases the likelihood of both types being present increases. Eosinophilic oesophagitis is an increasingly recognized condition causing oesophageal dysmotility with eosinophilic infiltration of the oesophageal epithelium.

---

## PART A: GORD — ENT RELEVANCE

### Presentation

- **Classical symptoms:** Retrosternal pain, heartburn, dyspepsia, waterbrash, regurgitation, vomiting — typically after meals or lying supine
- **Atypical/ENT symptoms:** Voice changes, coughing, sore throat, throat clearing, sinusitis — caused by proximal reflux and micro-aspiration
- **Risk factors:** Obesity, smoking, overeating, short interval between meals and sleeping, spicy foods, alcohol

### Underlying Mechanisms

- Sliding hiatus hernia
- Reduced lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) pressure
- Increased transient LOS relaxations
- Abnormal oesophageal peristalsis (failure to clear refluxate)
- Poor gastric motility and excessive acid production

### Investigation

- **Endoscopy:** All patients over 40 with new onset reflux — to identify oesophagitis, hiatus hernia, Barrett's, strictures, and exclude malignancy; performed off acid-lowering treatment
- **Oesophageal manometry:** Assesses peristalsis and identifies motility disorders; high-resolution manometry (up to 32 channels) gives more detailed information
- **24-hour pH monitoring:** Pathological if pH <4 for >5% of 24-hour period; allows symptom correlation
- **Impedance studies:** Required when non-acid reflux is suspected
- **Barium swallow:** Excludes dysmotility; may demonstrate reflux

### Management

- **Lifestyle modification:** Address risk factors — weight loss, dietary changes, smoking cessation
- **Medical:** Antacids for mild symptoms; proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for more severe disease
- **Surgical:** Laparoscopic floppy Nissen fundoplication (360-degree posterior wrap + crural tightening) — only after excluding dysmotility and confirming oesophagitis or abnormal acid exposure; complications include dysphagia, gas bloat, excess flatus

## PART B: LPR — PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND DIAGNOSIS

### Key Differences: GORD vs LPR

| Feature | GORD | LPR |
|---------|------|-----|
| Tissue damage mechanism | Acid-mediated | Pepsin-mediated |
| Epithelium affected | Resilient stratified squamous | Delicate laryngeal epithelium |
| Protective mechanisms | Peristalsis + bicarbonate neutralization | None (no peristalsis/neutralization) |
| Reflux position | Supine | Upright |
| Reflux type | Liquid, large volume | Gaseous aerosol, small volume |
| Tolerance threshold | pH <4 for >5% of day tolerated | Brief episodes (30 seconds, 3x/week) can cause damage |
| PPI dose | Once daily effective | Twice daily required |
| Treatment duration | Rapid response | 2–3 months before improvement |

### Pepsin — The Key Mediator in LPR

- Secreted as inactive pepsinogen; activated by cleavage at pH <4
- Once activated, remains significantly active almost up to neutrality (pH 7)
- Not irreversibly deactivated until pH >8
- Pepsin binds to mucosa and is taken up into epithelial cells by endocytosis
- Can be reactivated by another reflux event or ingestion of acidic bolus (e.g., cola drinks, pH <2)
- PPIs cannot directly neutralize pepsin — they only indirectly reduce pepsinogen activation by raising gastric pH

### LPR Symptoms (RSI — Reflux Symptom Index)

The RSI is a 9-item questionnaire scored 0–5 per item. A total score >10 suggests LPR; >20 moderate; >30 severe.

Items: hoarseness, throat clearing, excess throat mucus, difficulty swallowing, coughing after eating/lying down, breathing difficulties/choking, troublesome cough, globus sensation, heartburn/chest pain/indigestion.

### Examination Findings (RFS — Reflux Finding Score)

The RFS scores 8 laryngeal findings on endoscopy (maximum score 26); score >7 suggests LPR.

| RFS Finding | Significance |
|-------------|-------------|
| Infraglottic oedema (pseudosulcus) | PPV 67–90% for LPR in symptomatic patients |
| Ventricular obliteration | Laryngeal oedema |
| Erythema/hyperaemia | Non-specific |
| Vocal fold oedema | Non-specific |
| Diffuse laryngeal oedema | Non-specific |
| Posterior commissure hypertrophy | Common in LPR and GORD |
| Granuloma/granulation | Suggestive |
| Thick endolaryngeal mucus | Non-specific |

**Important caveat:** Laryngeal abnormalities have been reported in 64–86% of normal controls. No single sign is pathognomonic of LPR. RSI may correlate better with this subjective condition than RFS alone. LPR is only one cause of an inflamed larynx (other causes: smoking, infection, alcohol, allergy, voice abuse).

### Investigations for LPR

| Investigation | Role |
|---------------|------|
| Peptest | Non-invasive; detects pepsin in pharyngeal secretions; positive result strongly predictive; negative does not exclude |
| 24-hr dual-channel pH | Electrodes at UES and lower oesophagus; 4+ episodes/24 hrs at upper electrode suggest LPR |
| MCII (impedance + pH) | Detects weakly acid and non-acid reflux events that pH alone may miss |
| Oesophageal manometry | Not diagnostic for LPR itself; used to locate sphincters for pH probe placement; identifies underlying dysmotility |
| Barium swallow | Useful if dysphagia present or pharyngeal pouch suspected; not routinely used for LPR |

## PART C: LPR MANAGEMENT

### Combined Treatment Strategy

**Mild LPR:**
- Diet and lifestyle advice (see below)
- Alginate with potassium bicarbonate 10 mL TDS + nocte

**Moderate LPR:**
- Diet and lifestyle advice
- PPI low dose BD (e.g., pantoprazole 20 mg BD) + alginate nocte

**Severe LPR:**
- Diet and lifestyle advice
- PPI high dose BD (e.g., pantoprazole 40 mg BD) + alginate nocte
- Reassess at 2–3 months

### Diet and Lifestyle Advice

- Stop smoking
- Reduce alcohol (spirits, white/rosé wine particularly irritant)
- Avoid carbonated drinks (even sparkling water) and fruit juice
- Reduce caffeine
- Avoid fatty foods (fried, chips, cheese, pastry, chocolate)
- Elevate head of bed at night

### PPI Use in LPR

- BD dosing essential (drugs active for only 12–14 hours after each dose)
- Take 30 minutes before breakfast and evening meal (drug must be present in plasma when proton pumps activated)
- Treatment trial of at least 2–3 months required; total treatment time ~6 months
- Tapered withdrawal to avoid acid rebound:
  - Pantoprazole 40 mg BD → (2 months) → 20 mg BD → (2 months) → 20 mg OD → (2 months) → Stop
  - Alginate for 2 weeks during PPI withdrawal to reduce rebound symptoms
- Most patients do not need long-term PPIs

### Alginate

- Non-systemic treatment derived from seaweed; few side effects
- Protects oesophageal and upper aerodigestive tract mucosa
- Given after meals and/or before retiring at night; also useful before activities causing reflux (exercise, singing)
- Evidence for effectiveness of alginate preparation with potassium bicarbonate in LPR

### Management of Non-Responders

- 74% of non-responders actually have active reflux disease (breakthrough acid 22%, non-acid/weakly acid 52%)
- 22% have oesophageal dysmotility as underlying problem
- Check: Is diagnosis correct? Is patient compliant? Consider different PPI agent
- Investigations: Peptest, MCII + pH monitoring, high-resolution manometry
- If significant reflux persists despite medical treatment → consider antireflux surgery

### Antireflux Surgery

- Laparoscopic fundoplication
- Better results in patients with coexisting GORD symptoms, positive pH monitoring findings, or good PPI response
- Reserved for medically refractory LPR or patients requiring long-term high-dose PPIs, especially at young age

## PART D: EOSINOPHILIC OESOPHAGITIS

### Definition and Pathology

An increasingly recognized condition characterized by eosinophilic infiltration within the oesophageal epithelium. Clinical symptoms relate to oesophageal dysmotility. Reflux disease must be excluded as the underlying cause (eosinophilia can occur secondary to GORD).

### Endoscopic Features

- Fixed oesophageal rings (trachealization)
- Luminal narrowing
- Linear furrows
- White exudates (eosinophilic microabscesses)

### Diagnosis

- Biopsies showing eosinophilic infiltration of oesophageal epithelium (≥15 eosinophils per high-power field)
- Must exclude GORD as the cause — PPI trial before confirming diagnosis

### Management

- Topical steroids (swallowed fluticasone or budesonide)
- Avoidance of identifiable food allergens
- Cautious dilatation of strictures
- Treatment strategies are evolving

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. GORD is acid-mediated affecting resilient oesophageal epithelium; LPR is pepsin-mediated affecting vulnerable laryngopharyngeal mucosa — they are distinct pathophysiological entities
> 2. Pepsin remains active up to pH 7 (near neutral) and is not irreversibly deactivated until pH >8; it is taken up into epithelial cells by endocytosis and can be reactivated by subsequent acid exposure
> 3. LPR occurs mainly in the upright position via gaseous reflux (aerosol); GORD in the supine position via liquid reflux
> 4. RSI score >10 suggests LPR (9-item questionnaire); RFS score >7 suggests LPR (8 laryngeal findings) — but laryngeal signs are present in 64–86% of normal controls
> 5. Pseudosulcus vocalis (infraglottic oedema) has the highest PPV (67–90%) for LPR among examination findings
> 6. PPIs must be given BD for LPR (not OD as in GORD) because they are only active for 12–14 hours per dose; taken 30 minutes before meals
> 7. Peptest is a non-invasive test detecting pepsin in pharyngeal secretions — positive result is strongly predictive of LPR
> 8. 74% of PPI non-responders actually have active reflux (breakthrough acid or non-acid/weakly acid reflux); 22% have underlying oesophageal dysmotility
> 9. Eosinophilic oesophagitis: fixed rings, furrows, exudates on endoscopy; eosinophilic infiltration on biopsy; treated with topical steroids and allergen avoidance
> 10. LPR management is multimodal: lifestyle modification + alginate + PPI (BD, tapered withdrawal over months) — simply prescribing PPIs without lifestyle advice achieves poor outcomes


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - Cola drinks have pH <2 and can reactivate pepsin bound to laryngeal mucosa — advise patients to avoid all carbonated drinks including sparkling water
> - A "wet" or "gurgly" voice after swallowing suggests laryngeal penetration and should prompt instrumental assessment even before diagnosing LPR
> - Acid rebound is common on stopping PPI therapy — always taper gradually and use alginate cover during withdrawal
> - LPR is a diagnosis of exclusion in many ways: laryngeal signs overlap with smoking, allergy, voice abuse, and infection — single symptoms like isolated globus rarely indicate significant LPR
> - MCII monitoring is superior to pH monitoring alone because pH threshold of 4–4.5 misses weakly acid events (pH 4.5–7) that can still cause pepsin-mediated tissue damage
