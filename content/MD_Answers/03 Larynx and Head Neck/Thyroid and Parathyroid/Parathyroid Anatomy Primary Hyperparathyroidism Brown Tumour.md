# Parathyroid Glands — Surgical Anatomy, Primary Hyperparathyroidism and Brown Tumour

**Question:** *Describe surgical anatomy of parathyroid glands. Clinical features and syndromes associated with parathyroid adenoma. Radiological assessment and surgical management. How will you manage a case of Brown tumour?*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 53 (Developmental Anatomy), Chapter 71 (Hypercalcaemia), Chapter 72 (Investigation of HPT), Chapter 73 (Management of HPT), Chapter 76 (Bilateral Exploration), Chapter 77 (MIP)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The parathyroid glands regulate calcium homeostasis through parathyroid hormone (PTH). Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder, caused by a solitary adenoma in 80-85% of cases. Understanding the embryological origin and surgical anatomy of the parathyroid glands is essential, as their variable position — particularly the inferior glands — is the most common cause of failed parathyroidectomy. Brown tumour is a giant cell bone lesion resulting from excessive osteoclast activity in hyperparathyroidism.

---

## PART A: SURGICAL ANATOMY

### Embryological Origin

| Gland | Pharyngeal Pouch | Migration | Position Variability |
|-------|-----------------|-----------|---------------------|
| Superior (PIV) | 4th pouch | Short descent | CONSTANT (within 1 cm of cricothyroid joint) |
| Inferior (PIII) | 3rd pouch | Long descent (with thymus) | VARIABLE |

- Inferior glands migrate with thymus → longer path → more variable position
- In 80% of cases, glands on one side are symmetrical with the contralateral side

### Number and Size

- Usually 4 glands (range: 2-12)
- Supernumerary glands may be found in the thymus
- Normal size: 5 x 3 x 1 mm; weight: 30-40 mg each
- Adenoma: typically >500 mg; can be >10 g

### Typical Locations

| Gland | Position | Relation to RLN |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| Superior | Posterior surface of thyroid, within 1 cm of cricothyroid joint | POSTERIOR to RLN |
| Inferior | Near inferior thyroid pole, in/near thyrothymic tract | ANTERIOR to RLN |

### Key Anatomical Landmark

The **inferior thyroid artery** is the critical landmark:
- Superior glands: ~2.5 cm ABOVE the intersection of RLN with ITA
- Inferior glands: ~2.5 cm BELOW this intersection

### Blood Supply

- Predominantly from branches of the **inferior thyroid artery** (both glands)
- Superior glands may also receive supply from posterior branch of superior thyroid artery
- **End-artery supply** — vulnerable to devascularization
- Individual branch ligation (NOT trunk ligation) preserves blood supply

### Ectopic Locations

| Superior Gland (less common) | Inferior Gland (more common) |
|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Retropharyngeal/retro-oesophageal (most common ectopic) | Thyrothymic ligament |
| Upper thyroid pole posteriorly | Cervical thymus |
| Behind hyoid/larynx/pharynx | Anterior mediastinum |
| Carotid sheath | Carotid sheath |
| Intrathyroidal (~1%) | Intrathyroidal (~1%) |

### Relations

| Structure | Relation |
|-----------|---------|
| Tubercle of Zuckerkandl | Superior gland attached to cranial aspect |
| RLN | Superior gland posterior; inferior gland anterior |
| Inferior thyroid artery | Both glands within 2.5 cm of ITA-RLN intersection |
| Thyroid capsule | Glands lie within surgical capsule, outside true capsule |
| Tracheo-oesophageal groove | Posterior to this; may descend into retro-oesophageal space |

## PART B: PRIMARY HYPERPARATHYROIDISM

### Causes

| Cause | Frequency |
|-------|-----------|
| Solitary adenoma | 80-85% |
| Hyperplasia | 15-20% (usually familial) |
| Double adenoma | Debatable (may be asymmetrical hyperplasia) |
| Parathyroid carcinoma | <1% |

### Clinical Features — "Bones, Stones, Abdominal Moans, Psychic Groans"

| System | Manifestations |
|--------|---------------|
| Skeletal | Osteoporosis, osteitis fibrosa cystica, **Brown tumour**, subperiosteal bone resorption, "salt and pepper" skull |
| Renal | Nephrolithiasis (most common symptomatic presentation), nephrocalcinosis, renal impairment |
| Gastrointestinal | Peptic ulcers, pancreatitis, constipation, anorexia |
| Neuropsychiatric | Fatigue, depression, weakness, confusion, poor concentration |
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, QT shortening |
| Asymptomatic | Most patients now present with incidental hypercalcaemia |

### MEN Syndromes

| Syndrome | Gene | Parathyroid Involvement | Other Features |
|----------|------|------------------------|----------------|
| MEN 1 | MEN1 (Chr 11q13) | Hyperplasia (>95% by age 50); FIRST manifestation | Pituitary adenoma, pancreatic islet cell tumours |
| MEN 2A | RET (Chr 10q11.2) | HPT 20-30%; mild, asymmetric | MTC (95%), phaeochromocytoma (50%) |
| MEN 2B | RET (Codon 918) | **NO HPT** | Aggressive MTC, phaeochromocytoma, mucosal ganglioneuromas, Marfanoid |

## PART C: INVESTIGATIONS

### Biochemistry

| Test | Expected Finding |
|------|-----------------|
| Serum calcium (corrected) | Elevated |
| PTH (intact) | Elevated or inappropriately normal |
| Serum phosphate | Low |
| 24-hour urine calcium | Elevated (>10 mmol/day) |
| Ca/Cr clearance ratio | >0.01 (to EXCLUDE FHH) |
| 25-OH Vitamin D | Often low (measure in all patients) |
| ALP | Elevated if bone disease present |

**FHH exclusion:** Calcium/creatinine clearance ratio <0.01 suggests familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia — NOT surgical.

### Localization Studies

| Modality | Sensitivity | Key Features |
|----------|------------|-------------|
| Ultrasound | 40-80% | Hypoechoic, ovoid, peripheral vascular arc; operator-dependent |
| Sestamibi (99mTc) | 70-100% | Higher sensitivity for large adenomas; oxyphil cell content increases uptake |
| SPECT/SPECT-CT | Superior to planar | 3D localization; excellent for posterior glands |
| 4D-CT | 82% sensitivity, 92% specificity | Triple-phase (non-contrast, arterial, delayed); arterial enhancement pattern |
| MRI | Variable | Useful for ectopic mediastinal disease and reoperative surgery |
| Selective venous sampling | Reserved for reoperative | PTH gradient >2-fold is significant |

### Concordance

- **Concordant positive localization** (USS + sestamibi agree on same gland) → minimally invasive parathyroidectomy (MIP)
- **Discordant or negative** → bilateral exploration

## PART D: SURGICAL MANAGEMENT

### Indications for Surgery (NIH Consensus — Asymptomatic PHPT)

| Criterion | Threshold |
|-----------|-----------|
| Serum calcium | >0.25 mmol/L above upper limit of normal |
| Age | <50 years |
| Creatinine clearance | <60 mL/min |
| 24-hour urine calcium | >400 mg/day (10 mmol/day) |
| BMD T-score | <-2.5 at any site |
| Vertebral fracture | On imaging |

If NONE met → surveillance with annual calcium, BMD, and renal function monitoring.

### Minimally Invasive Parathyroidectomy (MIP)

**Prerequisites:** Concordant localization (USS + sestamibi), suspected single gland disease

| Step | Details |
|------|---------|
| Incision | 2-2.5 cm focused lateral or medial approach |
| Dissection | Targeted approach to identified gland |
| Excision | Remove enlarged gland |
| ioPTH | Intra-operative PTH: **50% drop at 10 minutes** = 98% predictive of cure |
| Closure | Same-day discharge possible |

### Bilateral Neck Exploration

**Indications:** Multigland disease, negative/discordant imaging, MEN syndromes, failed MIP

- Identify ALL four glands before removal
- Assess size asymmetry
- Excise clearly enlarged gland(s)
- Biopsy normal glands for frozen section confirmation

### Surgery for MEN Syndromes

| Syndrome | Approach |
|----------|---------|
| MEN 1 | Subtotal parathyroidectomy (3-3.5 glands) + bilateral transcervical thymectomy; total PTX has too high permanent hypoparathyroidism rate |
| MEN 2A | Remove enlarged glands only; forearm autograft if needed |

### Medical Management (Non-surgical)

| Agent | Effect |
|-------|--------|
| Adequate hydration | Maintain renal calcium excretion |
| Avoid thiazides/lithium | Worsen hypercalcaemia |
| Bisphosphonates | Improve BMD but do NOT lower calcium |
| Cinacalcet (calcimimetic) | Lowers calcium; increases CaSR sensitivity |

## PART E: BROWN TUMOUR

### Definition

A giant cell lesion of bone caused by excessive osteoclastic resorption in the setting of hyperparathyroidism. Also called osteitis fibrosa cystica.

### Pathogenesis

- Elevated PTH → excessive osteoclast activation → focal bone resorption
- Areas of bone replaced by fibrous tissue and multinucleated giant cells (osteoclasts)
- Haemorrhage within lesion → haemosiderin deposition → brown colour (hence "Brown tumour")

### Clinical Features

- Most commonly affects mandible, maxilla, pelvis, ribs, femur
- May present as jaw swelling — can mimic primary bone tumour or ameloblastoma
- Pain, pathological fracture, facial deformity
- Often the presenting feature leading to diagnosis of HPT

### Investigations

| Investigation | Finding |
|--------------|---------|
| X-ray/CT | Well-defined osteolytic lesion; "soap bubble" appearance |
| Skull X-ray | "Salt and pepper" appearance (granular demineralization) |
| Hand X-ray | Subperiosteal bone resorption (radial aspect of middle phalanges) |
| Serum calcium + PTH | Elevated — confirms HPT |
| Bone biopsy | Giant cells, fibrous tissue, haemosiderin — but NOT needed if biochemistry diagnostic |

### Management

1. **Treat the primary hyperparathyroidism** — parathyroidectomy is definitive
2. Brown tumour **resolves spontaneously** after correction of HPT (calcium normalizes → osteoclast activity ceases → bone remineralizes)
3. Resolution takes 6-12 months; serial imaging to confirm
4. Surgery for Brown tumour ONLY if:
   - Pathological fracture requiring stabilization
   - Significant cosmetic deformity (especially jaw)
   - Diagnostic uncertainty (to exclude true giant cell tumour or malignancy)
5. Post-operatively: monitor calcium closely — **hungry bone syndrome** risk (rapid calcium influx into demineralized bone)

---

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Superior parathyroids (4th pouch): constant position, posterior to RLN, within 1 cm of cricothyroid joint
> 2. Inferior parathyroids (3rd pouch): variable position (migrate with thymus), anterior to RLN
> 3. Inferior thyroid artery is the KEY surgical landmark — both glands within 2.5 cm of ITA-RLN intersection
> 4. PHPT: solitary adenoma 80-85%; most now present as asymptomatic incidental hypercalcaemia
> 5. FHH must be excluded before surgery: Ca/Cr clearance ratio <0.01 suggests FHH (NOT surgical)
> 6. Sestamibi + USS concordance → MIP with ioPTH (50% drop at 10 minutes = 98% cure)
> 7. MEN 1: HPT is the FIRST manifestation (>95% by age 50); subtotal parathyroidectomy + thymectomy
> 8. MEN 2B has NO hyperparathyroidism — distinguishes it from MEN 2A
> 9. Brown tumour resolves after parathyroidectomy — surgery for bone lesion only if fracture, deformity, or diagnostic uncertainty
> 10. Surgeon experience is the most important factor for success — failure rates higher in centres performing <50 cases/year


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - The most common ectopic location for a missing SUPERIOR gland is retropharyngeal/retro-oesophageal — descends due to swallowing and gravity
> - The most common ectopic location for a missing INFERIOR gland is the thymus/thyrothymic tract — delivers with gentle traction
> - A parathyroid gland moves within its own capsule when gently probed — fat and lymph nodes do not (useful intra-operative differentiation)
> - 4D-CT is emerging as the investigation of choice when USS and sestamibi are discordant or negative — arterial phase enhancement is the key diagnostic feature
> - Codon 634 mutation accounts for 85% of MEN 2A cases — genetic testing for RET proto-oncogene is essential in all MTC patients
