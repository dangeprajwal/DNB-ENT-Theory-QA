# Thyroid Embryology and Solitary Thyroid Nodule

**Question:** *Discuss the embryology of thyroid gland. Clinical features, investigations and management of a solitary thyroid nodule. How will you manage a case of follicular neoplasm (Bethesda category III)?*

**Source:** Scott-Brown's Otorhinolaryngology, 8th Edition - Volume 1, Chapter 53 (Developmental Anatomy), Chapter 58 (Pathology), Chapter 60 (Investigation of Thyroid Disease), Chapter 61 (Benign Thyroid Disease)

---

## INTRODUCTION

The thyroid gland is the largest endocrine gland and develops from two distinct primordia. A solitary thyroid nodule is one of the most common presentations in endocrine surgery, detected in 4-7% of the population by palpation and up to 50% on ultrasound. The challenge lies in distinguishing benign from malignant nodules. The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology provides a standardized framework for FNAC interpretation and guides management decisions.

---

## PART A: THYROID EMBRYOLOGY

### Development

The thyroid develops from two distinct primordia:

**1. Median Anlage (Main thyroid)**
- Originates as a midline diverticulum from the floor of the primitive pharynx at the **foramen caecum**
- Located between the first and second pharyngeal arches
- Connected to the tongue base via the **thyroglossal duct**
- Descends anterior to the pharyngeal gut, passing anterior to the hyoid bone and laryngeal cartilages
- Reaches final pre-tracheal position by week 7
- Thyroglossal duct normally obliterates by week 8-10

**2. Lateral Anlagen (Ultimobranchial bodies)**
- Arise from the 4th/5th pharyngeal pouch complex
- Fuse with the median anlage
- Contribute **C-cells (parafollicular cells)** — neural crest origin
- C-cells produce calcitonin
- Located in the middle and upper third of the thyroid gland

### Key Embryological Derivatives

| Structure | Significance |
|-----------|-------------|
| Foramen caecum | Vestigial pit at junction of anterior 2/3 and posterior 1/3 of tongue |
| Thyroglossal duct | Normally obliterates; persistence → thyroglossal duct cyst |
| Pyramidal lobe | Remnant of thyroglossal duct; present in 12-81%; usually left of midline |
| Tubercle of Zuckerkandl | Posterior extension of lateral lobe; RLN lies medial to it |

### Thyroglossal Duct Cyst

- Presents as midline neck swelling that moves with swallowing AND tongue protrusion
- Tract passes through or near the body of the hyoid bone
- **Sistrunk procedure:** Excision of cyst + tract + central portion of hyoid bone + core of tissue to foramen caecum
- Papillary thyroid carcinoma may arise within a thyroglossal duct cyst (~1%)

## PART B: SOLITARY THYROID NODULE — EVALUATION

### Clinical Assessment

- History: duration, growth rate, compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnoea, hoarseness), family history (MEN, FNMTC), radiation exposure
- Examination: size, consistency (hard = suspicious), fixity, cervical lymphadenopathy, vocal cord assessment

### Investigations

| Investigation | Purpose |
|--------------|---------|
| TSH | Single most useful blood test; suppressed → scintiscan |
| Thyroid antibodies (TPO, Tg) | Autoimmune thyroiditis |
| Ultrasound | Characterize nodule; U classification; guide FNAC |
| FNAC | Gold standard for cytological diagnosis |
| Thyroid scintiscan | Only if TSH suppressed (hot vs cold nodule) |
| CT/MRI | Retrosternal extension; airway assessment |

### Ultrasound U Classification (BTA)

| Grade | Features | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| U1 | Normal thyroid | No FNAC |
| U2 | Benign (isoechoic, halo, cystic, eggshell calcification, spongiform) | FNAC if ≥2 cm |
| U3 | Indeterminate (homogeneous, solid/mixed, equivocal) | FNAC if ≥1 cm |
| U4 | Suspicious (solid, hypoechoic, lobulated, disrupted calcification) | FNAC all |
| U5 | Malignant (hypoechoic, irregular, microcalcification, taller-than-wide) | FNAC all |

### Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology

| Category | Designation | Malignancy Risk | Recommended Action |
|----------|------------|----------------|-------------------|
| I (Thy1) | Non-diagnostic | 1-4% | Repeat FNAC |
| II (Thy2) | Benign | 0-3% | Clinical follow-up |
| III (Thy3a) | AUS/FLUS | 5-15% | Repeat FNAC or molecular testing |
| IV (Thy3f) | Follicular neoplasm | 15-30% | Diagnostic lobectomy |
| V (Thy4) | Suspicious for malignancy | 60-75% | Near-total thyroidectomy or lobectomy |
| VI (Thy5) | Malignant | 97-99% | Total thyroidectomy |

## PART C: MANAGEMENT ALGORITHM

```
SOLITARY THYROID NODULE — MANAGEMENT

TSH Suppressed ──► Scintiscan ──► Hot nodule → Medical/RAI
                                  Cold nodule → USS + FNAC

TSH Normal/Elevated ──► USS + FNAC

FNAC Result:
├── Thy1 (Non-diagnostic) → Repeat FNAC (up to 3 attempts)
├── Thy2 (Benign) → Follow-up USS at 6-12 months
├── Thy3a (AUS/FLUS) → Repeat FNAC ± molecular testing
├── Thy3f (Follicular neoplasm) → Diagnostic lobectomy
├── Thy4 (Suspicious) → Lobectomy or total thyroidectomy
└── Thy5 (Malignant) → Total thyroidectomy
```

### Management of Bethesda Category III (Thy3a — AUS/FLUS)

This is a specific challenge — 5-15% malignancy risk:

1. **Repeat FNAC:** First-line approach; may reclassify into definitive category
2. **Molecular testing:** Gene expression classifier (GEC), ThyroSeq, BRAF/RAS mutation panel — helps risk-stratify
3. **Clinical correlation:** Nodule size, US features, patient risk factors
4. **If persistently Thy3a:** Options include:
   - Active surveillance with serial USS (if low-risk features)
   - Diagnostic lobectomy (definitive histological diagnosis)

### Management of Bethesda Category IV (Thy3f — Follicular Neoplasm)

- 15-30% malignancy risk
- **Cannot distinguish follicular adenoma from carcinoma on FNAC** — requires histological assessment of capsular/vascular invasion
- **Diagnostic lobectomy (hemithyroidectomy)** is the standard approach
- If final histology shows carcinoma → completion thyroidectomy ± RAI
- If final histology shows adenoma → no further surgery needed

### Indications for Surgery in Benign Nodules

- Compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnoea, stridor)
- Cosmetic concerns
- Retrosternal extension
- Patient preference
- Thyrotoxicosis refractory to medical management

> [!abstract] Key Points for Examination
> 1. Thyroid develops from median anlage (foramen caecum) and lateral anlagen (ultimobranchial bodies from 4th/5th pouch — contribute C-cells)
> 2. Thyroglossal duct normally obliterates by week 8-10; persistence → thyroglossal duct cyst; ~1% harbour papillary carcinoma
> 3. Pyramidal lobe (thyroglossal duct remnant) present in 12-81% — must be excised during total thyroidectomy
> 4. Tubercle of Zuckerkandl is a key surgical landmark — RLN lies medial to it; superior parathyroid attached to its cranial aspect
> 5. TSH is the single most useful initial blood test for thyroid nodule evaluation
> 6. Bethesda Category III (AUS/FLUS): 5-15% malignancy → repeat FNAC or molecular testing
> 7. Bethesda Category IV (follicular neoplasm): 15-30% malignancy → diagnostic lobectomy (FNAC cannot distinguish adenoma from carcinoma)
> 8. Ultrasound features of malignancy: solid hypoechoic, microcalcification, irregular margins, taller-than-wide (U5)
> 9. Sistrunk procedure for thyroglossal duct cyst: excise cyst + tract + central hyoid + tissue core to foramen caecum
> 10. C-cells are neural crest origin, located in middle/upper third of thyroid — give rise to medullary thyroid carcinoma


---

> [!tip] Clinical Pearls
> - A "hot" nodule on scintiscan is almost never malignant (<1%) — suppress with medication or treat with radioiodine
> - Follicular neoplasm on FNAC = diagnostic lobectomy, NOT total thyroidectomy — avoid unnecessary bilateral surgery
> - Microcalcifications on USS are the single most specific ultrasound feature for papillary thyroid carcinoma
> - Always check vocal cord mobility pre-operatively in any thyroid nodule — a fixed cord suggests malignant invasion of RLN
> - Thyroid incidentalomas <1 cm with no suspicious US features do NOT require FNAC
