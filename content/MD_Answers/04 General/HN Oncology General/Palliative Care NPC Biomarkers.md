# Palliative Care in Head and Neck Cancer & NPC Staging and Biomarkers

**Question 1:** *Briefly discuss care of terminally ill patient. How will you communicate death to relatives?*

**Question 2:** *TNM classification for carcinoma nasopharynx. Role of biomarkers in carcinoma of nasopharynx.*

## INTRODUCTION

Palliative care is an essential component of head and neck oncology, with approximately 20% of patients qualifying for palliative care at initial diagnosis and an average survival of 5 months in this group. The management of the terminally ill patient requires a holistic approach addressing physical symptoms, psychological distress, spiritual needs and communication with families. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) represents a distinct entity within head and neck oncology with unique epidemiology, staging and biomarker profile. Plasma EBV DNA has emerged as a highly sensitive and specific biomarker for NPC, superior to traditional EBV serology, with applications in diagnosis, staging, treatment monitoring and surveillance for recurrence.

## PART A: PALLIATIVE CARE IN HEAD AND NECK CANCER

### Definition

The World Health Organization defines palliative care as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems — physical, psychosocial and spiritual.

### Symptom Burden in Terminal Head and Neck Cancer

Head and neck cancer patients have a particularly high symptom burden due to the involvement of aerodigestive structures critical for breathing, swallowing and communication.

| Symptom | Prevalence |
|---------|-----------|
| Pain | 62-99% |
| Dysphagia | 45-90% |
| Weight loss/anorexia | 43-98% |
| Breathing difficulty | 22-52% |
| Bleeding | 9-47% |

### Pain Management — WHO Analgesic Ladder

Pain management follows the WHO three-step analgesic ladder, with progression based on pain severity and response to treatment.

**Step 1 — Non-opioid:** Paracetamol and NSAIDs for mild pain. Regular paracetamol (1g four times daily) forms the foundation of analgesia.

**Step 2 — Weak opioid:** Codeine (30-60 mg four times daily) added to Step 1 agents for moderate pain not controlled by non-opioids alone.

**Step 3 — Strong opioid:** Morphine is the drug of choice for severe cancer pain. Commenced at low dose (5-10 mg immediate-release preparation four-hourly), titrated upward according to response, then converted to sustained-release preparation once stable dose established.

**Breakthrough pain management:** Short-acting morphine (one-sixth of 24-hour total dose) for intermittent breakthrough pain episodes. For patients already on ≥60 mg oral morphine equivalent daily, transmucosal fentanyl preparations (sublingual, buccal or intranasal) provide rapid onset analgesia for breakthrough episodes.

**Route considerations in H&N cancer:**
- Oral route may be compromised by dysphagia, oral tumours or trismus
- Gastrostomy administration: sustained-release preparations must NOT be crushed — use liquid morphine instead
- Transdermal fentanyl patches: used only when pain is stable (not for titration), changed every 72 hours
- Continuous subcutaneous infusion (CSCI) via syringe driver: preferred for patients unable to take oral medication and requiring continuous analgesia

**Neuropathic pain:** Common in head and neck cancer due to neural invasion. Gabapentin is first-line treatment; amitriptyline is an alternative. Dexamethasone is added for pain from nerve compression.

### Management of Specific Terminal Symptoms

**Respiratory secretions ("death rattle"):**
Glycopyrronium is the preferred anticholinergic (does not cross the blood-brain barrier, fewer central side effects). Alternatives include hyoscine hydrobromide (crosses BBB, may cause sedation) and hyoscine butylbromide.

**Terminal haemorrhage:**
Carotid artery rupture occurs in 3-5% of patients with advanced head and neck cancer, particularly in the context of previous surgery, [[Radiotherapy Principles Fractionation Advances|radiotherapy]] or tumour erosion of the vessel wall. Management involves pre-emptive planning:
- Dark-coloured towels at the bedside to reduce visual distress
- Midazolam 10 mg intramuscularly prepared in advance (pre-emptive prescription)
- The aim is rapid sedation to minimize patient distress — the haemorrhage itself is usually rapidly fatal
- Family should be counselled in advance about the possibility of catastrophic bleeding

**Airway obstruction:**
Acute stridor from tumour compression is managed with dexamethasone 8-16 mg subcutaneously. If this represents a terminal event, midazolam is administered for sedation and symptom relief. Pre-emptive prescription of emergency medications is essential.

**Depression:**
Patients with head and neck cancer have a disproportionately higher suicide risk compared to other cancer groups. Screening for depression is essential. SSRIs are first-line pharmacological treatment but carry a risk of gastrointestinal bleeding when combined with NSAIDs. Mirtazapine is an alternative — its side effects of weight gain and sedation may actually be beneficial in cachectic, anxious patients.

### Spiritual Care and Psychological Support

Spiritual care involves exploring the patient's sense of meaning, purpose and values. This is not confined to religious practice but encompasses existential concerns about dignity, legacy, reconciliation and the meaning of suffering. The palliative care team should facilitate access to spiritual care providers when appropriate.

### Ethics in End-of-Life Care

The four pillars of medical ethics guide palliative decision-making:
- **Autonomy:** Respecting the patient's right to make informed decisions including refusal of treatment
- **Beneficence:** Acting in the patient's best interest
- **Non-maleficence:** Avoiding harm, including futile interventions that prolong suffering
- **Justice:** Fair allocation of resources

**Advance care planning:** Discussions about the patient's wishes for end-of-life care should occur early, while the patient retains capacity. This includes preferences regarding resuscitation, place of death, and the extent of interventions desired.

## PART B: CARE OF THE DYING AND COMMUNICATING DEATH

### Five Priorities for Care of the Dying (Box 21.1)

The five priorities framework guides care in the final days of life:

1. **Recognition:** The possibility that the person is dying is recognized and communicated clearly to all team members and to the patient and family
2. **Sensitive communication:** Communication with the dying person and their family is honest, compassionate and occurs regularly
3. **Involvement in decisions:** The dying person and those identified as important to them are involved in decisions about treatment and care to the extent that they wish
4. **Family needs:** The needs of families and those close to the dying person are actively explored, respected and met as far as possible
5. **Individual plan of care:** An individualized plan of care is agreed and coordinated, including food, fluid, symptom control and psychological/spiritual support

### Clinically Assisted Hydration

There are no firm recommendations regarding clinically assisted hydration in the dying patient. Evidence suggests it may improve sedation and myoclonus but does not appear to improve thirst or delirium. Each case should be assessed individually in discussion with the family.

**Important practical point:** Transdermal fentanyl patches should NOT be commenced de novo in the actively dying patient. If a patient is already receiving transdermal fentanyl, the patch should be continued and supplemented with as-needed subcutaneous injections for breakthrough symptoms.

### Communicating Death to Relatives

Communication of death to family members is one of the most important and sensitive tasks in clinical practice. The following structured approach is recommended:

**Preparation:**
- Ensure you have correct identification of the deceased patient and the family members
- Find a quiet, private room free from interruptions
- Sit down at the same level as the family — do not stand over them
- Have a nurse or trained support person present

**Breaking the news:**
- Introduce yourself by name and role
- Use a warning shot: "I'm afraid I have some very sad news"
- Use clear, unambiguous language — state that the patient "has died" rather than euphemisms such as "passed away" or "lost"
- Allow silence and time for the information to be absorbed
- Be prepared for a range of emotional responses including shock, anger, distress and apparent calm

**After delivering the news:**
- Express genuine empathy and condolence
- Answer questions honestly and sensitively
- Explain what will happen next (viewing the body, death certification, post-mortem if applicable)
- Offer access to chaplaincy, bereavement counselling, social work support
- Provide written information about bereavement services and practical next steps
- Ensure the family knows how to contact the team if they have further questions
- Document the discussion in the clinical notes

## PART C: TNM CLASSIFICATION OF NASOPHARYNGEAL CARCINOMA (AJCC 8th Edition)

### T Classification

The T classification for NPC is distinct from other head and neck subsites, reflecting the unique anatomical relationships of the nasopharynx:

| T Stage | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| T1 | Confined to nasopharynx, or extends to oropharynx and/or nasal cavity without parapharyngeal involvement |
| T2 | Parapharyngeal extension and/or adjacent soft tissue involvement (medial pterygoid, lateral pterygoid, prevertebral muscles) |
| T3 | Infiltration of bony structures of skull base, cervical vertebrae, pterygoid structures, and/or paranasal sinuses |
| T4 | Intracranial extension, cranial nerve involvement, hypopharynx, orbit, extensive soft tissue infiltration beyond lateral surface of lateral pterygoid |

### N Classification

The N classification for NPC differs from the general head and neck nodal staging system. It is based on laterality and the caudal extent of nodal disease relative to the cricoid cartilage:

| N Stage | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| N0 | No regional lymph node metastasis |
| N1 | Unilateral cervical and/or unilateral or bilateral retropharyngeal nodes, above caudal border of cricoid, ≤6 cm |
| N2 | Bilateral cervical nodes, above caudal border of cricoid, ≤6 cm |
| N3 | >6 cm and/or extension below caudal border of cricoid cartilage |

### Stage Grouping

| Stage | TNM Combination |
|-------|----------------|
| I | T1 N0 M0 |
| II | T1 N1, T2 N0-1, M0 |
| III | T1-2 N2, T3 N0-2, M0 |
| IVA | T4 N0-2, any T N3, M0 |
| IVB | Any T, any N, M1 |

### Key Differences from General H&N Staging

NPC staging has several unique features that distinguish it from other head and neck sites:
- N classification is based on laterality and relationship to the caudal border of the cricoid cartilage, NOT individual node size
- Retropharyngeal nodes are included in the N classification (N1)
- Extranodal extension is NOT incorporated into NPC nodal staging (unlike oral cavity/oropharynx in AJCC 8th edition)
- Parapharyngeal extension alone (without other features) upstages to T2

## PART D: ROLE OF BIOMARKERS IN NASOPHARYNGEAL CARCINOMA

### EBV Serology (Traditional Biomarkers)

EBV serology has been used historically for NPC screening and diagnosis:

| Test | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| IgA VCA | 89% | 80% |
| IgA EA | 63% | 95% |

However, EBV serology has significant limitations: it shows only a weak correlation with tumour stage, is unable to reliably reflect tumour response to treatment, and does not demonstrate an increasing titre in recurrence. These limitations have led to the search for more reliable biomarkers.

### Plasma EBV DNA (Superior Biomarker)

Plasma EBV DNA has emerged as the most clinically useful biomarker for NPC, with sensitivity of 96% and specificity of 93% — substantially superior to EBV serology. Its clinical applications span the entire disease trajectory:

**Pre-treatment assessment:**
- High plasma EBV DNA levels before treatment correlate with larger tumour burden and more advanced stage
- Serves as a quantitative surrogate for disease extent

**Treatment monitoring:**
- Plasma EBV DNA shows a rapid decrease to undetectable levels within 1-2 weeks after completion of successful treatment
- Can be used to monitor treatment response during chemotherapy in lieu of interval imaging
- Provides real-time feedback on treatment efficacy

**Post-treatment surveillance:**
- Persistently elevated levels after treatment completion may signify persistent or metastatic disease and may justify adjuvant chemotherapy
- An initial drop followed by progressive rise may represent tumour recurrence
- Enables early detection of relapse before clinical or radiological evidence

**Limitations:** Plasma EBV DNA is elevated in any EBV-associated disease including infectious mononucleosis and EBV-associated lymphoma. Clinical correlation is essential.

### Tissue-Based Biomarkers

**EBER (EBV-encoded RNA) immunostaining:** Used for definitive confirmation of NPC diagnosis from tissue specimens including fine needle aspiration cytology. EBER is expressed in virtually all undifferentiated NPC (WHO Type III) and is the gold standard for confirming EBV association.

**Nasopharyngeal brushing for EBV DNA:** A non-invasive diagnostic technique with reported sensitivity of 98.9% and specificity of 99.3%. This has potential application in screening high-risk populations.
